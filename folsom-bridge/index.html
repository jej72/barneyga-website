    Folsom Bridge

    Folsom Bridge crosses Little River at the Brooks-Lowndes county line, just north of the former Foster settlement. It connected the old farm country of northern Brooks County with Hahira and later carried State Route 122. The name has survived through several wooden bridges, a long steel bridge, and the present concrete crossing.

    Roads leading to the crossing shaped travel, mail, farming, and the naval-stores trade long before Barney became a railroad town. The bridge history also clears up two recurring points of confusion. Folsom Bridge was not Joyce's Ferry, which became Miller Bridge farther downstream, and the evidence does not support William "Uncle Billy" Folsom as the bridge's individual namesake.

    The Folsom settlement

    The name most likely came from the Folsom family settlement east of Little River. Lawrence Folsom bought all of Land Lot 187 in the 12th District in November 1823. That lot and his other reported holdings lay in the neighborhood east of the crossing.

    M. M. Folsom supplied a contemporary description in 1885. He called Folsom Bridge a noted crossing and placed the ancestral Folsom settlement about a mile and a half east of it. He did not identify the ancestor by name, but his account supports the family-settlement explanation and its location on the Lowndes side of the river.

    An 1893 newspaper account traced the old homestead from Lawrence's son James Folsom to James's children. The Lowndes County map made about 1910 gives the land pattern more clearly. It labels A. J. Folsom in Land Lots 228, 187, and 182 along the road running east from the bridge toward Salem. The Lot 228 label sits at the east bridge approach. This is probably Andrew Jackson Folsom, one of James's sons, although the map gives only initials and does not supply acreage or a title history.

    An original Brooks County deed now establishes the Land Lot 228 division. On November 18, 1872, James J. Park and Penney Park conveyed W. W. Joyce the portion west of Little River in Brooks County for $560. The deed says the portion east of the river in Lowndes County had already been conveyed to "the Estate of Folsom." It also transferred the east-side ferry and bridge privileges to Joyce. The deed reads Lot 228, not 278, and was recorded March 25, 1873.

    This is the first located original parcel record tying bridge rights to the Folsom Bridge land lot. It does not call the structure Folsom Bridge, identify the Folsom estate, or date the first bridge. Read with the 1871 repair presentment and the 1908 map label, the connection is strong probable.

    The public Lowndes land-lot map supplies additional derivative leads. It groups Lots 141, 182, 187, and the east half of 228 as land associated with the James Folsom estate. Its tax-index notes place James's widow Anna on 496 acres in Lots 182 and 187 in 1869 and 1870. They also place Lawrence in Lots 182 and 187 before his death and James in those two lots in 1844. Those claims should remain attributed to the map database until the underlying tax and deed records are inspected.

    Together, the deed, family land succession, newspaper account, and map make the family settlement the strongest explanation for the bridge's name. Lawrence is the leading individual candidate because his land anchored that settlement, but no located record says that the bridge was named specifically for him.

    William Folsom lived in the broader neighborhood, but his reported plantation lay several miles west of the bridge and north of Barney. A widely repeated William-centered explanation does not fit the land evidence. The safest conclusion is that the bridge took its name from the older Folsom settlement, with the individual namesake unresolved.

    Coffee Road and the first crossing

    Georgia opened the southern section of Coffee Road in 1823. In 1885, Montgomery M. Folsom called Folsom Bridge a noted crossing and wrote that General John Coffee's road cutters crossed Little River there. He also said old settlers could still point out blazes on the pine trees. A modern Old Coffee Road marker and present road name in Land Lot 282, beside the Mount Zion Camp Ground neighborhood, add geographic support for a route from Camp Ground toward that crossing. Together, the evidence makes the Folsom Bridge corridor the strongest candidate for the original route and suggests that the later Joyce/Miller crossing may have been a downstream bypass or realignment. The retrospective account and modern marker cannot establish the exact 1823 roadbed or date such a change; no located road order, survey, or deed proves it.

    An 1869 Brooks County map shows a road entering the river area through Land Lot 227 and crossing into Lowndes County south of the later bridge. A road notice from the same year reached the Nashville road at Little River but did not mention Folsom Bridge or authorize a river span. The evidence supports an established crossing corridor by 1869. It does not prove that a bridge stood there then, much less that a bridge existed in 1823.

    The first direct record is a Brooks County grand-jury presentment dated November 24, 1871. It said that "The Folsom Bridge, on Little River, also needs attention" and asked Brooks and Lowndes counties to repair it jointly. The bridge and its name were therefore in use by 1871. Its original construction date and builder remain unknown.

    Keeping the crossing open

    The early bridge had to cross more than the river channel. Little River's swamp and lagoons made the approaches dangerous after heavy rain. A traveler described the lagoons as hazardous in 1873. Brooks County appropriated money for river-bridge lumber in 1875 and then for a separate bridge across a lagoon in the swamp.

    In 1876 contractor David Hiers built that lagoon bridge. The county directed the Morven District road commissioners to inspect his work before payment. Hiers can therefore be named as the builder of an approach span, but not as the builder of the original river bridge.

    The river bridge was rebuilt in 1885. In January 1886, a Valdosta dispatch reported that the bridge across Little River had burned during a cluster of fires near the Brooks-Lowndes line. The same night, William Ryals's gin house, a country schoolhouse, and the miller's dwelling at Dr. Bourquin's mill also burned. The report said someone had poured kerosene on Bourquin's mill, but the doctor discovered the attempt and drove the suspected incendiaries away. The Waycross Headlight reprinted the dispatch on February 3, and a February 6 report identified the burned crossing as Folsom Bridge. The reprint said no arrests had been made. No later arrest, indictment, trial, insurance claim, or loss estimate tied securely to these fires was found in the documented newspaper search. The surviving reports do not establish who set the fires or whether the entire bridge complex burned.

    The swamp approach appears again in February 1890. George T. Rountree had crossed Folsom Bridge and entered a deep slough on the Lowndes side when an assailant shot him in the leg. The wound was not considered dangerous. The following month, a separate report said Robert Dent and William Marshall had made substantial repairs to the bridge.

    An 1892 newspaper brief placed a bear near Folsom Bridge on Little River. It called the location Berrien County, which conflicts with the otherwise well-documented Brooks-Lowndes county-line location and should be treated as a reporting or reprint error rather than evidence about the bridge's jurisdiction.

    In April 1895, Lowndes County began a new bridge at Folsom, described as about 100 yards long and substantial. A May report said the work was complete. Neither article gives the builder, material, exact alignment, or reason for replacement.

    Bridge Superintendent Taylor recruited workers for another rebuilding in 1911. Repairs followed a May 1912 freshet. These reports probably concern the same southern crossing used during the nineteenth century, but they do not prove that each rebuilding followed precisely the same deck line or reused the same supports.

    Foster and McNeill's Road

    Foster developed in Brooks County just south of Folsom Bridge during the same period that James T. McNeill and Thomas A. Rozier improved the road from the bridge to Hahira.

    Lowndes County residents debated competing Hahira routes to Miller Bridge and Folsom Bridge in 1889. A September report says petitions had been filed for both roads, commissioners had examined and laid out the routes, and their returns awaited action by the county commissioners. By March 1890, McNeill & Rozier's workers were opening the Folsom route along a land line. An April report credited McNeill with nearly completing it. The name McNeill Road appeared by 1892, and Lowndes County formally adopted the Hahira-to-Folsom Bridge route in 1897.

    That road helps explain Foster's location and economy. Hahira had the nearest railroad depot, but without a direct road from Folsom Bridge it would have been an unlikely shipping point for Foster's naval-stores business. The new route gave wagons a practical connection through the bridge to the railroad. Its exact modern alignment and whether Foster was its sole purpose are not proved.

    The upstream bridge of 1924

    Brooks and Lowndes counties again rebuilt the crossing together in 1924. High water interrupted construction in October. By December, newspapers described a substantial new bridge built above previous high-water marks. The Hahira Board of Trade held a supper for commissioners and attorneys from both counties when the bridge opened on December 17.

    A January 1925 flood report supplies the clearest evidence for the remembered two wooden bridge sites. It placed the old bridge several hundred yards below the new one. Because Little River flows south here, the old crossing was south of the 1924 bridge.

    The higher bridge did not end the flood problems. Water washed the Brooks-side fill badly in January 1925. The counties added a relief bridge that spring. In 1926, Lowndes County raised the Brooks-side turnpike and lifted that end of the main bridge. By 1934 the structure was badly deteriorated. The counties divided future maintenance costs in 1935, with Lowndes paying 60 percent and Brooks 40 percent.

    A February 1926 report said the last bridge built at the crossing had washed away and left the road impassable. The wording may refer to the smaller 1925 relief bridge rather than the main 1924 river bridge, so the failed structure cannot be identified securely. During another major flood in April 1928, volunteers were credited with saving the Folsom highway bridge.

    Steel and concrete

    Federal bridge inventories identify a 39-span steel bridge built in 1941 on State Route 122. The 2008 inventory described it as a continuous steel stringer and multi-beam structure about 1,053 feet long. No located contract or opening report settles its exact relationship to the 1924 wooden alignment.

    The April 1948 flood restricted Highway 122 at Little River. Passenger cars could cross only at the driver's risk, and heavy traffic was barred. U.S. Geological Survey records estimate that the flood peaked at 45,000 cubic feet per second at the highway crossing on April 3. The inspected sources establish the restriction and flood magnitude but do not report structural failure.

    The present bridge replaced the steel structure during 2007 and 2008. Federal files first list it in the 2009 inventory and describe a 10-span prestressed-concrete bridge about 1,160 feet long. The inventories disagree by one year on the construction date, so 2007-08 is more accurate than assigning a single year.

    Folsom Bridge and Miller Bridge

    The 1908 Brooks County map labels Folsom Bridge in Land Lot 228 and Miller Bridge downstream in Land Lot 189. A companion Lowndes County map also shows both crossings. This map evidence agrees with R. E. L. Folsom's 1899 account, which placed Washington Joyce's ferry at the later Miller Bridge.

    The distinction matters. Joyce's Ferry belongs to the Miller Bridge history. Folsom Bridge belongs to the older Folsom settlement, the probable Coffee Road corridor, Foster, and the road to Hahira. The two crossings served related neighborhoods, but they were never the same place.

    What remains unresolved

    • when and by whom the first bridge was built;
    • whether the 1885 and 1911 rebuilds followed exactly the same line;
    • the extent of the 1886 fire and the builder, material, and alignment of the 1895 replacement;
    • the contractor and plan for the 1924 bridge;
    • the 1941 bridge's contract, opening date, and exact alignment;
    • a contemporary naming record identifying a specific Folsom;
    • the exact house sites and complete title boundaries of the Folsom properties near the crossing;
    • the original tax and deed records behind the online land-lot reconstruction for Lots 141, 182, 187, and 228.
    • which Folsom structure the February 1926 washout report described.

    The missing records are most likely to survive in Brooks and Lowndes county-purpose books, road orders, bridge contracts, and later state highway files.
