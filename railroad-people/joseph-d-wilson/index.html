    Joseph Daniel Wilson

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    Joseph Daniel Wilson (November 1862-January 20, 1939) was a Quitman clothing merchant, farmer, banker, railroad promoter, mayor, alderman, and city commissioner. His identity is verified through a continuous record chain from his parents' household to his death. The sources do not agree on the day of his birth. November 12 is the stronger reading, but Huxford also printed November 2 in the same family chapter.[1][2][3]

    The record is unusually good for Wilson's public life. Contemporary newspapers document his store, farming interests, Board of Trade work, two railway petitions, four years as mayor, later city commission service, church work, and Bank of Quitman directorship. They also correct one important mistake in the county history: he was not reelected mayor in 1912. He lost to E. L. Jelks and left office that April.[1][17][18][19]

    Identity and family

    Wilson was born in Brooks County to Jeremiah Wilson Jr. and Delilah Robinson, whose surname also appears as Robeson. Huxford names Joseph Daniel as the ninth of their thirteen children. The 1870 census places eight-year-old Joseph in the Brooks County household of Jerry and Delilah Wilson, with Quitman as the post office. Because that census did not state relationships, Ancestry's parent labels are inferences from the household, not words written by the enumerator.[1][4]

    The 1880 schedule removes that uncertainty. Seventeen-year-old Joseph was living in Quitman with Jermiah and Delela Wilson and was explicitly recorded as their son. He was single and working as a laborer.[5] The parents, siblings, age, birthplace, and place match the 1870 household and Huxford's genealogy.

    Huxford says Wilson grew up on the family farm about four miles northwest of Quitman. In 1885 he went to a business college in Poughkeepsie, New York, then returned to Quitman after graduating. The book calls it Poughkeepsie Business College. It does not supply a diploma, enrollment record, or graduation date, so the school history rests on Huxford's later account.[1]

    Clothing business

    Wilson worked as a mercantile clerk for three years after returning from New York. Huxford says he opened his own haberdashery in 1888.[1] The census record then traces the business across three decades.

    In 1900 he was single and boarding with Ancil and Mattie Sweat in Quitman. The census indexed his occupation as “presser clothing,” a reading that should not be forced into a more precise job title without the original schedule. The same record gives November 1862 as his birth month and year.[6] By 1910 he was a clothing-store merchant working on his own account. He and Daisy were boarding on Johnson Street with George W. Wade's family.[7]

    The 1920 census called him the proprietor of a clothing store. He owned the house at 502 Johnson Street free of mortgage and lived there with Daisy.[8] In 1930 the couple was at 404 Johnson Street in Brooks County's 1199th militia district. Wilson owned the $4,500 house, had a radio, and was the employer and proprietor of a men's furnishings store.[9]

    The store also appears under Wilson's own name. During the Hotel Marie fire in March 1927, burglars broke into both the Quitman Clothing Company and Joseph D. Wilson Company. Reports said little was taken.[24] An I. A. Harrell obituary supplies a partnership timeline that the censuses do not. Harrell had worked with Wilson in the clothing business for twenty-one years before leaving for Birdsey Milling Company about six years before his 1934 death. That points to an association of roughly 1907 through 1928, but the dates remain an inference from the obituary's rounded periods.[25]

    Sweat & Wilson

    Sweat & Wilson's bar was operating in Quitman by February 1893. A Savannah newspaper placed a shooting inside the bar, and the 1897 Quitman fire reports named the firm and its uninsured $300 loss.[10][11] None of those reports gives either proprietor's first name.

    The identification of Joseph Daniel Wilson as the Wilson partner is strong probable, not verified. The fit is specific. In 1900 Joseph D. Wilson boarded with Ancil Sweat and Mattie Wilson Sweat. A. Sweat and Joseph D. Wilson were also named together in Huxford's 1896 South Georgia Railroad petition list.[1][6] Ancil had already been a Quitman grocer, and the Sweat family connection continued into Wilson's household record. No competing adult Wilson in Quitman has emerged with the same business, household, and railroad ties.

    Still, no advertisement, liquor license, occupational-tax book, partnership filing, or directory entry found online spells out “Ancil Sweat and Joseph D. Wilson.” The partnership cannot be called verified until such a record is located. This dossier raises the older site assessment from possible to strong probable because the 1900 household and the shared railroad petition now provide a direct two-person bridge, while preserving the missing-name limit.

    Farming and agricultural trade

    Huxford says Wilson became one of Brooks County's largest landowners and shipped hundreds of railroad cars of watermelons and Georgia cane syrup in a season, along with cotton and Brooks County cured hams.[1] No land ledger, deed chain, freight ledger, or annual shipment account was found to test those quantities.

    The contemporary record does confirm the underlying businesses. In 1903 Wilson wrote a published letter endorsing Judge Tillman's syrup-treatment process. He described an observed test and argued that better syrup quality would raise prices and attract settlers. The letter proves his active interest in the cane-syrup trade, though it does not prove Huxford's shipment totals.[13]

    A 1913 item reprinted from the Quitman Advertiser said Wilson had a large watermelon acreage on his Brooks County farm. He had brought the newspaper the season's first small Watson melon and was said to take pride in its cultivation.[20] These independent records support a substantial farming and produce business without assigning unsupported acreage or carload numbers.

    South Georgia Railroad

    Huxford names Joseph D. Wilson among twenty-six men in the 1896 petition for a South Georgia Railroad charter. The capital was fixed at $65,000 in 650 shares.[1] Wilson was not elected to the first nine-member board and held no office in the first management slate. A contemporary report of the March 1896 organization meeting confirms that board and officer list, but it does not reproduce all charter petitioners.[14]

    Wilson's role should therefore be stated narrowly. He was strongly probably the Joseph D. Wilson named as charter petitioner or incorporator. The original petition, signature page, subscription book, and stock ledger have not been found online. No share count or continued ownership can be assigned to him.

    His later railway promotion is directly documented. A 1907 published charter petition for the Quitman, Valdosta and Thomasville Electric Railway and Power Company names Joseph D. Wilson among the applicants. The proposed fifty-mile electric road would run from Valdosta through Blue Springs and Quitman to Thomasville, with authority to furnish electric light and power. The petition said the applicants intended in good faith to obtain subscriptions and build the line.[15] This verifies Wilson's participation in a second transportation venture. It does not prove that the proposed road was completed.

    Board of Trade and city government

    Quitman's first Board of Trade organized in April 1900. J. W. Spain became president, W. T. Thompson vice president, and C. T. Tillman secretary-treasurer. Wilson and W. A. Davis were elected directors.[12] Huxford independently gives the same slate.[1]

    Wilson then spent much of the next three decades in municipal office. Huxford's roster places him on the board of aldermen in 1902-1907 and again in 1916-1921. It lists him as mayor for 1908-1909 and 1910-1911.[1] A 1911 newspaper profile confirms Wilson as mayor and names the six aldermen serving with him.[16]

    The end of his mayoralty is clear. In February 1912, the Macon Telegraph reported that Wilson would seek reelection and noted that the council had eliminated the city's floating debt.[17] On March 25, E. L. Jelks defeated him in the primary, 222 votes to 132. Parallel reports described a ninety-vote margin.[18] Jelks succeeded Wilson at the April 10 council meeting. That report said Wilson had served two terms since succeeding Jelks in 1908.[19]

    This contemporary sequence contradicts Huxford's later narrative statement that Wilson was reelected in 1912. Huxford's own mayoral roster agrees with the newspapers and assigns 1912-1913 to Jelks.[1] Wilson may later have served as mayor pro tem, as Huxford says, but that specific title and service through 1922 were not independently verified in the accessible newspaper record.

    Wilson returned to citywide office in 1930. He announced for a three-year city commission term in the first white primary held under Quitman's commission-manager system. On November 14 he defeated W. W. Williams. The nominees were to be elected December 6 and take office January 1, 1931.[21][22] Huxford says Wilson then became chairman of the commission for the three-year term and did not seek reelection.[1] The election is verified; the chairmanship is supported by the county history but was not separately found in a contemporary report.

    Bank, church, and organizations

    Huxford says Wilson was elected to the Bank of Quitman board on February 1, 1914, and remained a director until his death.[1] A 1931 stockholders' report confirms him on the seven-man board when it was unanimously reelected.[23] His 1939 obituary again called him a bank director.[26]

    The same sources place him in Quitman's Methodist community. Huxford says he served as a steward of First Methodist Church for more than thirty years.[1] A 1930 quarterly conference report names him among the newly elected stewards, and his obituary says he had served the church for many years.[21][26]

    Huxford also lists him as a member of the Chamber of Commerce, Sholto Masonic Lodge, Alee Temple of the Shrine in Savannah, the Royal Arcanum, and the Quitman Country Club.[1] These memberships were not all independently verified. They remain attributed to Huxford rather than treated as a set of separately proved affiliations.

    Marriage and home

    Wilson married Daisy Maye Justus of Baltimore in 1902. The Baltimore Sun reported on August 14 that a marriage license had been issued the previous day to Joseph D. Wilson, age thirty-nine, of Quitman, and Daisy Justus, age twenty-five.[3] Huxford gives August 13 as the marriage date and names Daisy's parents as Charles Edward and Jennie Salena Justus.[1] The newspaper proves the license date. Huxford supplies the ceremony date, which may have been the same day but is not established by the license notice alone.

    The 1910, 1920, and 1930 censuses keep the couple together and show no children in their homes.[7][8][9] Huxford says they had none. It also describes their large collection of camellias and azaleas. A 1908 social item records Daisy returning to Quitman after several months with relatives in Baltimore and New York, a small independent link to her Maryland family.[1][27]

    Death and burial

    Wilson died in the Quitman hospital on January 20, 1939, after a long illness attributed to heart trouble. The state death index names him Joseph Daniel Wilson, age seventy-six, and records the same date and place.[28] The same-day newspaper report called him a retired businessman and former mayor. It named his wife Daisy, brother Dr. R. E. L. Wilson, and sisters Mrs. A. C. Berry and Mrs. Tom Beasley as survivors. The funeral was set for the next afternoon at the residence.[26]

    He was buried in Oak Hill Cemetery in Quitman. Find a Grave reports November 12, 1862, and January 20, 1939, and links him to Jeremiah, Delilah, and Daisy.[2] Its family links are derivative, but the cemetery, name, and dates agree with the wider record.

    Huxford's chapter contains an internal birth-date conflict. The child list gives November 2, 1862, while the narrative biography gives November 12. The 1900 census gives only November 1862, and Find a Grave gives November 12.[1][2][6] November 12 is therefore the stronger reading, but the exact day is not free of conflict.

    Huxford says Wilson's casket blanket was made from Pink Perfection camellias cut from the Wilson garden and lilies of the valley.[1] That detail was not found in the contemporary death notices and remains a later local-history account.

    Evidence assessment

    1. Joseph D. Wilson is verified as Joseph Daniel Wilson, son of Jeremiah Wilson Jr. and Delilah Robinson or Robeson Wilson. The 1870 and 1880 households, later censuses, Huxford genealogy, death index, and cemetery record form a continuous identity chain.
    2. His clothing business is verified from the 1900, 1910, 1920, and 1930 censuses, named-store reporting, and the Harrell business association. Huxford's 1888 opening date remains a later-history claim.
    3. His farming and produce trade are verified in broad outline. The “hundreds of cars” and largest-landowner claims come from Huxford and were not tested against deeds or freight ledgers.
    4. His identity as the 1896 South Georgia Railroad petitioner is strong probable because Huxford supplies the charter list but the original petition is missing. His 1907 electric-railway petition is verified from the published charter text.
    5. His terms as alderman and mayor are verified in broad outline. The claim that he was reelected mayor in 1912 is contradicted. He lost the primary and left office in April.
    6. His election to the city commission in 1930 is verified. Huxford's statement that he chaired it for three years is strong probable pending commission minutes or another contemporary report.
    7. His partnership in Sweat & Wilson is strong probable. The household, family, business, and railroad links are unusually specific, but no source found online names both proprietors in full.

    Open questions and limits

    • The original 1896 South Georgia Railroad charter petition, signature page, subscription book, and stock ledger were not located online.
    • No source found online gives Wilson's South Georgia Railroad share count or places him in later company governance.
    • No full-name Sweat & Wilson advertisement, license, tax entry, partnership filing, or directory listing was found.
    • Huxford's November 2 and November 12 birth dates conflict. The latter has better support, but the discrepancy remains.
    • Huxford's claim of a 1912 mayoral reelection is contradicted by contemporary election and succession reports.
    • No deed chain or freight ledger was found to test the size of Wilson's landholdings or Huxford's shipment totals.
    • No probate packet, will, estate inventory, complete store ledger, or city-commission minute book was located in the accessible online collections.
    • The accessible Georgia marriage collection did not return a county marriage record. The Baltimore license notice and Huxford account establish the 1902 marriage, but they are not a civil marriage certificate.

    Search coverage

    Research began with the stored South Georgia Railroad files, Huxford OCR and scan, the A. Sweat dossier, census references, and preserved newspaper search results. Online work then covered Ancestry's 1870, 1880, 1900, 1910, 1920, and 1930 federal census collections; Georgia marriage, death, probate, property-tax, cemetery, and obituary indexes; the Newspapers.com marriage index; Find a Grave; direct Newspapers.com page readbacks; Georgia Historic Newspapers for the railroad organization report; the Internet Archive copy of Huxford; and broader exact-name web searches.

    Searches used "Joseph D. Wilson", "Joseph D Wilson", "Jos. D. Wilson", "Joseph Daniel Wilson", "Joseph Wilson", "Daisy Justus", "Daisy Maye Wilson", "Sweat & Wilson", and "Sweat and Wilson", combined with Quitman, Brooks County, mayor, alderman, commissioner, bank, clothing, haberdashery, farming, watermelon, syrup, Methodist, railroad, obituary, death, and funeral. Namesakes were rejected unless a record supplied Quitman, Brooks County, Daisy, Wilson's parents or siblings, his business, or a known public office.

    No claim is made that unindexed courthouse, bank, church, or corporate records were searched.
