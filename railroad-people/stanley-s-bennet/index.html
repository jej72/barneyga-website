    Stanley S. Bennet

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    Stanley S. Bennet (November 7, 1867-September 15, 1942) spent his life in Quitman as a lawyer and public official. He served six consecutive two-year terms as mayor, held Brooks County's chief school office for twenty years, represented the Seventh District in the Georgia Senate, and worked on the State Highway Board through 1929. His business and transportation interests included two Quitman banks, Atlantic Coast Line legal work, the proposed South Georgia Railroad, and a later electric railway proposal.[1-8]

    Name and identity

    The family spelling was Bennet, with one final t. Newspapers and Mercer's alumni bulletin often printed Bennett. Place, profession, relatives, and offices make clear that both spellings referred to the same Quitman lawyer.[1][2][7]

    His middle initial requires care. Find a Grave memorial 151026461 expands it as Spencer, and Huxford names his son Stanley Spencer Bennet Jr. Those two details make Spencer a strong-probable expansion, but no inspected contemporary record spells out the father's middle name. The dossier therefore retains Stanley S. Bennet as the controlled form.[1][3]

    He was not his brother Samuel Stevens Bennet, an Albany lawyer, or his son Stanley Spencer Bennet Jr., who joined the Brooks County bar in 1922. He also must be distinguished from his father, Judge William Baker Bennet, and his lawyer brothers Joseph W., Matt C., Hilliard G., and William B. Bennet II.[1]

    Family and education

    Bennet was born at Quitman on November 7, 1867. His parents were William Baker Bennet and Martha J. Campbell Bennet. William was a lawyer, judge, legislator, school commissioner, Baptist minister, and Confederate veteran. Martha was a daughter of the Rev. Jesse H. Campbell. Huxford says all six Bennet sons who reached adulthood became lawyers.[1]

    The educational record contains a genuine date conflict. Mercer's contemporary alumni bulletin places Stanley S. Bennett, A.B., in the section headed 1887. It says he taught a private school in Quitman from 1888 to 1890 and was admitted to the bar in 1890. Huxford instead says he graduated in 1888 and gives December 20, 1889, as his admission date. The safe conclusion is that he earned an A.B. from Mercer in the late 1880s, taught locally, read law with his father, and entered practice in 1889 or 1890.[1][2]

    He married Minnie Parks Hightower in 1892. The family memorial names four children who survived him:

    • Louise Bennet, later dean of women at Shorter College;
    • Stanley Spencer Bennet Jr., a Quitman lawyer;
    • Paul H. Bennet, a farmer and businessman who chaired a wartime Office of Price Administration board; and
    • Mildred Bennet, a Quitman teacher.[1]

    A February 1900 report says Mayor and Mrs. S. S. Bennett had become parents of a son during the preceding week. Huxford's family account identifies that child as Stanley Spencer Jr.[1][9]

    Find a Grave records Minnie as Minnie Parks Hightower Bennet, 1869-1957, and links her to Stanley. The memorial is derivative cemetery evidence rather than an original marriage return.[3]

    The memorial also links likely 1900, 1910, and 1920 census entries for Stanley in Quitman. Their Ancestry record IDs are preserved in the source list, but the complete household fields and original images were not independently recovered. They are useful continuity leads, not the basis for the child list or exact family dates.[4][7][8]

    Law practice

    After admission, Bennet practiced with his father as Bennet & Bennet. He later worked with William H. Long and Sam T. Harrell. By 1912 the firm was Bennet, Long & Harrell; Long's election to the City Court bench led to the Bennet & Harrell partnership.[1]

    His practice reached Georgia's appellate courts. In Pike v. State, an appeal from Brooks Superior Court, Stanley S. Bennet appeared with W. S. Humphreys and J. D. Kilpatrick for the plaintiff in error. The judgment was affirmed.[10]

    In 1905 he represented Perry Peeples in a prosecution arising from complaints made by Barney citizens and the town. The state failed to substantiate its accusations, and the contemporary account identifies Senator Bennet as defense counsel.[11]

    Huxford describes Bennet as Brooks County attorney for much of the fifty years beginning in 1892 and as longtime Atlantic Coast Line division counsel. Contemporary court reporting places him in cases involving railroad counsel, but no inspected Atlantic Coast Line appointment or personnel record fixes the beginning or length of that service.[1][12]

    Mayor of Quitman

    Bennet served as mayor for the paired terms 1892-1893, 1894-1895, 1896-1897, 1898-1899, 1900-1901, and 1902-1903. Huxford's municipal table supplies the complete run, while contemporary election reports verify its later years.[1]

    The March 1900 mass meeting unanimously nominated the incumbent for another two-year term. The report said he had already completed four terms. In March 1902 he was again nominated for mayor and the board of education, and the nomination was treated as election.[13-15]

    His administration confronted the cost of municipal infrastructure. Bennet and the aldermen called Quitman's first bond election for October 4, 1893, proposing $15,000 for a light and water system. The episode belongs to his municipal record whether or not every feature of the original proposal was ultimately constructed as first planned.[1]

    Schools and civic work

    Bennet served as Brooks County school commissioner or superintendent from March 1892 until June 1912. Huxford's biographical sketch shortens the service to 1892-1904, but its detailed office table extends it to 1912. A contemporary retirement notice resolves the conflict: Bennet left the post in June 1912 after twenty continuous years.[1][16]

    He remained active in local education and culture. In 1912 he joined S. S. Rountree, H. W. Stubbs, D. G. Malloy, and Lee W. Branch on the committee responsible for Quitman's Carnegie library building.[1]

    Huxford says Bennet served four years as worshipful master of Sholto Masonic Lodge. A July 1902 newspaper report independently places him in lodge work, presenting Dr. M. C. Wilkinson with a past master's jewel on behalf of the Quitman Masons. Huxford also records Rotary membership, Baptist affiliation, and nineteen years leading the men's Bible class.[1][17]

    At a countywide farmers' rally in November 1913, Bennet delivered the welcome before an estimated crowd of three to four thousand. The program joined farm improvement, livestock, transportation, and local economic development.[18]

    His public activity was partisan as well as civic. In September 1894 he and W. S. Humphreys addressed a Brooks County Democratic rally and barbecue.[37]

    Georgia Senate

    Bennet represented the Seventh Senatorial District in the 1905-1907 term. The official 1905 Senate journal lists S. S. Bennet in the chamber, and Georgia's 1925 official register independently records the term. A March 1904 report had described him as unopposed for the seat.[1][5][6][19]

    Huxford says Bennet served in both houses of the General Assembly. The inspected official House roster does not support a House term, and its 1905-1906 Brooks County entry names Lee W. Branch. Without a House journal entry or election return, Bennet's service in the lower chamber remains unresolved and should not be stated as fact.[1][6]

    Banking and manufacturing

    Bennet helped incorporate Citizens Bank of Quitman in December 1905 with W. W. Walker, J. H. McCall, R. A. Griffin, and I. N. Johnson. The bank had $50,000 capital. Its first reported officers were Bennet as president, Walker as vice president, and J. Russell Davis as cashier.[20][21]

    The leadership soon changed. By February 1908 Walker was president and Bennet vice president, a position Bennet still held in a 1911 Quitman banking profile. This was a progression of offices, not a continuous Bennet presidency.[22][23]

    He also served on the Bank of Quitman board. The 1926 annual meeting returned Bennet with S. S. Rountree, H. W. Stubbs, Joseph D. Wilson, E. L. Jelks, Denmark Groover, and S. M. Turner. Huxford identifies Bennet as that bank's attorney as well.[1][24]

    When Atlantic & Gulf Mills was reorganized as the Quitman Manufacturing Company in 1914, Huxford included Bennet among the local stockholders. The located evidence does not establish an executive office for him in the company.[1]

    South Georgia Railroad

    Huxford lists S. S. Bennet among the twenty-six applicants for the South Georgia Railroad charter in 1896. The identity is strong probable: Stanley was then Quitman's mayor and a practicing lawyer, Huxford uses the same initials in the charter list and biography, and his later railroad promotion supplies a consistent identity chain. The original petition has not been located, so the initials-to-full-name identification is not original-record verified.[1]

    Bennet was not elected to the first board and held no first officer post. The March 23, 1896 organization report names the nine directors and the president, vice president, treasurer, and superintendent without Bennet. A petitioner should not be converted into a founding director.[25]

    He took a direct role in a later expansion discussion. Bennet chaired a February 23, 1899 Quitman mass meeting called to consider a railroad southward from town. The meeting appointed Francis J. Spain, John G. McCall, and Joseph Mabbett to investigate. Contemporary reporting said the proposed line might become an extension of the South Georgia Railway. The meeting proves Bennet's leadership in the discussion, but it does not make him a company director or officer.[26]

    Electric railway proposal

    In 1907 Bennet joined the published application for the Quitman, Valdosta and Thomasville Electric Railway and Power Company. The proposed fifty-mile line would run from Valdosta through Blue Springs and Quitman to Thomasville, provide local street service, cross the South Georgia Railroad, and furnish electric light and power.[27-29]

    On May 23, 1907, Bennet, A. C. McLeod, and W. A. May swore that the petition's signatures were genuine and its facts true to the best of their knowledge. The legal notices verify Bennet as petitioner and affiant. They do not prove that the charter was granted, shares were paid for, or the railway was constructed.[27-29]

    State Highway Board

    By January 1925 Bennet was serving on the State Highway Board. On July 15 the Georgia Senate confirmed Governor Clifford Walker's appointment of S. S. Bennett to succeed himself after a forty-five-minute executive session. Georgia's official register lists his commission through January 1, 1930.[6][30][31]

    Huxford calls him a member of the first State Highway Board. The official register and confirmation prove his later tenure but do not, by themselves, establish his exact initial appointment date. The first-board claim therefore remains Huxford-supported rather than independently verified.[1][6]

    His board service was active. Bennet spoke at the July 1926 dedication of Bainbridge's Flint River Memorial Bridge. He inspected flood-blocked roads around Quitman in August 1928, discussed the planned paving of the highway from the Thomas County line into Quitman in February 1929, and joined the Atlanta-Rome motorcade marking completion of that paved route in December 1929.[32-36]

    The Georgia Senate confirmed W. C. Vereen as his successor in August 1929; the official register gives January 1, 1930, as Bennet's commission endpoint.[6][38]

    Death and burial

    Huxford's memorial says Bennet died at his Quitman home on September 15, 1942. It names Minnie and the four children as survivors. Find a Grave memorial 151026461 gives the same birth and death dates and places him in Oak Hill Cemetery in Quitman.[1][3]

    No identity-secure obituary, funeral notice, Georgia death certificate, burial register, or readable marker transcription was found in the documented online evidence. The date is strongly supported by two matching derivative sources. Oak Hill burial is strong probable rather than verified from a cemetery register or inspected marker.[1][3]

    Research limits

    The documented research covered local project files; Huxford's county history; Mercer's alumni bulletin; official Georgia Senate and state-register volumes; available census and genealogy indexes; Find a Grave; Georgia Historic Newspapers; saved Newspapers.com result sets; and targeted searches for family, school, law, municipal, legislative, bank, manufacturing, railroad, highway, church, lodge, death, funeral, and burial evidence. Name forms included Stanley S. Bennet, Stanley S. Bennett, S. S. Bennet, S. S. Bennett, Stanley Bennet, and Stanley Bennett.

    Not found in the documented online evidence were an original birth or baptism record, marriage return, complete federal census household images, the original bar-admission minute, county school-board minutes, a Georgia House journal entry, the original 1896 South Georgia Railroad petition, a South Georgia Railway office or directorship, an Atlantic Coast Line appointment record, the original first Highway Board appointment, a death certificate, an identity-secure obituary or funeral notice, a burial register, or an inspected readable grave marker.
