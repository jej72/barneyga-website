    Hugh Archibald McEachern

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    Naval-stores executive, newspaper owner, civic officer, proposed postmaster, and South Georgia Railroad charter petitioner

    Identification

    The H. A. McEachern associated with the South Georgia Railroad was Hugh Archibald McEachern, born in Robeson County, North Carolina, on December 24, 1856. He died at his Jacksonville home on December 23, 1905, one day before his forty-ninth birthday.[1]

    This identification rests on a connected record rather than initials alone. In January 1892, federal postal papers were addressed to H. A. McEachern near Cecil, Georgia, and signed in full by Hugh A. McEachern. Contemporary reports next place H. A. McEachern in Quitman as a turpentine-tool manufacturer, newspaper editor, and school-board member. A 1900 charter petition names Hugh A. McEachern of Lowndes County in the related barrel-head business. His Jacksonville obituary then traces the same career from North Carolina teaching through Georgia naval stores and into Florida.[2]

    Huxford's history names H. A. McEachern among the petitioners for the South Georgia Railroad charter. No competing man with those initials was found in Quitman's business and civic record during the incorporation period. The railroad identification is therefore strong probable. It remains one step below verified because the original charter petition has not been located.[3]

    North Carolina family and early life

    The 1860 census recorded three-year-old H. A. McEachern in the Robeson County household of Neill C. and Ellen H. McEachern. They are his strong-probable parents. The census did not state household relationships, so the conclusion cannot be treated like a modern birth certificate.[4] A later profile of his brother A. R. McEachern also named Neill C. and Ellen Powers McEachern as the parents, providing useful independent support.[5]

    By 1870 Hugh was living with Christy and Mary McEachern in St. Pauls Township. The 1880 census described him as Christy's unmarried nephew and a schoolteacher. That occupation agrees with his 1905 obituary, which said he taught in his native county before following the turpentine trade southward.[6] The records reviewed do not explain when his parents died or why he entered Christy and Mary's household.

    Hugh married Fannie Morrison in Columbus County, North Carolina, on June 1, 1882.[7] A cemetery-card index and memorial place her in Whiteville Memorial Cemetery. The memorial gives her death as December 3, 1887, while the index appears to label that date as her burial date. The underlying card image was not inspected, so her exact death date remains unresolved.[8]

    He married Flora Morrison in Columbus County on December 18, 1889.[9] Francis Morrison, apparently Fannie, and a younger Flora Morrison appear in the same 1870 Whiteville household of M. R. and Malissa Morrison. That makes it strongly probable that Hugh married two sisters. Since the 1870 census did not state relationships and the original marriage images were unavailable, this relationship remains unproved.

    Brooks County and the proposed McEachern post office

    The earliest Georgia record found is a Post Office Department location packet dated January 6, 1892. It concerned a proposed Brooks County office called McEachern. Hugh signed as the proposed postmaster, and the accompanying diagram placed the location near Cecil, Adel, Hahira, Little River, and Ocapilco Creek.[10]

    The papers prove that the federal department considered the proposal and that Hugh was its intended postmaster. They do not prove that the office opened, that he received a formal appointment, or that he owned the settlement site. No appointment register or later post-office record was found online. The surname on the proposal is important evidence of Hugh's presence in the northern Brooks County area, but it should not be turned into a claim that he founded or owned a town.

    Huxford lists Hugh as foreman of the Brooks Superior Court grand jury for the May 1895 term.[11] This is a compiled transcription, not an inspected original presentment. It nevertheless fits the contemporary record of a man entering Quitman's business and civic life.

    Turpentine equipment and the Quitman Free-Press

    On October 9, 1895, a special term of Brooks Superior Court granted a charter to the Blount Turpentine Tool Company. The $15,000 company joined H. A. McEachern and Edward Blount of Quitman with A. C. McLeod of Valdosta and J. R. Young of Savannah. It planned to manufacture patented barrel rims and heads, along with hacks, hackstocks, pullers, and scrapers used in turpentine work.[12] The venture puts Hugh inside the naval-stores supply business before the railroad was incorporated.

    He was also briefly a newspaper proprietor and editor. Huxford wrote that Hugh and James J. Reese bought the Quitman Free-Press in September 1895, merged Reese's Brooks County News into it, and incorporated the Free-Press Publishing Company in February 1896.[13] A contemporary newspaper called McEachern the Free Press editor in October 1895.[14] In September 1896, reports said that he sold his interest to attorney W. C. Wade so he could devote his full time to other business.[15]

    The contemporary reports verify his editorial and financial interest and its sale. Huxford is currently the source for the exact purchase, merger, and February incorporation sequence. No original publishing-company charter file was found.

    Civic service in Quitman

    By March 1896 Hugh was one of five sitting members of the Quitman board of education. Citizens renominated the full board, and the Savannah report credited the town with an unusually strong graded-school system for a place of about 2,500 people.[16] The articles do not give the date he first joined the board.

    This position, the grand-jury listing, and his newspaper work show a short but substantial period of civic involvement. They do not establish election to any other city or county office.

    The South Georgia Railroad

    Huxford lists H. A. McEachern among twenty-six petitioners for the South Georgia Railroad charter. The proposed capital was $65,000, divided into 650 shares of $100 each.[3] By March 23, 1896, stockholders had accepted the charter, elected directors and officers, and called the first ten percent payment on subscribed stock.[17]

    Hugh's role must be stated narrowly. He was not named to the January subscription committee. He was not elected to the first board and held no first officer post. No located stock ledger says how many shares he subscribed for, how much he paid, or whether he performed right-of-way or construction work. The evidence supports charter petitioner or incorporator, not railroad manager.

    His commercial interests made the route relevant to him. The line would connect Quitman with Adel through a timber and turpentine district where he already had business ties. That is reasonable context, but no source states his personal motive for joining the petition.

    Valdosta and Ellis-Young

    The Valdosta Times reported on January 29, 1898, that McEachern, formerly of Quitman and then representing Ellis, Young Company of Savannah, had moved his family to Valdosta. They occupied a residence on Toombs Street.[18]

    In January 1899, an advertisement signed H. A. McEachern, Valdosta, sought an experienced and sober man either to buy an interest in a first-class turpentine place or serve as its superintendent.[19]

    A later history of the Valdosta Presbyterian church listed Hugh as an elder from 1899 through 1904.[20] The dates were published in 1910 and are useful retrospective evidence, although they are not contemporary session minutes.

    In May 1900 Hugh, identified by full name as a resident of Lowndes County, joined John R. Young and John W. Motte Jr. in petitioning for the Standard Barrel Head Company. The Savannah-based company proposed to make patented and ordinary barrel heads, kegs, tubs, staves, casks, and related wooden goods. Initial capital was $15,000, with authority requested to increase it to $50,000.[21]

    The 1895 and 1900 charters show continuity. In both, Hugh worked with J. R. Young in businesses supplying containers or equipment to the naval-stores trade.

    Jacksonville and the naval-stores industry

    Hugh moved to Jacksonville in 1900. His obituary said that he became an incorporator and large shareholder of Mutual Naval Stores Company, then served as vice president and general manager under A. S. McMillan and D. H. McMillan.[1]

    He helped organize the Turpentine Operators' Association in August 1901. Speaking as both an operator and factor, he urged a central body with local branches and served on the committee that drafted its constitution and bylaws. The attendance list associated him with Mutual Naval Stores and with H. A. McEachern & Co. at Raiford, Florida, where one still was reported.[22] At the permanent convention in September, members elected him treasurer without opposition.[23]

    The association also dealt with production and storage. In 1902 Hugh argued that operators should reduce output so they could earn more while conserving timber. In 1903 he served on a three-man committee considering a tank and warehouse company.[24]

    When six naval-stores concerns combined in October 1902 to create Consolidated Naval Stores Company, Hugh became a vice president and director.[25] A charter notice listed him as subscribing for 1,000 shares at $100 each, a nominal total of $100,000. The notice also indicated that allocations would be settled after appraisal of the constituent companies. It does not prove that he paid $100,000 in cash or retained that exact holding.[26]

    By December 1904 he was still a Consolidated vice president. A Valdosta report said he acquired a half interest from M. M. Smith in a 70,000-acre tract in DeSoto County, Florida. It described the amount involved as $150,000 after delays for title examination.[27] The deed was not found. The article does not say clearly whether $150,000 was the price of Hugh's half interest or the value of the broader transaction.

    Banking, development, church, and community work

    By March 1905 Hugh was president of Jacksonville Development Company. The company planned to acquire and improve suburban land, construct homes chosen by buyers, provide water service, and sell on installment terms.[28] He was also a director of Florida Bank and Trust Company, a member of Jacksonville's Board of Trade, and active on the board of First Presbyterian Church.[1][32] A February 1905 report placed him on the finance committee for a large interdenominational revival.[29]

    These positions show how far his career had moved from teaching and turpentine tools. By the final year of his life he worked across naval-stores finance, banking, timberland, and residential development. The evidence does not support calling him a railroad executive or assigning his later wealth to the South Georgia Railroad.

    Final illness, will, and death

    Hugh signed his will on November 29, 1905. Probate indexes in Duval County, Florida, and Robeson County, North Carolina, name his wife Flora and daughter Edna. They disagree on a brother's initials, reading them as A. B. in Florida and A. R. in North Carolina. The will image was not inspected, so that discrepancy remains unresolved.[30]

    He had been ill for about six months when he died at 10:30 on the morning of December 23 at 229 West Ashley Street. His wife and five children were with him.[1] Funeral services were held at the home the following afternoon. First Presbyterian pastor William E. Boggs officiated with W. A. Hobson of First Baptist Church. The Board of Trade attended, and Solomon Lodge No. 20 conducted Masonic rites at Evergreen Cemetery.[31]

    The Jacksonville Board of Trade adopted a memorial by a unanimous rising vote in January 1906. It recalled his six years in the city, his work in naval stores, banking, and development, and his standing in the southern pine belt.[32] One OCR rendering gives his death date as December 3. The obituary, funeral sequence, and a parallel printing of the memorial establish December 23.

    Children and later household

    The 1910 census found Flora, a widow, still at 229 West Ashley Street. It reported that she had borne six children and that five were living. The household and later records identify the five survivors:[33]

    Child Record trail
    Edna Earle McEachern Born about 1885 or 1886; named in Hugh's will; married Charles Seton Fleming in 1906
    McLeod A. McEachern Age seventeen in 1910; apparently the Archibald McEachern in Flora's 1920 household
    Ruth T. McEachern Age seventeen in 1910; present in Flora's household
    Marie B. McEachern Age fifteen in 1910; later recorded as Marie Nobbs
    Hugh A. McEachern Jr. Age five in 1910; remained with Flora through the 1930 census

    The sixth child, who died before 1910, has not been identified. McLeod and Ruth were both recorded as seventeen, but the index alone is not enough to call them twins. Edna was born during Hugh's marriage to Fannie and is securely documented as his daughter. The exact mother-child relationships for the later children were not independently established by birth records.

    Flora remained in Jacksonville. The 1920 census again placed her at the Ashley Street house, and the 1930 census found her at 3527 Oak Street with Marie's family and Hugh Jr.[34] The Florida death index records Flora Morrison McEachern's death in Duval County in 1951. A cemetery memorial gives October 11, 1865, to February 24, 1951, but the exact birth date is derivative until a marker or civil record is inspected.[35]

    Later Jacksonville references to Hugh A. McEachern may concern Hugh Jr., born around 1905. They should not be assigned to his father, who died that year.

    What remains unresolved

    • The original South Georgia Railroad charter petition has not been found. Huxford remains the source naming Hugh as a petitioner.
    • No evidence places him on the subscription committee, first board, or officer slate, and no stock subscription amount is known.
    • The 1892 postal packet does not prove that the McEachern office opened or that Hugh owned the site.
    • The original Free-Press Publishing Company charter and the underlying DeSoto County deed have not been located.
    • Neill and Ellen are strong-probable parents, but no inspected birth or baptismal record states the relationship.
    • Fannie and Flora were probably sisters, but that relationship has not been verified from an original record.
    • Hugh and Flora's sixth child remains unidentified.
    • The original will image is needed to resolve whether Hugh named a brother A. R. or A. B. McEachern.

    Assessment

    Hugh McEachern had a limited, documented role in the South Georgia Railroad. He joined the charter effort while he was a Quitman manufacturer, editor, and school-board member with direct interests in the timber economy the line hoped to serve. The surviving evidence does not show him financing or managing the railroad after organization.

    His larger career belonged to naval stores. Over little more than a decade, he moved from making turpentine tools and barrel parts to representing a Savannah factor, operating a still, organizing a trade association, managing a major factorage company, acquiring timberland interests, and entering banking and real-estate development. His Georgia record explains why his name appeared on the railroad petition. His Florida record shows that the petition was one early episode in a much broader business life.
