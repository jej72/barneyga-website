    Eugene Henry Beck

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    Eugene Henry Beck (July 13, 1850-February 22, 1935) was an educator, legislator, farm owner, and railroad promoter. Stephen W. Edmondson's local history says Beck became principal of Barney School in 1908. Contemporary records place him teaching in Barney, representing Brooks County in the 1911-1912 General Assembly, promoting the proposed Georgia, Alabama and Western Railroad, and running for state school superintendent in 1912. He later served as Lowndes County school superintendent.[1][4][5][8][12][14][20][27]

    Beck's career in education lasted more than fifty years. A 1933 profile reported that he taught in Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas and served in both houses of the Georgia General Assembly.[4] Contemporary records also document his early attempt to remove Georgia's male-only restriction on admission to the bar. A few details remain unsettled. His exact legislative term in the early 1880s has not been verified from an official roster, and derivative family sources conflict over the date and order of some marriages.

    Identity and early life

    The E. H. Beck associated with Barney was Eugene Henry Beck. His 1935 death certificate gives his full name, birth at Eton in Murray County on July 13, 1850, and parents J. W. Beck and Margery Stuart. His son Henry H. Beck supplied the information. The certificate identifies Beck as an educator and records his death at Valdosta on February 22, 1935, followed by burial at Sunset Hill Cemetery the next day.[1]

    The 1860 census places Eugene, indexed as Ugene H. Beck, in Murray County with John W. and Margry Beck and their other children. This childhood household agrees with the death certificate.[2] Beck's photographed grave marker reads “Eugene Henry Beck / 1850-1935” and bears a Masonic emblem.[3]

    A long newspaper profile published in 1933 supplies the fullest account of his early career. Beck said his family left Murray County during the Civil War while his father served as a captain in the 60th Georgia. The family later lived near Palmetto in Campbell County. Beck recalled selling crossties and fuel to help pay for his education at Kentucky Wesleyan, where he said he completed a four-year course in three years. The profile is a contemporary interview with an elderly Beck, so it is valuable first-person recollection rather than a college record.[4]

    The same profile says he taught in Campbell County and Homer, Louisiana, then joined the State College of Agricultural and Mechanical Arts at Dahlonega at Governor Joseph E. Brown's request. He taught there for eight years. He later worked at Rusk Masonic Institute in Texas before returning to Georgia.[4] No diploma, college register, or teacher-license record was located online, so those details remain supported by the interview rather than institutional files.

    Marriage and family

    An original Lumpkin County record shows Eugene H. Beck marrying Eva Rivers on October 20, 1883.[6] Later in life he was married to Edmonia Hopson. The 1910 Barney census lists Eugene, age 59, with Edmonia, 26, daughter Eugenia, 3, and infant son Henry H.[5] The 1920 and 1930 censuses continue the household with Edmonia, first in Banks County and later in Valdosta.[7]

    The cemetery memorial links Beck to several spouses and children, but its family account is internally inconsistent. It dates his marriage to Edmonia to 1905 in one place and 1906 in another, and it disagrees with itself about whether she was his third or fourth wife.[3] No original Florida marriage record was located during this search. The date and order of that marriage should therefore remain unresolved.

    The memorial links several children to the family. The 1910 census independently verifies Eugenia and Henry in the household. A 1947 profile of Henry H. Beck says he was born at Barney and was the son of the late E. H. Beck, who died in 1935. That article provides an additional bridge from the Barney household to the later Valdosta family.[23]

    Barney educator

    Local historian Stephen W. Edmondson wrote that Beck had taught at Pavo and Morven before replacing C. W. Murphy as principal of Barney School in 1908. According to Edmondson, Beck remained through 1912, then returned to north Georgia. D. H. Purvis taught with him.[8] The 1910 census independently confirms that Eugene H. Beck lived at Barney and worked as a public-school teacher.[5]

    The exact end of Beck's principalship is less certain than Edmondson's year range suggests. A June 1912 report praised S. A. Boone and Miss Hollingsworth for their work at Barney School while naming Beck as master of ceremonies at the closing rally.[11] Beck was still active in the school's public program, but that article indicates Boone was then teaching there. No school-board minutes or contract records were located to fix the changeover date.

    Contemporary reports show that Beck's educational work extended beyond his own classroom. Governor Hoke Smith appointed him in 1911 to the trustees of the new South Georgia Agricultural, Industrial and Normal College at Valdosta. Another report listed him among the trustees charged with choosing the site.[9][10] The institution later became South Georgia State Normal College and belongs to the institutional history of Valdosta State University.

    At a large educational rally in Barney in June 1912, Beck served as master of ceremonies and used the gathering as part of his campaign for state school superintendent.[11] He challenged incumbent M. L. Brittain in the Democratic primary. Contemporary candidate lists show the contest, and post-election coverage reports Brittain as the easy winner. No reliable statewide vote total was recovered.[12][13]

    Brooks County legislator

    A statewide roster printed in August 1910 identifies “E. H. Beck, Barney” as Brooks County's representative. The original page was reviewed by James E. Jones and independently matched to Eugene H. Beck, the public-school teacher in the 1910 Barney census. A parallel roster appeared the following day.[14][15] Beck therefore represented Brooks County in the 1911-1912 General Assembly.

    This was not his first legislative service. A 1911 profile said he had served with C. R. Pendleton in the early 1880s, then went West before returning to Georgia.[16] Beck's 1933 profile says he was first elected from Lumpkin County in 1882 and later represented Murray County in 1915 and the 43rd Senate District in 1917.[4] The later terms fit his documented move back to north Georgia. The exact early House term still needs confirmation from an official roster or journal.

    The effort to admit women to the Georgia bar

    Beck sponsored legislation in 1911 and 1912 to remove Georgia's male-only restriction on admission to the bar. The 1912 proposal would have substituted “all citizens” for the sex-specific language governing qualified applicants. It followed Atlanta Law School graduate Georgia McIntyre Weaver's exclusion from practice.[17]

    Beck's 1911 proposal initially met resistance in committee, was reconsidered, and then failed in the House by a vote of 85 to 77. Because 93 affirmative votes were required, it needed eight more votes to pass. A Mercer Law history independently confirms that sequence.[17][18] Beck returned with revised language in 1912, but the bill number and final disposition were not recovered. Georgia did not admit women to the bar on equal terms until 1916. The evidence supports Beck's advocacy, but it does not support a claim that his 1912 version passed.

    Farm ownership at Barney

    Beck was a teacher who also owned a farm near Barney. In November 1911, a contemporary report said he sold his farm 1 1/2 miles east of town to William C. Hendrix of LaFayette for $4,000. Hendrix's son Tildon was expected to occupy it.[19]

    The article verifies the location, sale, buyer, and reported price. It does not give acreage, a legal description, deed-book reference, mortgage terms, or Beck's purchase date. No online deed or tax record supplied those missing details. The 1910 census calls Beck a teacher, so the safest description is that he was an educator who owned a farm, not that farming was his principal occupation.[5]

    Georgia, Alabama and Western Railroad

    Beck joined T. A. Rozier and Fleming Bates Walker of Barney among the ten petitioners for the Georgia, Alabama and Western Railroad Company. The petition printed Walker's name as “F. B. Walker.” The 1911 charter notice proposed a line of about 150 miles from Milltown through Hahira, Barney, Pavo, Meigs, Coolidge, Colquitt, and Blakely to the Alabama line. The organizers proposed $200,000 in common stock, with authority to increase it to $3 million.[20]

    He did more than sign the petition. A November 1911 dispatch called the organizers promoters and reported that Professor Beck had returned from Atlanta in the railroad's interest. He believed the remaining portion of the initial stock issue could be placed.[21] The Secretary of State granted the charter on January 25, 1912.[22]

    Reports from the board meeting on May 31 named Beck as a director and the company's financial agent at Barney. The board authorized a survey and construction-cost estimate and discussed a larger Brunswick-to-Dothan route.[24][25] No located source states how much stock Beck subscribed, how much capital the company collected, or whether any stock subscription became fully paid.

    No stock ledger, bank record, right-of-way deed, engineering estimate, construction contract, or operating return was located. Georgia's official 1916 railroad map, showing mileage in operation as of June 30, 1915, has no east-west railroad through Barney.[26] Beck's verified roles were promoter, incorporator, director, and financial agent of a chartered project. The evidence does not show that the company placed track in operation.

    Later educational career

    After leaving Barney, Beck returned to north Georgia. His 1933 profile says he taught at Eton, represented Murray County in 1915, and served in the state senate in 1917.[4] Contemporary 1915 House rosters also identify “E. H. Beck, Eton” as Murray County's representative.[31][32] An unidentified 1935 obituary clipping says Barrow County instead, but the contemporary rosters and Beck's own later profile support Murray. Barrow is probably an obituary error.[30] The 1920 census places him in Banks County with Edmonia.[7]

    By the mid-1920s he was Lowndes County school superintendent. Georgia's official 1925 register lists E. H. Beck in that office.[27] A 1926 Barney school-improvement report identifies Superintendent Beck as a former Barney teacher, directly connecting the Lowndes official to the earlier educator.[28] He was still superintendent in 1930, and the 1933 profile says his second four-year term ended at the beginning of that year.[4][29]

    Beck died at his Valdosta home on February 22, 1935, after a long illness. A contemporary obituary remembered his education work and legislative career and reported that he had taught more than 8,000 students. Some of its legislative claims require separate official confirmation, but its date, place, and general career agree with the death certificate and earlier profile.[1][30]

    Search coverage and limits

    Research began with the Barney census, local histories, stored newspaper collections, prior railroad work, memory files, and earlier user-reviewed images. Three independent research lanes then covered genealogy and education, public office and civic work, and property, business, and the railroad.

    Online work checked federal census indexes for 1850 through 1930, Georgia death and marriage records, Find a Grave, Georgia Historic Newspapers, Newspapers.com, the 1925 Georgia official register, the Railroad Commission's 1916 map, Mercer Law's history of the women-lawyers bill, Georgia Archives legislative guidance, Internet Archive, and Google Books. Searches used full-name, initial, spelling, occupation, office, family, place, school, property, election, bill, railroad, death, and obituary combinations.

    The investigation was broad online research, not a courthouse or archive-room examination. The 1870 and 1900 census entries, original Edmonia marriage record, Brooks County deed, railway company files, exact early House roster, and 1912 bill disposition remain the clearest targets for future work.
