    Judge James Oliver Morton

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    December 20, 1820 to October 16, 1911 | farmer, enslaver, Inferior Court justice, county commissioner, banker, civic organizer, and railroad charter petitioner

    Identity and evidence

    James Oliver Morton spent most of his adult life on the southern edge of Quitman, Georgia. In public records his name appears as James O. Morton, J. O. Morton, James Morton, and, in two mistranscribed census indexes, Jas O. Martin and James O. Morten. The title "Judge" came from his service on the Brooks County Inferior Court. It did not mean that he was a Superior Court judge.[1]

    Morton can be followed as the same man across the 1850, 1860, 1870, 1880, and 1910 federal censuses. His age, Georgia birthplace, wife Sarah or Sallie, children Simeon and Lavinia, occupation, and Lowndes-to-Brooks residence all align.[2][3][4][5][6] Contemporary notices about his bank presidency, farm, county offices, and death match that record. No material evidence found in this search belongs to a competing James Oliver Morton in Brooks County.

    Early life, marriage, and migration

    Morton was born in Screven County, Georgia, on December 20, 1820. The fullest local biography names his parents as Silas Morton and Sabina Archer and says that he studied in local schools and at Chatham Academy in Savannah. It also says he married Sarah Young in August 1843, then moved to the part of Lowndes County that became Brooks County in 1858.[7] Two later genealogical books agree on the 1820 birth year, 1843 marriage, Sarah Young, and the names of their children, but neither is an original civil record.[8][9]

    The original marriage entry was not found in the Georgia marriage indexes searched for this dossier. The 1910 census offers useful independent support: James and Sarah reported that they had been married for 66 years, placing their marriage about 1843 or 1844.[6] The precise August 1843 date therefore rests on the local biography and family-history compilations.

    In 1850 the Mortons lived in Lowndes County. James was 29, Sarah 24, Simeon 6, and Lavinia 3. Morton was a farmer with $5,000 in real estate.[2] In 1860, after Brooks County was created, the same family lived near Tallokas. His census entry reported $15,000 in real estate and $22,915 in personal property.[3]

    Household and children

    James and Sarah had two known children. Simeon L. Morton was born about 1844, and Lavinia Calhoun Morton about 1847.[2][3] Huxford's family sketch says Simeon joined the Savannah Volunteer Guards and was killed in the final fighting around Richmond. That statement was not confirmed in a compiled military service record during this search, so it should be treated as a family-history claim rather than a settled military fact.[7]

    Lavinia married Henry Gray Turner, who became a Georgia legislator, congressman, and judge. The 1870 household shows Lavinia and Henry Turner living with her parents, along with their children James and infant Henry.[4] James and Sarah were alone in their 1880 household.[5] In 1910, both were still at their home on Nankin Road. James was 89 and Sarah 85.[6]

    Sarah's family was central to Morton's move and community standing. A newspaper account after the 1911 death of Sarah's sister Lavinia Young described the Young, Morton, and Avera families as early neighboring settlers whose original homes remained occupied. The article rounded Morton's age upward, but its account of the family cluster agrees with the censuses and local history.[10]

    Land, wealth, and slavery

    Morton's antebellum prosperity depended in substantial part on enslaved labor. The 1850 slave schedule lists seven people enslaved by James O. Morton: three males, ages 30, 22, and 20, and four females, ages 16, 16, 13, and 12.[11] By 1860 the schedule lists 24 people, ranging from infancy to age 50.[12] The schedules record age, sex, and color, but not names. No names for these 31 schedule entries were recovered.

    The rise in his reported wealth tracks that expansion. His real estate increased from $5,000 in 1850 to $15,000 in 1860, while the 1860 census assigned $22,915 to personal property, a category that included the monetary valuation of enslaved people.[2][3] The local family sketch describes the farm as land cleared with "the labor of a few slaves." The federal schedules show that phrase understates the size of the forced-labor operation by 1860.[7][11][12]

    Morton also appears on the September 11, 1850 federal agricultural schedule in Lowndes County. The online index identifies the schedule and page but does not transcribe the acreage, livestock, or production columns, so those figures are not claimed here.[13] In 1870, after emancipation and the Civil War, the census reported $14,000 in real estate and $3,000 in personal property. His household included two Black farm laborers, Matthew Young and Paul Colson.[4]

    His plantation remained active outside Quitman. An 1891 report placed it three miles from town.[14] An 1890 Quitman tax-list index identifies Morton and reports 20 employees, but the underlying columns were not transcribed in the online record.[15] The farm was still a large commercial operation after emancipation. The surviving records do not explain its labor contracts, wages, tenancy, or living conditions.

    County government and the title "Judge"

    Morton first held public office in Lowndes County. Huxford's office list places him on that county's Inferior Court from 1855 to 1859.[16] When Brooks County was organized, Governor Joseph E. Brown commissioned the first justices on December 11, 1858. Morton, John J. Pike, and James T. Robeson had served in Lowndes and continued in the new county. The Brooks minutes place Morton in court on January 15, 1859.[1]

    The best term reconstruction is more precise than the broad summaries printed after his death. Morton served from 1859 until 1861. He was elected again on January 10, 1861 but declined the new term, and Isaac Johnson took the seat on March 27. Morton returned on April 3, 1866 to complete William Hudson's unexpired term and served until 1868.[1] A 1911 obituary said he served only the first two years, while another said he served "throughout the sixties." Neither captures both terms.[17][18]

    The Inferior Court handled much of the county's daily administration. One surviving example authorized Morton in August 1860 to have a bridge built across the Okapilco at the old Stage Bridge.[19] He also served as foreman of the grand jury in February 1866 and November 1870.[20]

    Brooks County created a five-member Board of Commissioners in 1881. Morton was appointed to the first board and became its first chairman. He served from September 1881 until 1885, when he resigned and F. J. Spain was recommended for the vacancy.[21][22] This office, like the Inferior Court, put him directly into county finance and infrastructure rather than ordinary private litigation.

    Civil War record and its limits

    Morton's wartime record is not simple. An 1886 local retrospective said that when the Piscola Volunteers organized under their flag, Morton was first lieutenant. It dated that organization to June 1, 1861.[23] Huxford's military chapter gives a different chronology, placing the company's formation in July and its muster on July 23 as Company I, 26th Georgia Infantry. Morton does not appear on the final company roster printed there.[24]

    The same county history does identify him as adjutant of the 81st Battalion, Brooks County militia, commissioned March 4, 1862. Huxford cautioned that the militia records were incomplete.[25] No compiled Confederate service record for Morton was found in the reviewed material. The supportable conclusion is that he held local military responsibilities and was associated with the Piscola Volunteers at their early organization, but his service in the final Confederate company roster is unproved.

    His home front activity is clearer. At an August 10, 1861 meeting, Morton joined the committee that recommended a special county tax for destitute soldiers' families.[26] He was later chosen for a county committee that procured provisions for the army.[27] A disturbing 1864 newspaper report also identified enslaved people on Morton's and neighboring plantations among those punished after authorities alleged an insurrection plot. The account says confessions were extracted under the lash. It reflects both the violence of slavery and the local wartime system Morton helped govern.[28]

    Agriculture and community institutions

    Farming remained Morton's main occupation throughout the census record.[2][3][4][5] In March 1869 he helped organize the Brooks County Agricultural Society and became its first president. The society promoted improvements in farming, gardening, household production, and agricultural education.[29] He later served as master of the Quitman Grange, which won the best general Grange display at the 1877 Thomasville fair.[30]

    A detailed 1878 fair report shows Morton experimenting with crop methods. From one and a half acres he reported 90 bushels of non-rusting oats, followed by 300 bushels of sweet potatoes. He had sown the oats on January 14 and fertilized them with 25 bushels of cottonseed. He also displayed corn from the 1877 crop and explained that excluding light from a tightly closed crib prevented weevil damage.[31]

    Morton chaired the Brooks County Fair Association as it developed a permanent local fair in 1901. A 1900 meeting thanked him for attending every session and helping manage the exhibition, and Huxford identifies him as the permanent association's first president.[32][33]

    His civic work extended beyond agriculture. He was one of the directors selected when the Brooks County Library Association organized on January 31, 1880.[34] He served as a Quitman Academy trustee and joined the 1893 conveyance of academy property to the city school board.[35] In 1889 he became a director of the Quitman Hotel Company and served with R. C. McIntosh and J. C. McDonald on the committee that planned the Hotel Marie.[36][37]

    Morton also participated in commerce and local industry. He was a partner in W. Hudson & Company, a general merchandise firm formed in 1869, and was among the early directors of the Quitman Factory venture.[38] In March 1897, Quitman's largest recorded fire destroyed property belonging to him. Newspapers estimated his loss at $2,000 with $800 insurance. Two days later, his teams were clearing damaged machinery and he was preparing to rebuild in brick.[39][40]

    In 1904 a local report identified Morton as a stockholder in Quitman's new long-staple cotton mill. He marked the start of operations by feeding the first cotton into its picker.[41] His public work and private investments repeatedly met in projects meant to expand Quitman's economy.

    Bank of Quitman

    Morton's longest business association was with the Bank of Quitman. In 1878 he joined Clayton Groover and F. J. Spain in the private bank Denmark, Groover & Company. It was reorganized under a state charter as the Bank of Quitman in 1888, with Morton among the first directors.[42] When Clayton Groover and cashier E. A. Groover resigned in January 1895, Morton became president and Jeff Davis became cashier.[43]

    He remained president until his death. A 1905 profile reported capital of $100,000, surplus and undivided profits exceeding $67,000, deposits above $389,000, and loans of $428,589.95. It credited $112,000 in dividends since organization.[44] A 1911 report gave capital of $100,000 and surplus of $140,000. It emphasized the bank's farm-loan policy and the directors' weekly Thursday meetings, held without salaries.[45]

    That 1911 story called the 90-year-old Morton 96 and declared him the oldest bank president in the United States. October obituaries variously called him the oldest or second-oldest. No national banking census was found to prove either ranking.[17][18][46] The age error and disagreement make the national distinction unsafe. What is well supported is that he remained an active bank president into his nineties.

    The Atlanta Constitution reported that he worked at the bank as late as the Thursday before his death.[17] A more colorful Valdosta account said he signed papers without eyeglasses, told his driver he probably would not return, and set aside his funeral expenses before going to bed. That account also misstated his age, so its personal details should be read as contemporary remembrance rather than verified verbatim fact.[46] On November 5, the directors elected longtime vice president S. S. Rountree to succeed him.[47]

    Railroad roles

    Morton's direct place in the South Georgia Railroad was limited but real. He was one of the 26 men named in the February 1896 charter petition.[48] No checked source places him on the January subscription committee, the first board elected in March, or among the original officers. No stock ledger or subscription amount for him has been found. It is safest to call him a charter petitioner, not an operating officer or proven stockholder.

    He later took a documented role in the connected West Coast Railway. Morton attended its December 1900 stockholders' meeting at Greenville, Florida, with Henry G. Turner, F. J. Spain, R. C. McIntosh, S. S. Rountree, and Florida participants. The planned line would run from Greenville toward Perry and the Gulf coast and work closely with the South Georgia Railway.[49] The report proves that Morton took part as a West Coast Railway stockholder. It does not enlarge his office in the South Georgia Railroad itself.

    Final years, death, and burial

    The 1910 census found James and Sarah on Nankin Road, owning their home free of mortgage. The occupation field is poorly transcribed, but the employer and Bank of Quitman entries make the intended occupation clear.[6] A September 1911 newspaper described him as still active and living in the family's longstanding home.[10]

    Morton died at his home just south of Quitman at about 11 p.m. on Monday, October 16, 1911. He was 90 years old and two months short of his 91st birthday. The Atlanta Constitution reported that he had been ill only a few days. His wife Sarah and daughter Lavinia Turner survived him.[17] This corrects older secondary summaries that placed his death in September or made him 91 or 92.

    The funeral was held at the residence and conducted by the Rev. C. A. Campbell of First Presbyterian Church. Morton was buried in West End Cemetery in Quitman.[17] The Find a Grave index agrees on the birth date, death date, cemetery, wife, daughter, and father, but the contemporary funeral report is the stronger evidence for death and burial.[50] No civil death certificate, probate file, original cemetery register, or will was inspected.

    Morton helped organize Brooks County and spent half a century in its public and private institutions. He experimented with crops, ran a bank, supported schools and civic projects, and joined railroad promotion. He also built much of his early wealth through enslaved labor and exercised authority within a slave society. Any honest account of his place in Brooks County has to hold those facts together.

    Open questions and research limits

    • No original marriage register was found. August 1843 is supported by later family histories and broadly by the 1910 marriage-duration answer.
    • A targeted 1900 census index search did not identify James and Sarah Morton. Their documented presence in 1880 and 1910 leaves that one census-year residence unresolved.
    • The slave schedules do not name the people Morton enslaved. Deeds, probate records, labor contracts, and Freedmen's Bureau material were not located in the accessible online sweep.
    • The 1850 agricultural schedule was identified, but its image columns were not transcribed. The 1890 tax index's "20 employees" field was not verified against the original digest image.
    • No compiled military service record confirms service in the final Piscola Volunteers roster. The local-company retrospective and militia record support a narrower wartime role.
    • No evidence was found for the national rank claimed for Morton among elderly bank presidents.
    • No Bank of Quitman stock ledger or South Georgia Railroad subscription ledger was found.
