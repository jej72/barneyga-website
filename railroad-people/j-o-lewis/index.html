    John Owen Lewis

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    John Owen Lewis (May 10, 1864-October 17, 1939) was a Quitman merchant, banker, civic official, mill director, Presbyterian elder, and promoter of two railroad ventures. He spent his childhood at Scottsville in Albemarle County, Virginia, and settled in Quitman by 1890. His identity and life dates are verified through his original Georgia death certificate, a continuous 1870-1930 census chain, his 1893 marriage report, contemporary obituaries, and cemetery evidence.[2][3][4][5][6][7][8][9][26][27]

    Huxford lists “J. O. Lewis” among the twenty-six men who petitioned in 1896 for the charter of the South Georgia Railroad.[1] The petitioner was strongly probably John Owen Lewis. The identification rests on his residence, business standing, banking role, and later participation in another Quitman railway petition. It is not graded verified because the known charter list gives only initials, and the original 1896 petition or signature page has not been found.

    Identity and Virginia family

    Lewis was born at Scottsville, Virginia, on May 10, 1864. His original death certificate names his parents as John Owen Lewis, born in Albemarle County, and Sallie Hunt, born in Richmond.[2] The record calls him a retired general-merchandise merchant and gives his residence as 208 North Quincy Street in Quitman.

    The census provides a continuous childhood record. In 1870, seven-year-old John O. Lewis lived in St. Anne's Parish, Albemarle County, with his father and siblings. The father's first initial was indexed as C, but the household matches the family found in the next census.[3] In 1880, sixteen-year-old John O. Lewis was a student in the Scottsville household of John O. and Sallie Lewis.[4]

    Three generations carried nearly the same name. The subject's father was the elder John Owen Lewis of Scottsville. The Quitman merchant was sometimes called J. O. Lewis Jr. while his father was living. His own son, born in 1899, was also John Owen Lewis and later lived in Quanah and Acme, Texas. The 1939 obituaries for Lewis and his sister Minnie Lewis Rountree separate these men by residence and relationship.[26][28] Texas records for the son do not belong to the railroad petitioner.

    The former site estimate of about 1847-about 1931 came from a malformed obituary-index result and described no secure match. It is contradicted by Lewis's death certificate, census sequence, wife, children, and contemporary obituary. Those dates are rejected rather than retained as a competing lifespan.

    Marriage and household

    John O. Lewis married Rosa Lena McCall at Quitman Baptist Church on Wednesday evening, June 7, 1893. Rev. J. F. Eden performed the ceremony, and R. C. McIntosh was best man. A contemporary report described Lewis as the junior partner in R. C. McIntosh & Co., one of Quitman's larger mercantile firms, and as a former city councilman. Rosa Lena was a daughter of Capt. John G. McCall.[10]

    The 1900 census records Lewis as a grocery dealer who owned his home free of mortgage. Rosa and children Rosa Hunt, Minnie Clayborne, and John Owen were in the household.[5] In 1910, Lewis was a retail hardware merchant and employer at house 208. The index misreads him as John D. Lewis, but Rosa and all five children, the address, occupation, age, and birthplace establish that this was John O. Lewis.[6]

    The five children were Rosa Hunt, Minnie Clayborne, John Owen, Frank McCall, and Virgil Bobo Lewis. The 1900 and 1910 censuses, Huxford's McCall genealogy, and Lewis's obituary agree on this family group.[1][5][6][26] The obituary names the married daughters as Mrs. Allie McDonald and Mrs. James H. Rice. It places John in Quanah, Texas, Frank in Anderson, South Carolina, and Virgil in Quitman.[26]

    By 1920, Lewis described his work as farm real-estate dealing on his own account. The family still lived at 208 North Quincy Street.[7] In 1930, he was a real-estate dealer at 128 Quincy Street, in a home valued at $5,000.[8] The different street numbers may reflect a move or renumbering. The available records do not resolve which.

    Merchant career and property losses

    Lewis had entered Quitman's business community by 1890, when he joined the board of the Quitman Real Estate and Investment Company. A report said the year-old company had returned 13 percent after organizational expenses. It does not state Lewis's investment.[11] Huxford's transcription of an April 1897 business list identifies him as a groceries and hardware dealer.[1]

    Fire repeatedly threatened his property. In November 1897, a major Quitman fire reached his store building, but the surviving report did not list it among the buildings destroyed.[12] His residence burned on July 26, 1899. One account estimated the loss at $2,500 with $1,500 insurance. Another said nearly all the furniture was saved, silverware was lost, and insurance totaled $1,600. The $100 insurance discrepancy remains unresolved.[13][14]

    On December 27, 1903, fire spread from Tillman Hardware Company into Lewis's general-merchandise store. Lewis carried no insurance, but most of his stock was removed. A firewall and the town fire engine kept the whole block from burning.[15][16] In January 1911, burglars stole twenty-five pistols from his hardware store. It was the third pistol-only burglary there in two years.[17]

    Lewis sold the hardware business to Nathaniel Merrell on January 20, 1912. The newspaper said he was expected to enter real estate.[18] The 1920 and 1930 censuses later verify that occupation, though they do not identify a firm name, partners, acreage, or transactions.[7][8]

    Banking, manufacturing, and public service

    Lewis served on Quitman's city council in 1891. His 1893 marriage report independently described him as a former councilman.[1][10] In May 1903 he served as foreman of the Brooks Superior Court grand jury.[1] Huxford's table is a list of grand-jury foremen, not Masonic officers.

    At the Merchants and Farmers' Bank annual meeting in February 1896, the directors elected Lewis vice president.[19][20] Huxford later names him among the directors of the First National Bank of Quitman in 1905. That bank opened with $100,000 in capital and a reported $40,000 surplus.[1] Lewis's obituary summarizes this record by saying he had served as a director of two Quitman banks.[29]

    Atlantic & Gulf Mills stockholders reelected Lewis to the board in October 1907.[21] The same report names other men as president, vice president, and secretary-treasurer, so Lewis should be described as a director, not an officer.

    A Georgia Supreme Court calendar in October 1907 lists W. C. McCall v. J. O. Lewis from Brooks County as submitted.[24] The notice provides no facts or outcome. No opinion or court file located online could safely expand the entry.

    Lewis's obituary describes him as a retired business and civic leader and says he had been an elder of the Quitman Presbyterian Church for more than thirty years.[26][29] In 1925 he served on Quitman's advisory committee for the campaign to move Columbia Theological Seminary to Georgia.[25] A 1913 Atlanta fundraising list also records a $100 subscription from J. O. Lewis of Quitman in the context of the Oglethorpe University campaign.[23]

    Rosa Lena Lewis had a separate public record. She served in Presbyterian women's organizations, the Quitman United Daughters of the Confederacy, Belgian relief work, and the Southwest Georgia Presbyterial Auxiliary.[30][31][32] These were her activities and are not assigned to her husband.

    South Georgia Railroad

    Huxford names J. O. Lewis among twenty-six petitioners for the South Georgia Railroad charter in 1896. The proposed capital was $65,000 in 650 shares.[1] Huxford distinguishes the petitioner group from the nine first directors and four first officers. Lewis was not named in either smaller group.

    The identity of the petitioner as John Owen Lewis is strong probable. At the time, he was an adult Quitman merchant, city-council veteran, real-estate-company director, and bank vice president. The same man later joined a published railway petition. No competing Brooks County businessman with the initials J. O. Lewis emerged from the census, newspaper, or local-history search.

    The initials-only charter list leaves one evidentiary gap. The original petition, signature page, subscription book, stock ledger, and corporate minutes were not found online. No source located for this dossier gives Lewis's South Georgia Railroad share count, investment, land contribution, or a later company office.

    Quitman, Valdosta and Thomasville railway proposal

    Lewis's later railway activity is directly documented. A published 1907 petition names J. O. Lewis among the applicants for the Quitman, Valdosta and Thomasville Electric Railway and Power Company. The proposed street railway was to run about fifty miles from Valdosta through Quitman to Thomasville, cross the South Georgia Railroad at Quitman, and begin with $30,000 in capital stock.[22]

    The petition appeared repeatedly during the statutory notice period. It verifies Lewis's applicant role, but it gives no individual subscription or office and does not show that the line was built. This was a separate proposed corporation and cannot be used to assign Lewis a later office in the South Georgia Railroad.

    Death and burial

    Lewis died at his Quitman home at 2:30 p.m. on October 17, 1939. His death certificate gives carcinoma of the prostate with metastasis to the liver as the cause and says he had been ill for several years. His son Virgil supplied the personal information.[2] A contemporary obituary says Lewis was seventy-five, had lived in Quitman for more than forty years, and had retired from active business about ten or twelve years earlier.[26]

    Funeral services were held at the Presbyterian church on October 18. The death certificate records burial at Quitman that day.[2][26] A cemetery memorial places him in Oak Hill Cemetery.[27] The cemetery identification is strong probable because the memorial and family links agree with the primary record, but the marker inscription was not directly transcribed during this search.

    Rosa Lena survived him. Her cemetery memorial gives February 17, 1868-July 19, 1963.[33] Her identity as Lewis's wife is verified by the marriage report, censuses, death certificate, and obituary. Her exact dates remain strong probable pending inspection of a marker or later Georgia death record.

    Evidence assessment

    1. The Quitman merchant is verified as John Owen Lewis, born May 10, 1864, and deceased October 17, 1939. The original death certificate and continuous census chain control the identification.
    2. His parents, wife, five children, Virginia childhood, Quitman household, occupations, and death are verified. Oak Hill Cemetery is strong probable because the marker itself was not read directly.
    3. His grocery, hardware, real-estate, bank, mill, council, grand-jury, and church roles are supported by contemporary or linked local records.
    4. His identification as the 1896 South Georgia Railroad petitioner is strong probable. Huxford's list uses initials only, and the original petition remains missing.
    5. Lewis was not among the South Georgia Railroad's first directors or officers. No share count or later company position was found.
    6. His role as a 1907 applicant for the Quitman, Valdosta and Thomasville Electric Railway and Power Company is verified from the published petition.
    7. The 1907 court case, exact South Georgia Railroad investment, real-estate transactions, estate inventory, and probate disposition remain unresolved.

    Open questions and limits

    • The original 1896 South Georgia Railroad petition, signatures, subscription book, stock ledger, and corporate minutes were not located online.
    • No source found online gives Lewis's South Georgia Railroad share count or establishes later service as a company director or officer.
    • The facts and outcome of W. C. McCall v. J. O. Lewis remain unknown.
    • No complete deed chain, real-estate ledger, store inventory, business partnership file, or estate inventory was found.
    • A matching original Brooks County civil marriage entry did not appear in the inspected online collection. The contemporary church report supplies the wedding date and place.
    • No matching Brooks County probate file was found in the inspected Ancestry collection. A Stewart County John O. Lewis probate entry belongs to an unrelated family.
    • Quitman coverage in the national city-directory collection was inadequate.
    • The Oak Hill marker and Rosa Lena's marker were not directly transcribed, and Rosa Lena's exact death date was not checked against a civil death record.
