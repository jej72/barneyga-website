    John William Hitch

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    John William Hitch of Morven, the eldest son of Dr. Robert Marcus Hitch, was a farmer, merchant, postmaster, Brooks County commissioner, and South Georgia Railroad director. The identification of him as commissioner is strong probable, while the railroad directorship is verified. The name "James W. Hitch" survives in two passages of Folks Huxford's 1948 county history, but the strongest available evidence contradicts the existence of a second Brooks County public figure by that name. Huxford's own index assigns the commissioner passage to John, and his errata expressly changes the director's first name from James to John.[1]

    This dossier keeps Huxford's James wording where the source actually prints it, but it does not turn that wording into a second man's biography. The evidence and its limits belong here, in John William Hitch's dossier.

    The commissioner identity

    Huxford's county-government roster prints "James W. Hitch" as a Brooks County commissioner from 1893 through 1900. A contemporary report in the Atlanta Constitution confirms that "J. W. Hitch" sat on the five-member board in December 1894 with S. S. Rountree, Robert L. Groover, J. N. McLean, and Mitchell Brice. The report establishes the office and initials, but it does not expand the first name.[1-2]

    The board's December 26, 1894 meeting was a direct response to the violence surrounding Joseph Isom's killing six days earlier. After Isom was shot, a white posse searching for Waverly Pike killed three Black men and abused other Black residents. The commissioners condemned the murders and abuse and called for offenders to be brought to trial. The board itself had existed since 1881; it was this meeting and resolution, not the board's creation, that followed the Isom crisis.[1-2,30]

    This episode belongs to the broader history of racial violence in Brooks County. In this dossier, it also supplies independent evidence that J. W. Hitch was then serving as commissioner. The articles report institutional action and do not record Hitch's personal words or vote.[2,21]

    Huxford's index resolves the book's internal conflict. It has no entry for James W. Hitch. Its entry for "John W." directs readers to pages 198, 255, 375, 478, and 585. Page 375 is the roster that prints the commissioner as James. The other references concern John William Hitch's railroad role, postmaster service, family sketch, and the errata. The index therefore shows that Huxford regarded the commissioner as John even though the roster retained the wrong first name.[1]

    John William Hitch is the only matching adult found in the local record. He lived in the Morven district, held the Morven post office, operated businesses there, farmed, and served on the South Georgia Railroad board during the same period. No census household, death record, obituary, cemetery record, family entry, or contemporary Brooks County article was found for a separate adult James W. Hitch who could have held the office.[1-15]

    His parentage supplies another fixed point in the identification. Huxford names John as the eldest son of Morven physician Dr. Robert Marcus Hitch. The 1870 census places eight-year-old William in Robert and Martha Hitch's Brooks County household, and the 1880 census records sixteen-year-old J. W. Hitch as a son in R. M. Hitch's Morven household. The Morven references to J. W. Hitch therefore fit a documented local son of Dr. Hitch, while no comparable Brooks County record chain was found for a James.[1,3-4]

    The sound conclusion is that J. W. Hitch's service as commissioner is verified and his identification as John William Hitch is strong probable. The claim that a separate James served is contradicted.

    Birth and family

    John William Hitch was the eldest son of Dr. Robert Marcus Hitch and Martha Serena Fall Hitch. Huxford gives his birth as November 16, 1862. The 1870 census lists eight-year-old William Hitch with Robert and Martha in Brooks County. In 1880, sixteen-year-old J. W. Hitch was in the household of R. M. Hitch in the Morven district.[1,3-4]

    The birth year is not fully settled. Huxford and the 1900 census support 1862, while the derivative Find a Grave record gives 1863. No original birth record or cemetery-marker image was inspected. November 16, 1862 is therefore strong probable rather than verified.[1,5,15]

    John's siblings help confirm the identity chain. A 1901 report of B. F. Hitch's death at his brother J. W. Hitch's Morven home also names brothers R. M. and C. M. Hitch. John's 1935 obituary names the same family circle, including R. M. Hitch, C. M. Hitch, L. C. Chapman, and T. A. Rozier.[14,16]

    Marriage and children

    An authenticated indexed marriage record identifies "J W Hitch" and "Simmie B Rountree" as married in Lowndes County on December 7, 1893. That direct date corrects Huxford's 1892 date and the 1900 census's implied 1894 date.[6]

    The 1900 household in Brooks County contained John, wife Simmie, son William, and daughters Marie and Jewel. The record is misindexed as John Fitch, but the manuscript details and family members identify the household. By 1910, the family was in Quitman with Marie, Jewel, and Virginia. That index reverses his name to William J. Hitch, yet the wife and children preserve the chain.[5,7]

    Morven postmaster, merchant, and farmer

    Huxford's transcription of federal appointments says John W. Hitch became Morven postmaster on May 3, 1895. An Atlanta Journal appointment notice from that day identifies "J. W. Hitch" as the successor to J. J. Scruggs at Morven. Enoch Hall Pike received the next appointment on February 27, 1899.[1,8]

    By 1899, newspaper descriptions made J. W. Hitch one of Morven's most visible business figures. A Valdosta Times county survey credited his work and the new railroad with the town's growth. It listed Hitch & Edmondson as operating two general stores and described Hitch as a successful hay farmer. Another report said he sold thousands of bales of pea-vine hay at twelve dollars per ton.[9-10]

    The Atlanta Constitution reported that Hitch planted 150 acres in pea-vine hay and called him a progressive farmer. A broader Valdosta Times profile described him as one of Brooks County's best farmers, engaged in extensive mercantile business and connected with ownership of the South Georgia Railroad. Later items noted town-lot sales and a chicken-farm venture.[11-13]

    These reports generally use initials rather than a full first name. Their Morven setting, dates, post-office appointment, Huxford index, family record, and railroad continuity make John William Hitch the strong-probable subject. No competing local James identity was found.

    South Georgia Railroad

    The railroad record contains three distinct roles that should not be collapsed.

    First, Huxford says that a January 29, 1896 meeting appointed Mitchell Brice, Fleming Bates Walker, S. M. Young, Dr. T. W. Hunter, and "James W. Hitch" to solicit subscriptions throughout Brooks County. Huxford's index does not cite this page, and his errata does not correct it. The original Quitman Free Press issue behind the passage was not located online. John is the strong-probable committee member because he is the only documented local J. W. Hitch and was then commissioner, merchant, postmaster, and soon a railroad organizer. The first name on this committee cannot be called verified until the original report, commissioner minutes, or a subscription ledger appears.[1]

    Second, Huxford's transcribed charter list reads "J. V. Hitch". The locality and immediate continuity with the first board support "J. W. Hitch" as the strong-probable identity, but the preserved text does not verify that reading.[1]

    Third, the founding director is firmly identified. Contemporary reports of the March 23, 1896 stockholders' meeting name "J. W. Hitch" as a director. Huxford's errata expressly changes the page 198 director from "James W. Hitch" to "John W. Hitch". Later reports show J. W. Hitch re-elected to the board in 1899. In February 1900, he sold his railroad stock and Henry G. Turner replaced him as a director.[1,17-21]

    The Savannah Morning News also reported in April 1896 that J. W. Hitch & Co. of Morven received a contract to furnish crossties. The report verifies a company contract, but it does not state Hitch's individual profit, acreage, or subscription amount.[29]

    The verified railroad biography belongs to John William Hitch. The page 197 subscription-committee wording remains a documented but unresolved first-name problem.

    Later life in Georgia and Florida

    The 1910 census placed John in Quitman and described him as a mechanic at a lumber mill. By 1920, John and Simmie were living at Bronson in Levy County, Florida, with daughters Jewel and Virginia. In 1930, J. W. Hitch was a truck farmer at Canal Point in Palm Beach County. Simmie's mother, Nancy M. Rountree, lived with them.[7,22-23]

    Florida's death index records John William Hitch's death in Palm Beach County in 1935. The Palm Beach Post reported his death at Canal Point on February 7, 1935 and connected him with relatives in Quitman and Savannah. Find a Grave places him in Woodlawn Cemetery at West Palm Beach. The cemetery claim is derivative because the marker image and cemetery register were not inspected.[15-16,24]

    The excluded Rev. James W. Hitch

    Rev. James W. Hitch was a different man. Contemporary reports identify him as a Waycross member of Simon W. Hitch's family, an Emory student in 1900, a lawyer before Methodist mission service, and the husband of Reubie Lilley, whom he married in Seoul on September 1, 1909.[25-27]

    The student and missionary belonged to the Simon W. Hitch family in Waycross, while the Brooks County record chain identifies John as a son of Robert Marcus Hitch, already married and farming in Brooks County by 1900. The two men are distinct. Quitman references in missionary articles arise from church and family connections, not from the Brooks County office.

    Map, property, and record limits

    The 1908 R. D. Flippen map of Brooks County was inspected at full resolution and in a Morven-area crop. No Hitch label was found. The map does not establish a Hitch residence or parcel, but its selective labeling also means the absence cannot disprove ownership.[28]

    No online image was located for the Brooks County commissioner minutes, appointment papers, oath or bond, relevant deeds, tax digests, probate file, South Georgia Railroad subscription ledger, or the January 1896 Quitman Free Press report. These are gaps in the accessible record, not negative findings.
