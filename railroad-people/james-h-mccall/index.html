    Dr. James H. McCall

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    Identity confidence: Verified.

    Dr. James H. McCall was a physician, druggist, merchant, farmer, banker, school official, and civic investor in Quitman, Georgia. Huxford names him among the twenty-six men in the 1896 petition to charter the South Georgia Railroad. That role is strong probable because the original petition was not located. He was not the railroad director identified in contemporary reports as J. G. McCall. That man was his brother, John Goldwire McCall.[1-3]

    McCall's public career stretched from the first years after the Civil War until his death in 1909. He helped organize the Brooks County Medical Society, served on the county Board of Education for nearly three decades, held municipal office, led local investment companies, and became an early officer of two Quitman banks. His public work is well documented, but several personal details remain unsettled, including the conflict over his birth year and the identity of his medical school.[1-8]

    Birth, parents, and settlement near Quitman

    McCall was probably born in Screven County, Georgia, on October 19, 1837. The 1900 census gives October 1837, and his Oak Hill Cemetery memorial gives October 19, 1837. His contemporary obituary instead says 1838. The month and 1837 year are supported by two sources, but the exact day remains derivative until the marker image is directly inspected.[4-5][17]

    His parents were Francis Stephen McCall and Ann Dopson McCall. The 1850 Lowndes County census places thirteen-year-old James in their household, where the index reads his middle initial as J. Huxford's compiled McCall account identifies James H. as one of their thirteen children and John Goldwire McCall as one of his brothers. The obituary says James came with his father at about age eight to the McCarty farm south of the future Quitman. Huxford dates the family's move into the area to about 1845. Brooks County was not created until 1858, and Quitman was not yet the county seat when the family arrived.[1][4][17]

    The household chain continues after the war. In 1870 James was a thirty-one-year-old apothecary in Quitman with wife Eddie and their oldest child. In 1880 J. H. McCall was a druggist on Screen Street with Eddie C. and three children. The 1900 census placed James H., Eddie C., and son James H. in the Nankin district; the elder James was recorded as a farmer who owned his home free of mortgage. His 1860 location remains unresolved.[17]

    Huxford describes Francis McCall as a successful farmer who also operated a grist mill and blacksmith shop. The family belonged to Concord Baptist Church. These details come from a family-history section that Huxford says was supplied by families and sometimes arranged by the compiler. They are useful, but they do not carry the same weight as a contemporary record.[1]

    Medical training and Confederate service

    McCall studied medicine after reaching adulthood and practiced before the Civil War. His 1909 obituary says he was a graduate, but the surviving OCR damages the institution's name. No accessible medical catalogue, diploma, register, or licensing record was located that safely identifies his school or graduation date.[4]

    The same obituary says he joined a Florida regiment and served during the Civil War. A compiled service profile for J. H. McCall, a twenty-four-year-old Brooks County resident, records enlistment on March 1, 1862, as a private in Company I, 2nd Florida Cavalry, discharge on December 23, 1862, and an allegiance oath at Baldwin, Florida, on May 17, 1865. The residence, age, initials, and Florida connection make the match strong probable. It remains short of verified until the original compiled-service-record images are checked. The obituary's statement that he served "through the war" should not be read as continuous service in that single unit.[4][20]

    Marriage and children

    McCall married Edna Caldwell Brooks in 1865 or 1866. His obituary calls her Eddie Brooks of Ninety Six, South Carolina, and dates the marriage to 1865. Huxford calls her Edna Caldwell Brooks of Edgefield, South Carolina. The 1900 census reports thirty-four years of marriage, pointing to 1866. The family structure agrees closely enough to show that Edna and Eddie are forms of the same woman's name, but no original marriage return was located.[1][4][6][17]

    They had four children:

    • Tillie McCall, who married Dr. Samuel S. Gaulden;
    • Constance McCall, who married James Russell Davis;
    • Brooks B. McCall, later associated with Madison, Florida, who married Henri Lipscomb; and
    • James H. McCall Jr.[1][4]

    The children named in the obituary match Huxford's family account. Huxford expands the son's name as Brooks Barnes, while his 1899 wedding report and cemetery memorial use Brooks Butler. Brooks B. is the safest form unless an original birth record settles the middle name. The agreement on the four-child set helps distinguish the physician from unrelated people with the same initials.[1][4][17][21]

    Medicine, the drugstore, and commerce

    After the war, McCall entered the drug business in Quitman. His obituary says he first worked with a Mr. Giles and later in McCall & Groover. Huxford dates McCall's store to 1866 and says Clayton Groover joined him about 1870. Huxford also places their drugstore at the corner where the Citizens Bank building later stood.[1][4]

    McCall was present when Brooks County physicians organized the Brooks County Medical Society in January 1868, and the group elected him secretary. The society adopted a common fee schedule and a shared book identifying delinquent accounts. That policy belonged to the society as a body. The record does not show that McCall personally proposed it.[1]

    In August 1886 McCall sold his drug stock and store to John S. Harris and Dr. T. M. Mathews, then entered the dry-goods business with C. M. and T. J. Powers. The accessible sources do not state when he stopped regularly practicing medicine or how long the Powers association lasted.[4][21]

    The honorific Dr. continued to identify him in civic and business reporting. A 1904 report named Dr. J. H. McCall of Quitman as Brooks County's representative to an interstate sugar-cane growers' convention. A 1903 Florida notice also recorded Dr. J. H. McCall of Quitman among prominent visitors to Perry.[9-10]

    Agriculture

    McCall signed the March 1869 call to organize the Brooks County Agricultural Society. The 1900 census called him a farmer. An 1891 report described him as one of Quitman's largest cotton buyers and planters. A 1897 Quitman report credited him with killing ninety-eight hogs averaging about 204 pounds and having about as many left. His agricultural work is well supported, but the exact size and location of his landholdings remain unresolved.[1][7][17][21]

    The evidence reviewed for this dossier does not establish whether McCall personally enslaved people before the Civil War. His father was described as a planter, and the broader McCall family account discusses enslaved labor in connection with other family property. Those facts cannot be transferred to James without a slave schedule, deed, probate record, or another direct source.

    Libraries, schools, and municipal office

    McCall was vice president of Quitman's first library association in 1877. He was also a board member when the Brooks County Library Association organized on January 31, 1880.[1]

    In May 1880 he was appointed to the Brooks County Board of Education. Huxford's table gives his term as 1880 through 1909 and notes that he died. The notation describes a member appointed in 1880 who remained on the board until his death. Jeff Davis replaced him in May 1909.[1]

    McCall's school work also included a land transaction. In September 1895 he sold the Quitman school board two acres in Town Lot 32 for $750 as the site of a new school. The surviving summary does not substitute for the original deed, which was not located.[1]

    Quitman elected McCall as an alderman in 1882 and again in 1883. The records checked do not establish a term as mayor or a countywide elective office.[1]

    Banking and civic investment

    McCall took part in several of Quitman's late nineteenth-century business ventures. Huxford identifies him as a first director and first president of the Quitman Real Estate and Investment Company, organized in 1889 with reported capital of $25,000. A June 1890 newspaper report names him as treasurer and director when the company reported its first-year results. The titles may describe different dates and should not be collapsed into a single office. He was also a charter member of the Quitman Hotel Company, the organization associated with the Hotel Marie project.[1][21]

    His banking career involved two institutions and requires a year-by-year reading. A January 1889 report identifies him as vice president of the Bank of Quitman. When Merchants & Farmers Bank opened in 1891, two contemporary reports named McCall as its first president. Huxford lists him as president again in 1893 and 1897 and as vice president in 1895. Newspapers confirm his reelection as president in 1896. In February 1900 he declined another term as president but remained a director.[1][11-13][21]

    His obituary says he remained president from about 1890 until Merchants & Farmers became First National Bank in 1905, after which he continued as a stockholder and director. Huxford's dated officer lists and the 1900 resignation report show that his title varied during that period, so the obituary is best read as a broad summary rather than an exact annual roster.[1][4][21]

    Florida newspaper reports connect a Dr. J. H. McCall of Georgia with banking interests in Perry in 1905 and describe him as interested in numerous banks and two railroads. A 1903 Perry notice explicitly says the visitor was from Quitman, making the identification plausible. The 1905 articles, however, omit Quitman in some versions and contain broad promotional claims. They are best treated as probable references to this McCall, not as proof of every stated investment.[10][14]

    Political and community activity

    McCall served on a committee at a Brooks County Democratic mass meeting in 1870. The appointment places him in the county's Democratic political organization during Reconstruction. It does not by itself prove a later party office or a continuous political leadership role.[1]

    In May 1890, a newspaper reported that McCall and R. K. McCord tried to mediate a violent dispute involving the Badger and Brooks parties near Quitman. The attempted settlement failed. The report documents an effort at mediation, but it does not establish that McCall held an official law-enforcement or judicial role. A targeted search found no identity-secure article tying him personally to racial violence. It would be misleading to assign him views or conduct from unrelated articles about the era.[15][21]

    South Georgia Railroad

    Huxford prints James H. McCall's full name among the twenty-six men named in the February 1896 petition for a charter for the South Georgia Railroad. This supports a strong-probable identification as a charter incorporator, pending the original petition.[1]

    His documented railroad role stops there. He does not appear on the January subscription committee, the first board of directors, or the first slate of officers. Contemporary reporting names J. G. McCall as a founding director, and Huxford identifies that man as James's brother John Goldwire McCall.[1-3]

    The charter petition does not prove that James purchased stock. No share certificate, stock ledger, subscription amount, corporate office, right-of-way assignment, construction role, or railroad employment record was located for him. Later Florida reporting that probably refers to McCall says he had interests in two railroads, but it does not name them and cannot establish a South Georgia Railroad investment.[14]

    Illness, death, and burial

    McCall's health declined before his death at his home in west Quitman in February 1909. A Valdosta Daily Times obituary reprinted from the Quitman Free Press says he died at midnight and was to be buried that afternoon in Quitman's "new cemetery." It names his wife and all four children, securely tying the notice to the physician, druggist, and banker described elsewhere.[4]

    The best-supported date is February 11, 1909. His Oak Hill Cemetery memorial gives that date, and a South Carolina notice published February 18 says he died "last Thursday," which also points to February 11. The Valdosta reprint appeared February 13 and says he died at midnight "last night," creating a February 11-12 ambiguity if read without the other evidence. A later American Medical Association directory gives February 15, but that date is contradicted by the nearer newspaper reports.[4-6][16]

    The obituary's period name "new cemetery" establishes burial in Quitman. Find a Grave memorial 86612063 identifies the site as Oak Hill Cemetery and matches the family and February 11 death date. Oak Hill is strong probable rather than fully verified because a cemetery register was not located and the marker image was not independently audited for this release.[4-5]

    Namesake controls

    Several men can be confused with Dr. McCall:

    • John Goldwire McCall was his brother and the South Georgia Railroad's founding director. His initials were J. G., not J. H.[1-3]
    • James H. McCall Jr. was his son. Records after the father's death require generational checking.[1][4]
    • A Brooks County census search result indexed a James H. McCall born about 1851. That age is incompatible with the physician who practiced before the Civil War and married by 1866. The result was not used without a household-level match.[17]
    • A Dr. J. H. McCall of Huntingdon, Tennessee, appeared in February 1909 reports after being robbed while returning from Havana. He was not the Quitman physician and has been excluded.[18-19]
    • Search hits in Nashville, Michigan, Oregon, Australia, and other places lacked the Quitman family and career chain and were excluded.

    Record conflicts and limits

    • Birth year: the 1900 census and cemetery memorial support October 1837; the contemporary obituary says 1838.
    • Death date: cemetery memorial and South Carolina notice support February 11, 1909; the midnight wording in a February 13 reprint is ambiguous, and a later medical directory says February 15.
    • Wife's name: Edna Caldwell Brooks and Eddie Brooks refer to the same spouse, but no original marriage record was found.
    • Medical education: graduation is reported, but the institution and date remain unresolved.
    • Military service: the Company I, 2nd Florida Cavalry match is strong probable, but original service images were not secured.
    • Census chain: 1850, 1870, 1880, and 1900 households are identity-secure; his 1860 location is unresolved.
    • Burial: Quitman is verified from the obituary; Oak Hill Cemetery is strong probable pending a register or fully audited marker image.
    • Property and estate: no complete deed chain, probate file, or inventory was located.
    • Railroad: incorporation is documented; stock ownership, board service, and an operating role are not.

    Research note

    This dossier was completed August 21, 2026. Research covered the existing South Georgia Railroad collection, Huxford's county history and index, Newspapers.com, Ancestry census, military, and cemetery results, Find a Grave, public web indexes, local family material, banking and business reports, death notices, and false-match controls. Twenty-five targeted newspaper queries covered name variants, medicine, drugstores, banking, real estate, the Hotel Marie, the railroad, politics, schools, agriculture, violence, illness, death, and family. The 1850, 1870, 1880, and 1900 census households were matched; the 1860 location remains unresolved. Accessible evidence was preserved in the project package. Important gaps and conflicts are stated above rather than filled from inference.
