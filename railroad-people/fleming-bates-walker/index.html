    Fleming Bates Walker

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    Fleming Bates Walker was a farmer, merchant, banker, school trustee, and Brooks County commissioner whose work helped shape Barney during the town's railroad era. He was born in Brooks County on November 17, 1859, according to his death certificate, and died at Little-Griffin Hospital in Valdosta on July 25, 1934. Census records disagree about the year, most notably the 1900 census, which gives November 1860. The certificate is the strongest direct record for the exact date, but the conflict remains.[1][2]

    Walker helped raise Brooks County's local share for the railroad from Adel to Quitman in 1896 and was named in the charter petition for the company that became the South Georgia Railroad. He was not listed among its first directors or officers. Later, he joined an effort to organize a separate Georgia, Alabama and Western Railroad, but no evidence found in this research shows that the proposed line was built.[3][4]

    His career reached well beyond railroads. Contemporary records document a general-merchandise business, large farming interests, leadership in commodity associations, a bank vice-presidency, two periods on the county commission, and years of work for Barney's school. The record also contains losses and public crises: two major fires, the Bank of Barney receivership, and the 1928 floods. These episodes show a working local businessman and public official, not simply a name in a railroad charter.

    Parents, childhood, and the three Fleming Walkers

    The Barney merchant was one of three generations with nearly the same name. His father, Fleming B. Walker, was born about 1818 or 1819 and was dead by May 1893. The subject lived from 1859 or 1860 to 1934. His son, also Fleming B. Walker, was born about 1903 and survived him. Records that use only initials, or add inconsistent suffixes such as Jr., Sr., or III, can easily merge the three men.[1][5][6][44]

    The 1870 census places ten-year-old Fleming with Fleming Walker, age fifty-one, Sarah Walker, age forty-seven, and eight younger household members near Quitman. The schedule did not state relationships. The 1880 census is more precise: nineteen-year-old F. B. Walker was the unmarried son of F. B. and Sarah Walker and worked on the family farm. It identifies six other children in the household as his siblings.[5][7]

    The 1934 death certificate names his parents as Fleming B. Walker and Sarah Jones. His daughter, Mrs. S. L. Strickland, supplied the information. An original will made by Joseph F. Walker establishes that the elder Fleming was a brother of Joseph F., John W., and Isham A. Walker. This makes the younger Fleming a first cousin of Deborah Kesiah Walker Conoly, whose father was Isham A.[1][8]

    One maternal-name conflict remains. In 1900 an eighty-one-year-old Eliza Walker, recorded as Fleming's mother, lived in his household. Earlier censuses call the elder Fleming's wife Sarah, and the death certificate calls the subject's mother Sarah Jones. Eliza may have been another name for Sarah, but the available records do not prove that. They also do not justify inventing a second wife or stepmother.[1][2][5][7]

    Marriage and children

    The Brooks County marriage register records a license for Fleming B. Walker and Susan A. Walker on April 27, 1895. They married two days later. Family oral history reported by James E. Jones says Walker had earlier married Alice Pace and that she died in a horse-and-buggy accident. No marriage record, death record, or contemporary accident report for Alice was located in the online research completed for this dossier. Her marriage to Walker and the reported manner of her death therefore remain attributed oral history rather than independently documented conclusions.[9][48]

    The 1900, 1910, and 1920 censuses document three children:

    • Coma Lee Walker, born about June 1896. She later married W. A. Maxwell and remained at Barney.
    • Susie B. Walker, born about December 1898. She became Mrs. S. L. Strickland and died in 1936.
    • Fleming B. Walker, born about 1903. He married Deborah Elizabeth Joyce in 1924 and later administered his father's estate.[2][6][10][11]

    The 1934 obituary names the same three surviving children. No fourth child appears in the census chain. This does not exclude a child who died between censuses, but no original record located in this research supports online family-tree claims for an additional daughter named Willie Mae.[12]

    The 1940 census provides a useful namesake check after Walker's death. His daughter Coma Maxwell's Barney household included an eighty-year-old widowed mother entered as Mrs. F. B. Walker. The original image shows that this was Walker's widow, Susan, using her married style. It was not another man named Fleming.[13]

    Farm, home, and work

    Walker described himself differently as his work changed. In 1900 he was a farmer who owned his farm free of mortgage. By 1910 he lived on Main Street in Barney and worked as a salesman in general merchandise. The 1920 census called him a merchant working on his own account and said he owned his home free of mortgage. In 1930 he lived on Church Street and was again listed as a general farmer, although the household was not located on a farm.[2][6][10][14]

    Newspapers fill out that outline. An 1899 Brooks County profile called F. B. Walker a successful farmer near Morven. Because his father was dead by 1893, this is a strong probable reference to the younger man. A 1909 report placed his farm one mile south of Barney. In February 1910 he bought the John Berryhill farm, within a mile of town, for a reported $9,000. The article said railroad tracks bordered the property on the east and west and called it one of South Georgia's best farms.[15][16][18][44]

    The 1908 Brooks County map shows that Walker's existing place and the later Berryhill purchase were separate properties. It labels "F. B. Walker Pl." in Land Lot 280. Directly west, it associates John Berryhill with the adjoining Land Lots 326 and 319. James E. Jones identified that western property as the Berryhill farm Walker bought in 1910, and the map and sale notice fit his reading. The map places the Berryhill residence marker in Lot 326 near Lot 319, but it does not draw the farm's legal boundaries. The exact acreage in each lot remains unproved.[16][47]

    Cotton reports repeatedly connect Walker to Barney's first bales. He bought a 510-pound first bale in 1906. In August 1909 Covington Graham sold another first bale to Walker, then identified as president of Barney Supply Company. In 1915 a first bale came from Walker's farm. These short items do not establish his total acreage or annual production, but they show his place in the local cotton market.[17][18]

    His farming expanded into livestock, cooperative marketing, and tobacco. Walker and Ingram Brothers displayed purebred hogs in a 1915 Brooks County farm-products pageant. He served on the temporary board organizing a South Georgia sweet-potato growers association in 1921 and on the Southwest Georgia Watermelon Growers Association's executive committee from 1922 through 1925. In 1928 Brooks County selected him as a master-farmer candidate. The next year newspapers reported that he had ninety acres in tobacco, expected more than 100,000 pounds, and had already sold more than 25,000 pounds.[19][20][21]

    In 1925 Walker joined a petition for the proposed Sowega Fertilizer Company after growers oversubscribed its planned minimum capital. The report proves the incorporation effort. It does not prove that the plant was built or that Walker became an officer.[22]

    Walker & Moody and other mercantile work

    Walker operated a store at Barney by 1906. In a signed political dispatch that February, he said he had polled fifteen men at his store and found all of them supporting Hoke Smith for governor. The note is modest, but it directly identifies the store and shows how it also served as a place for local political conversation.[23]

    Fire struck the Walker & Moody general-merchandise store in September 1906. The Macon Telegraph reported that Walker opened the store at about six in the morning and found fire burning through the floor. Smoke and heat kept him from entering. Another report estimated the damage at about $1,500, much of it caused by smoke and water. The building was saved.[24]

    Family oral history reported by James E. Jones identifies Walker's partner as his nephew James I. Moody. The contemporary record is consistent with that account: the 1910 census lists Walker as a general-merchandise salesman and Moody as a general-merchandise merchant. The newspaper accounts name the firm but not both partners, and the census does not state that the two men were partners. Moody's identification therefore rests on attributed family oral history rather than independent documentary proof.[6][24][46]

    Huxford's history later included F. B. Walker Sr. among Barney merchants. In this setting, Sr. distinguishes the subject from his adult son. It cannot refer to the subject's father, who was dead by May 1893.[3][44]

    Banking

    Walker first appears in a bank record at Adel. In September 1905 the Atlanta Journal reported that Citizens Bank had organized with $25,000 capital and named F. B. Walker among five directors. The bank planned a branch at Barney. This proves the directorship and the plan, but not that the branch opened or became the later Bank of Barney.[25]

    The Bank of Barney had recently opened by February 1906. A 1909 to 1910 Georgia business directory lists it as Barney Banking Company, with W. D. Manley as president, F. B. Walker as vice president, and deposits of $28,090. A 1926 newspaper also called Walker a banker and merchant. No located source fixes his ownership percentage or the exact dates of his bank service.[26][27][45]

    When the bank entered receivership during the wider collapse of W. D. Manley's banking interests, attorneys for depositors asked Judge W. E. Thomas to protect the assets. The Valdosta Daily Times reported on August 3, 1926, that Thomas appointed Walker temporary receiver. He was a local temporary receiver, not the later federal receiver over Manley's estate. The appointment does not show that Walker caused the bank's failure or owned the institution.[28]

    County government and local politics

    Walker's first documented local candidacy came in December 1903, when Barney Democrats nominated him for town council. The report proves the nomination, not the general-election result or a completed term.[29]

    His county service is clearer. Huxford's roster places Walker on the Brooks County Board of Commissioners in two periods, 1909 through 1917 and 1921 through 1929. Contemporary election reports support that outline:

    • In April 1908 the Atlanta Journal reported that Walker and J. R. Carter were elected commissioners.
    • In April 1912 Walker defeated E. E. or Eugene Hodges by five votes. The opponent's name varies between reports.
    • In March 1920 Walker defeated B. F. Scruggs in the Democratic primary. A December 1922 report called Walker and Carter holdover commissioners.
    • Roy Lawson defeated Walker in the February 1928 primary. Reports still called Walker a commissioner in May, consistent with his existing term continuing through the year.
    • Walker sought a return in the March 1932 primary, but no result was located. His 1934 obituaries called him a former commissioner.[3][30][31][32][33]

    Huxford's separate list of commission chairmen does not name Walker. The evidence supports service on the board, not a claim that he chaired it.[3]

    Walker had other political roles. A 1906 dispatch tied him to Hoke Smith's campaign, and in April 1908 the Brooks County Hoke Smith Club elected him vice president for the Barney district. An 1892 report may identify him as a delegate to the state Democratic convention, but initials alone make that earlier reference only possible.[23][34]

    School and civic work

    After Brooks County created local school districts in 1907, Huxford names Walker, John G. Scruggs, and J. B. Ingram as Barney's first trustees. Walker also served on the Barney district committee for the 1911 Brooks County Fair.[3][35]

    In 1926 the Valdosta Daily Times called him one of Barney's leading supporters during the community's effort to gain high-school accreditation. A 1929 notice said L. W. Graham received a commission as Barney district trustee, succeeding Walker. The records show sustained school work but do not supply the minutes, exact first appointment date, or every term.[27][36]

    The 1928 floods produced one of the clearest accounts of Walker's public work. He recruited volunteers to fill sandbags, and newspapers credited the group with saving the Rock Creek and Folsom highway bridges. The reported avoided loss was $20,000. A companion farm report quoted his belief that Brooks County farmers could recover from the season's flood damage.[37]

    Two railroad projects

    Walker's South Georgia Railroad role began with the Brooks County subscription campaign. Huxford says he served on the county-at-large committee formed on January 29, 1896, to raise the local share for the Adel-to-Quitman line. Five days later the committee reported the required money raised. Huxford also includes him among the twenty-six charter petitioners.[3]

    His name does not appear in Huxford's list of the first board or company officers. The careful conclusion is that Walker helped raise the local share and was a named incorporator. The available record does not establish how much he personally subscribed, whether or how long he held stock, or any later office in the South Georgia company. The original subscription report, charter petition, and stock ledger were not recovered.

    In 1911 Walker joined a separate railroad venture. He, E. H. Beck, T. A. Rozier, and seven other petitioners sought a charter for the Georgia, Alabama and Western Railroad. The proposed 150-mile line would run from Milltown through Hahira, Barney, Pavo, Meigs, Coolidge, Colquitt, and Blakely to the Alabama line. Proposed capital was $200,000, with authority to increase it to $3 million. Georgia granted the charter on January 25, 1912.[4][38]

    Later reports print B. F. Walker of Barney as vice president. The initials are probably transposed because the charter petition consistently names F. B. Walker, but the printed form cannot simply be corrected. His vice-presidency is therefore strong probable. No located evidence shows that the company built or operated the proposed railroad.[38]

    Fires and public crises

    The 1906 Walker & Moody store fire was not Walker's only large loss. In November 1923 an uninsured barn on his farm about two miles south of Barney burned. Reports estimated the loss at $2,000 and listed 1,000 bushels of corn, 1,000 bundles of fodder, five tons of hay, implements, harness, and two hogs. Sparks from a sugar furnace were reported as the probable cause.[39]

    The papers credited Dink Hendry, a Black farm worker, with saving eleven mules by tearing open the rear of the barn and driving them out. His action is part of the documented event and should not disappear from an account centered on the property owner.[39]

    This fire was separate from both the 1906 store fire and a 1922 blaze that destroyed two F. M. Blease stores beside the Bank of Barney. The 1922 reports say the bank suffered slight damage. They do not tie the burned stores to Walker.[24][40]

    The 1901 inquest

    In May 1901 the Valdosta Times reported that six men, including F. B. Walker, served on an inquest jury after the body of a Black man identified in the reporting as Dave or Eli Dodson was recovered from Little River near the South Georgia Railroad bridge north of Barney. Initials and location make the younger Walker a strong probable identification. The report places him in the official response after a reported lynching. It does not connect him to the killing.[41]

    Targeted newspaper searches found no criminal charge or civil case against this Fleming Walker. Visible legal hits concerned public office, the bank receivership, the inquest, namesakes, or unrelated men. That negative search is not proof that no other court record exists.

    Death, burial, and estate

    Walker entered Little-Griffin Hospital in Valdosta and died there at 5:45 p.m. on July 25, 1934. His death certificate gives his age as seventy-four years and eight months, residence as Barney, occupation as farmer, and burial at Corinth Cemetery near Morven on July 26.[1]

    The handwritten principal cause reads acute septic cholecystitis. Myocarditis is listed as a contributory cause. The original image was independently read during this research because the indexed transcription did not reliably capture the medical wording.[1]

    Four newspapers reported his death after several weeks of illness. The fullest notice named his widow and three children and scheduled the funeral at the family home in Barney. Some reports used a Quitman dateline or said he died there. The original certificate controls the place of death and resolves that conflict in favor of Valdosta. The obituary's wording about interment at Barney is less precise than the certificate's identification of Corinth Cemetery.[1][12]

    On September 3, 1934, his son, Fleming B. Walker Jr., became administrator of the estate under a $10,000 Brooks County bond. The record is an administrator's bond, not a will, inventory, account, or final settlement. No complete probate packet was located.[42]

    The Find a Grave index calls the subject Fleming Bates Walker Jr. and gives January 17, 1860, as his birth date. Both points conflict with the death certificate and the administrator's bond. Direct access to the memorial was blocked, so its marker photograph could not be inspected. The memorial is useful as a cemetery locator, but it does not settle the identity or birth-date conflicts.[1][42][43]

    What remains unresolved

    The accessible online record supports Walker's parents, documented 1895 marriage, children, household history, farming, merchant and banking work, county service, school work, railroad-organizing roles, death, burial, and estate administrator. Family oral history adds an earlier marriage to Alice Pace and reports her accidental death, but no documentary record for either claim was located.[48]

    No reliable 1860 census entry was found, even though the death certificate would place him alive at enumeration. No deed chain, exact acreage schedule, railroad stock ledger, commissioner minutes, election returns, school-board minutes, bank receivership file, full probate packet, or readable cemetery-marker image was recovered. The 1896 railroad committee and charter details still depend on Huxford's precise but retrospective account. The original documents behind that account remain an archival target.

    The birth date is not fully settled. November 17, 1859, comes from the death certificate; November 1860 comes from the 1900 census; other census ages imply 1860 or 1861; and the Find a Grave index gives January 17, 1860. The maternal name Eliza in 1900 also remains unreconciled with Sarah Jones in the stronger parent chain.

    Those gaps do not erase the larger picture. Fleming Bates Walker spent more than three decades helping turn Barney into a working farm, market, banking, school, road, and railroad community. His verified railroad role was limited but real. His fuller importance lies in the combination of private business and recurring public work that connected the town to the rest of Brooks County.
