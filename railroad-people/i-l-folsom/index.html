    Israel Lee Folsom

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    Israel Lee Folsom (April 11, 1860-February 4, 1926) was a Brooks County farmer and plantation operator whose adult life became closely tied to Barney. He appears in the local record as a cotton producer, a promoter of two railroad ventures, and one of the community's substantial landholders. His full name and life dates are verified through a continuous census and family record, his original marriage entry, a contemporary obituary, an original probate bond, and the inscription on his grave marker.[2][3][4][5][6][7][8][9][23][24][25]

    Huxford lists “I. L. Folsom” among the twenty-six men who petitioned in 1896 for the charter of the South Georgia Railroad.[1] The petitioner was strongly probably Israel Lee Folsom. The identification is supported by his age, Brooks County residence, farming interests, later Barney residence, and later service as a director of another proposed railroad. It is not graded verified because the known charter list gives only initials, and the original petition or signature page was not found online.

    Identity and parents

    Folsom was born in Brooks County on April 11, 1860. The federal census taken that year records Israel Folsom, four months old, in the household of E. J. and Priscilla J. Folsom near the Tallokas post office. The schedule did not state family relationships, but the household, age, county, and later records establish a strong continuity.[2]

    In 1870, “Isreal” Folsom, age ten, lived in Quitman with Jane Folsom and the same family group.[3] The 1880 census makes the relationship explicit. Israel L. Folsom, age twenty, was recorded as the son of P. J. Folsom in Quitman. He was single and working as a farm laborer.[4]

    These records verify Priscilla Jane Folsom as his mother. Cemetery and family-history sources expand his parents' names to Ebenezer George Folsom and Priscilla Jane Rogers Folsom.[26] The father's full name is strong probable rather than verified in this dossier. The 1860 census index reads E. J., while the expanded name Ebenezer George comes from derivative cemetery material. No original parent-naming record inspected for Israel resolves that initial conflict.

    The grave marker supplies the middle name that the census and railroad sources leave abbreviated. It reads “Israel Lee Folsom” and gives April 11, 1860, and February 4, 1926.[25] The photographed inscription verifies the name and dates. Family links attached to the cemetery memorial remain derivative unless independently supported.

    Marriage and children

    Israel L. Folsom married Ann C. Walker in Brooks County on December 11, 1884. The date and names appear in the original county marriage book.[5] Later census schedules identify her variously as Ann, Ann C., and Annie. “Civility,” the middle name used by cemetery sources, was not found in an original record inspected for this dossier, so she is called Ann C. Walker here.

    The 1900 census records Israel as a farmer who owned a mortgaged farm. It gives April 1860 as his birth month and year and says he and Ann had been married fifteen years. Their household included Turner, Fleming Bates, Jane, Benjamin, and Cora.[6] Ann's fertility columns were recorded as six children born and six living. That conflicts with the contemporary report of a daughter's death in 1895, and the household itself lists only five children. The 1900 fertility count cannot be accepted as exact.

    By 1910 the family was living on First Avenue in Barney. Ancestry's primary index calls the head H. L. Folsom, but the original schedule reads I. L. Folsom. He was a farmer and employer. Ann C. reported that they had been married twenty-five years and had nine children, eight of whom were living. The household listed Turner W., Fleming B., Sarah J., Benjamin, Cora L., Manor B., A. J., and I. L. Jr.[7] The marriage duration was rounded. The original marriage entry fixes the wedding date in December 1884.

    The eight children accounted for in the census sequence were Turner Walker, Fleming Bates, Sarah Jane, Benjamin Franklin, Cora L., Manor B., Allen James, and Israel L. Jr. Contemporary newspaper reports identify the missing child as a daughter, although they do not give her name or age. On the morning of July 21, 1895, Israel walked out to inspect one of his farms a few miles east of Quitman. One of his little girls tried to follow him. He told her to remain at a tenant's house, but she continued after him. When Israel returned and learned that she had gone on, he searched and found her drowned in a nearby branch. The report supposed that she had fallen from a log while trying to cross.[29]

    The 1910 census later reported nine children born and eight living, while the census sequence accounts for the other eight children. Despite the faulty 1900 count, the 1910 total and the contemporary death report strongly identify the girl who drowned in 1895 as the ninth child. A derivative cemetery memorial calls her “Unknown daughter Folsom,” gives the same death date, and assigns her to Camp Ground Cemetery, but it displays no marker photograph or original burial record.[30] Her name, exact age, and burial place therefore remain unverified, and no separate funeral notice was found.

    In 1920, I. L. Folsom was still farming at Barney. He owned his farm free of mortgage and worked as its manager on his own account. Annie and sons Ben, Manor, A. J., and I. L. Jr. remained in the household.[8]

    Turner Walker Folsom's original Georgia death certificate later named his parents as I. L. Folsom and Annie Walker.[9] That certificate verifies the parent-child relationship. It belongs to Turner, not Israel, and is not evidence of Israel's own cause of death.

    Farming and property

    Folsom's first substantial newspaper appearance came in March 1896, the same month the South Georgia Railroad organized. Fire destroyed his large barn near the Brooks-Lowndes county line. One account called him an energetic and prosperous young farmer. Reports listed about 700 bushels of corn, 3,000 bundles of fodder, hay, ground peas, mules, a horse, a cow, and hogs among the losses. The gate had apparently been saturated with oil, and arson was suspected. The loss was uninsured and estimated at several thousand dollars.[10][11]

    The Barney cotton reports show the scale of his later farming. In August 1906, I. L. Folsom, printed once as Folsome, brought Barney's first bale of new cotton to market. It weighed 510 pounds.[13][14] Two years later, eight bales were ginned at Barney in one day, six of them belonging to Folsom.[16] These reports show that he was a significant producer. They do not establish that he owned the gin.

    Another fire struck property at Barney in April 1908. A brief report said three houses associated with Folsom, E. Jones, and R. P. Pinkey were destroyed, and that Folsom's loss was partly insured.[15] The compressed account appears to assign one house to each man. It does not support a claim that Folsom owned all three.

    In January 1917, Israel Folsom of Barney attended a Sea Island cotton growers' convention in Valdosta. Growers were deciding whether long-staple cotton remained viable as the boll weevil spread through the region.[20] His attendance places him among the commercial planters dealing directly with the crop crisis.

    The clearest description of his agricultural scale appeared in 1923. The Americus Times-Recorder said he operated several large plantations, was held in high esteem, and was guarding about 200 bales of cotton at his Barney home amid threats connected with the prosecution of his son's killer.[22] The article verifies substantial plantation operations and a large cotton holding at that moment. It does not supply acreage, deeds, tenant lists, or an estate valuation.

    South Georgia Railroad

    Huxford's county history lists I. L. Folsom among twenty-six petitioners for the South Georgia Railroad charter in 1896. The proposed capital was $65,000 in 650 shares.[1] Huxford separates the petitioners from the nine first directors and four officers. Folsom appears only in the larger petitioner list.

    A contemporary report of the March 1896 organization meeting confirms the first directors and officers but does not reproduce all twenty-six petitioners.[12] Folsom was not on that first board and held no listed office. No source found online gives his subscription amount, share count, right-of-way contribution, or later participation in the company.

    The identification of the petitioner as Israel Lee Folsom is strong probable. The case rests on a specific combination: an adult farmer named Israel Folsom was prominent in Brooks County at the time of the petition; the census chain places Israel L. continuously in the county; later schedules place I. L. at Barney; and contemporary reports show the Barney man promoting another railroad. No competing Brooks County adult with the same initials and profile emerged from the search. Still, the initials-only charter list leaves a small evidentiary gap that should not be hidden.

    Georgia, Alabama and Western Railway

    Folsom's later railway role is directly documented. In June 1912, several newspapers named I. L. Folsom of Barney as a director of the proposed Georgia, Alabama and Western Railway Company. Its planned route ran through Hahira, Barney, Pavo, Meigs, Colquitt, and Blakely toward Dothan, with an intended eastward extension toward Brunswick. The directors authorized surveys and cost estimates.[17][18][19]

    This directorship is verified as printed and shows that Folsom remained interested in transportation development sixteen years after the South Georgia Railroad petition. The reports describe the company's officers and directors as prominent business men and expected stockholders, but they do not state Folsom's investment. The 1912 project was a separate corporation. It cannot be used to turn his 1896 petitioner role into an unrecorded South Georgia Railroad board seat or office.

    Barney community and family notices

    Newspaper social columns add small but useful checks on the household. In August 1913, Mrs. I. L. Folsom and sons Ben and Manor returned to Barney after visiting near Quitman. A September notice recorded I. L. Folsom making a business trip to Adel.[27][28] These notices agree with the 1910 and 1920 family schedules.

    In October 1914, Turner Folsom of Barney was injured when his gun discharged. The report identified him as a son of I. L. Folsom.[21] The item supplies contemporary support for the relationship later stated on Turner's death certificate.

    The same evidence does not establish a store, bank office, elected post, or named partnership for Israel. No reliable online source found in this search placed him in those roles.

    Turner Folsom's killing

    Turner Folsom was shot and killed in July 1923. Contemporary reporting described the encounter as a dispute involving Max Jordan, a tenant or farm worker associated with the Folsom and McGill plantation network.[22] Later that year, while Jordan awaited trial, Israel Folsom told authorities that witnesses were being threatened. The claim of intimidation was his allegation and should remain attributed to him.

    The coverage is important for Israel's biography because it directly identifies Turner as his married son, places Israel at Barney, and describes the scale of the family's plantation and cotton interests. It also explains why the 1923 articles devote unusual attention to Israel's property and standing in the community. Trial follow-up appeared in November, but the accessible reports do not provide a complete court file or final appellate record.[22]

    Death, probate, and burial

    Folsom suffered a stroke on Sunday, January 31, 1926, and died at his Barney home on Thursday morning, February 4. The Valdosta Daily Times called him a prominent citizen and wealthy farmer. He was sixty-five by the dates on his marker, although the obituary called him sixty-six.[23][25]

    The obituary named his widow and seven surviving children: Mrs. Grady Hendrix, Mrs. T. H. Byrd, F. B. Folsom, B. F. Folsom, Manor Folsom, A. J. Folsom, and I. L. Folsom Jr. Turner had died in 1923. The funeral was set for February 5 at Camp Ground Church near Morven, where Folsom was buried.[23]

    On April 5, 1926, the Brooks County Court of Ordinary appointed Ann C. Folsom administratrix of I. L. Folsom's estate. The original administrator's bond survives.[24] It verifies the widow's legal role and closes the identity chain, but it does not reveal the estate inventory, land descriptions, debts, heirs' distributions, or final value.

    His marker at Camp Ground Cemetery reads “Israel Lee Folsom / Apr. 11, 1860 / Feb. 4, 1926.”[25] The inscription agrees with the census birth month and year, the obituary's death date, and the probate sequence.

    Evidence assessment

    1. The Barney farmer is verified as Israel Lee Folsom. The 1860-1920 censuses, original marriage record, family record, obituary, probate bond, and grave inscription form a continuous identity chain.
    2. His marriage to Ann C. Walker and eight surviving children in 1910 are verified. Contemporary reports identify the ninth child as a young daughter who drowned in 1895, but they do not give her name or age. The 1900 fertility count conflicts with that death and is treated as a census error.
    3. His career as a farmer and substantial plantation operator is verified in broad outline. Exact acreage, title history, cotton production totals, and estate value were not found.
    4. His identity as the I. L. Folsom named in the 1896 South Georgia Railroad charter petition is strong probable, not verified. The published list uses initials only, and the original petition was not located.
    5. He was not named among the South Georgia Railroad's first directors or officers. No later South Georgia board service or stockholding was found.
    6. His 1912 directorship in the Georgia, Alabama and Western Railway Company is verified. That proposed railway was separate from the South Georgia Railroad.
    7. The full name Ebenezer George Folsom for Israel's father is strong probable. Israel's mother, Priscilla Jane, is verified through the census chain.
    8. A possible 1881 coroner-jury reference was rejected from the narrative because surviving reprints conflict between I. L. and L. L. Folsom and provide no identifying bridge.

    Open questions and limits

    • The original 1896 South Georgia Railroad charter petition, signatures, subscription book, stock ledger, and corporate minutes were not located online.
    • No source found online states Folsom's South Georgia Railroad share count or establishes later service as a company director or officer.
    • No complete deed chain, plantation map, tax series, farm ledger, tenant ledger, or cotton account was found.
    • The probate index and administrator's bond were located, but the estate inventory, returns, distributions, and final settlement were not.
    • The 1895 reports identify the ninth child as a young daughter and describe her drowning, but they do not give her name, exact age, burial place, or a funeral notice.
    • The 1900 census says six children had been born and all six were living, which conflicts with both the 1895 drowning and the five children listed in the household. The later 1910 count of nine born and eight living fits the drowned daughter and the eight surviving children.
    • The father's expanded name, Ebenezer George Folsom, remains strong probable because the 1860 record was indexed E. J.
    • The reports do not establish ownership of a Barney cotton gin, a named commercial partnership, a bank office, or an elected public position.
    • A possible 1881 coroner-jury service cannot be assigned to him because contemporary reprints conflict on the juror's initials.

    Search coverage

    Research covered the local Barney and South Georgia Railroad files, Huxford's county history, newspaper corpora, and stored census references. Online work covered the federal censuses for 1860, 1870, 1880, 1900, 1910, and 1920; Brooks County marriage and probate collections; Georgia death records; cemetery evidence; Georgia Historic Newspapers; preserved Newspapers.com searches and direct image pages; the Internet Archive copy of Huxford; and broad exact-name web searches. Two July 1895 reports document the daughter's drowning.

    Search variants included "I. L. Folsom", "I L Folsom", "I. L. Folsome", "Israel Folsom", "Israel L. Folsom", "Israel Lee Folsom", "Ann C. Folsom", "Annie Folsom", and "Turner Folsom", combined with Barney, Brooks County, Quitman, Morven, railroad, railway, director, farmer, plantation, cotton, fire, court, death, funeral, obituary, probate, and cemetery. Namesakes were rejected unless a record supplied the correct age, Brooks County or Barney location, Ann or the children, farming profile, or known railroad role.

    Original images were inspected for the 1884 marriage, 1910 and 1920 censuses, Turner's death certificate, the 1926 administrator's bond, and Israel's marker. No claim is made that unindexed courthouse, corporate, plantation, or church records were searched.
