    Dr. Edwin A. Jelks

    Back to the Railroad Organizers index

    1836-1908 | Quitman physician, Confederate surgeon, civic investor, and later South Georgia Railway director

    A note about his name

    The man usually called Edwin Augustine Jelks in local histories is securely identified in contemporary and official records as Dr. Edwin A. Jelks. His medical school, the federal censuses, military indexes, newspapers, marriage register, and gravestone use the initial but do not spell out his middle name.[1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8]

    Two later sources disagree about what the A stood for. A detailed family biography written by Dr. E. L. Jelks and published in Folks Huxford's 1948 county history calls him Edwin Augustine Jelks. Find a Grave memorial 36063788 calls him Edwin Augustus Jelks.[9][10] No primary record located in this investigation resolves the difference. This dossier therefore uses Edwin A. Jelks in its title and treats both expansions as later claims.

    This identity control matters. Edwin was not his son Edwin Briggs Jelks, his nephew and later medical partner Dr. Edwin Lamkin Jelks, or his nephew O. K. Jelks of the Bank of Quitman. He was also not the Dr. E. A. Jelks of Oviedo, Florida, whose profitable citrus acreage appeared in an 1889 newspaper report.[9][11]

    Childhood, family, and education

    The family account gives his birth as October 1, 1836, at Hawkinsville, Georgia, to James Oliver Jelks and Mary Polhill Jelks. His gravestone independently confirms the date.[8][9] In 1850, thirteen-year-old Edwin lived in Pulaski County with I. O. Jelks, Mary Jelks, and four boys whose names and ages match brothers described by the family history. Because the 1850 census did not state household relationships, the parental identification comes from the combination of that household and the later family account.[1][9]

    The family biography says the Jelkses moved to Hamilton County, Florida, while Edwin was young. A Mercer University catalogue places Edwin A. Jelks of Hamilton, Florida, among the students for 1855-1856. It confirms attendance, but not a degree.[12] The biography says Jelks taught school for two years after Mercer and began medical study at Jefferson Medical College in Philadelphia in September 1858.[9]

    The strongest evidence for the end of his medical training is a primary source from the Medical College of the State of South Carolina. Its annual circular for the 1859-1860 session lists "Jelks, E A" of Belleville, Florida, with L. A. Folsom as his preceptor, then names him among the graduates.[13][14] This proves that he completed the Charleston program during that session. The school's circular does not confirm every part of Huxford's story about the withdrawal of Southern students from Philadelphia, but it fits the account's basic sequence. The American Medical Association's directory of deceased physicians also identifies him as a 1860 medical graduate.[15]

    By June 1860, the federal census placed twenty-three-year-old E. A. Jelks in Brooks County, Georgia, with the Tallokas post office. He was already practicing as a physician and reported $200 in personal property.[2] This identity-secure census entry conflicts with a later memorial biography that places him in Calhoun County, Alabama, that same year. A second enumeration or a namesake is possible, but the Brooks County record is the one tied clearly to the Quitman doctor.

    Building a practice in Quitman

    The family biography says Jelks joined the practice of Dr. Henry Briggs in the young town of Quitman. The territory was broad, roads were poor, and much of a country doctor's work required travel by horse. The description is a family recollection rather than a surviving office ledger, but contemporary records support the larger picture of a long medical career.[9]

    In 1870 Jelks was a physician in Quitman with real property valued at $4,000 and personal property at $1,000. His household included his wife Constance and daughter Elizabeth.[3] The 1880 census again called him a physician and placed the family at 12 Court Street.[4] In 1900 he still reported medicine as his occupation and owned his Quitman home free of mortgage.[5]

    Huxford identifies Jelks as a participant in the Brooks County Medical Society formed in 1868. The same county history's physician register lists him as a Pulaski County native and an 1860 graduate of the South Carolina medical school.[16] No original society minutes were found, so the precise organizing role remains Huxford-based.

    His statewide professional standing is directly documented. In April 1902 the Valdosta Times reported that Jelks, chairman of the Georgia State Board of Medical Examiners, had gone to Augusta and Atlanta to examine applicants for licenses.[17] A March 1903 report named him as the physician attending Mitchell Brice after Brice suffered paralysis.[18] These notices show that Jelks remained professionally active late in life, even if he had begun reducing the most demanding parts of his practice.

    The memorial published after his death remembered him as a particularly capable surgeon and as a doctor who continued to care for poor patients. It also said Governor Allen D. Candler appointed him to the state medical examining board. The praise is valuable evidence of how Quitman remembered him, but its superlatives are eulogy, not an objective ranking.[19]

    Confederate medical service

    Jelks entered Confederate service with the 26th Georgia Infantry. Two National Park Service indexes preserve complementary records. The regimental entry associates Edwin A. Jelks with Company C and field and staff, and shows a progression from private to surgeon. A second entry for Confederate general and staff officers shows Edw. A. Jelks entering as assistant surgeon and leaving as surgeon.[20][21]

    The precise chronology comes from a later biographical register of Confederate physicians, reproduced on the Find a Grave memorial. It says Jelks enlisted as a private in the Piscola Volunteers at Quitman on July 23, 1861, was detailed as an assistant surgeon that September, received a Confederate appointment as assistant surgeon in February 1862, and was appointed surgeon in May. It also says he was paroled as surgeon of the 26th Georgia at Appomattox on April 9, 1865.[10] These details are consistent with the official rank and unit indexes, but the underlying individual service cards and parole image were not accessible. The dates should therefore be read as strong compiled-record evidence, not as a substitute for the original file.

    The National Park Service's unit history says the 26th Georgia first served on St. Simons Island and at Savannah, then moved to Virginia. It fought in campaigns from the Seven Days through Cold Harbor, later served with Jubal Early in the Shenandoah Valley, and ended at Appomattox.[22] Huxford's biography places Jelks with the medical service during several of those campaigns.[9] A unit's battle history does not prove one surgeon's presence at every engagement. The safe conclusion is that Jelks served as a Confederate medical officer with a regiment that endured those campaigns.

    One story from that service resurfaced in Pennsylvania newspapers in 1898. According to a modern York County history article that quotes the period reports, Jelks paid the heirs of Jacob Rudy $125 for a horse obtained near York during the Gettysburg campaign. Jelks explained that he had paid Rudy in Confederate money on June 29, 1863 and later decided that payment had not been fair. The story rests on the York newspaper correspondence and is credible as a reported act of restitution, though the original letters themselves were not located.[23][24]

    Two marriages and four children

    A compiled Georgia marriage index records E. A. Jelks and C. D. Atkinson marrying in Camden County on November 10, 1863.[25] Huxford identifies her as Constance Atkinson, daughter of Edmund Atkinson and Sarah Elizabeth Waddell Atkinson. The 1870 and 1880 censuses place Constance or C. A. Jelks in Edwin's household.[3][4][9] The original Camden County entry was not available, so the exact date remains strong but not primary-record proof.

    Edwin and Constance had four children:

    • Mary Elizabeth Jelks, born November 6, 1868, and died July 9, 1885.
    • Sarah Josephine Jelks, born July 28, 1870. She married D. A. Denmark and died in March 1940.
    • Edwin Briggs Jelks, born December 18, 1873. He married Anne Louise Felder in 1898 and died in December 1930.
    • Nellie Constance Jelks, born December 19, 1879, and died December 3, 1880.[9]

    The 1880 census supports the four-child household and the children's approximate ages.[4] The exact dates come mainly from the family history. Find a Grave gives Mary Elizabeth a birth year of 1863, but Huxford's 1868 date agrees with her census age of eleven in 1880. The 1863 claim is therefore contradicted by the better evidence.

    Constance died in 1890, according to Huxford.[9] The original Brooks County marriage register proves Edwin's second marriage. A license for Dr. E. A. Jelks and Miss Janie E. Sinclair was issued January 17, 1891, and the minister certified that he married them on January 20.[7]

    The 1900 census found Edwin and Janie married nine years. His daughter Josephine and Alma and Willie Albritton shared their home.[5] Huxford describes Alma and Willie as Janie's orphaned nieces who became members of the household. The census called them boarders, so they should not be recast as daughters. Edwin and Janie had no children together.[5][9]

    Business and investment in a growing town

    Jelks's life in Quitman extended far beyond his medical office. The record shows a doctor who repeatedly invested in the town's buildings, industry, utilities, and financial institutions.

    In January 1880 a contemporary newspaper reported the sale of a factory property to Jelks, Mabbett, and Hines for slightly under $14,000.[26] The following year, a federal court report named E. A. Jelks and others as defendants in litigation over the Quitman Mills land.[27] These reports directly connect him to the mill property. Huxford adds that Jelks, Harry Mabbett, Joseph Mabbett, and H. B. Hines sought a charter for Quitman Mills in September 1880 with $20,000 in capital, and that Jelks served as president.[28] The presidency remains a strong secondary-source claim because the original charter and minutes were not located.

    Huxford also says Jelks initially subscribed $2,000 when the cotton mill was revived in 1899, with all listed subscriptions later increased by 25 percent.[28] That would make his commitment $2,500, but the larger number is arithmetic based on Huxford's account, not an independently printed subscription amount.

    Contemporary newspapers document his property development. In September 1889 Jelks had begun three two-story brick stores in Quitman. A January 1890 survey of local construction valued the three buildings at $10,000.[29][30] In June 1890 he was named as a director of the Quitman Real Estate and Investment Company, which was reported to have earned a 13 percent net return after its first year and organization expenses.[31]

    His documented Bank of Quitman directorship spans fourteen years, from 1891 to 1905. Newspapers recorded his election or reelection in 1891, 1892, and 1893, and a 1905 report again listed him on the board.[32][33][34][35] Huxford's statement that he was among the bank's first directors is consistent with this pattern, though the earliest organization record was not inspected.

    Jelks also helped organize new services. In February 1896 a newspaper named him among the men forming a telephone stock company in Quitman after enough customers had subscribed to make service practical.[36] Huxford later placed him among the incorporators of the Quitman Telephone Company. The county history also linked him to the first board of the Quitman Library Association and to the Hotel Marie corporation.[37] A 1912 anniversary article independently named him among the library's first directors.[38] The hotel evidence is less clean: an 1889 newspaper's OCR renders one director as "Dr. E. K. Jelks," while Huxford identifies E. A. Jelks. The likely OCR error should not be hidden.

    There is no sound evidence that Edwin A. Jelks was a farmer or commercial grower. The 1889 citrus story belongs to a doctor in Oviedo, Florida.[11] His cotton-mill investments and a household garden described by Huxford do not make him a planter.

    Schools, church, politics, and civic work

    Jelks was repeatedly chosen for Quitman's school leadership. Newspapers named him in school-board nominations in 1892, described him as a current board member in 1896, reported his successful nomination in 1900, and again placed him on the board in 1902.[39][40][41][42] This proves service by 1896 and repeated public selection across a decade. It does not prove one uninterrupted term from 1892 through 1902.

    He also appeared in broader civic work. In 1873 he joined a local committee supporting an inland-water communication proposal.[43] In 1890 he served on a committee considering a $35,000 hydraulic cotton compress and was selected as a Brooks County delegate to the state Democratic convention.[44][45] The evidence shows local influence, but not a career of elective office.

    The family biography says Jelks joined the Quitman Presbyterian Church on September 14, 1877 and became an elder on May 19, 1878, serving for the rest of his life.[9] These exact dates were not confirmed in church minutes. They are credible family and local-history testimony and should retain that label.

    Railroads and the South Georgia Railroad

    Huxford lists E. A. Jelks among the twenty-six petitioners for the South Georgia Railroad charter in 1896.[46] No original charter petition, stock ledger, subscription book, or corporate minute book was found. Contemporary reports of the permanent organization name the first directors and officers, and Jelks was not among them.[47][48] He was also absent from the surviving reports of the January subscription committees.

    The 1896 evidence supports one narrow conclusion: according to Huxford, Jelks was a charter petitioner. He was not a first director, officer, or subscription committeeman, and no stockholding or investment amount can be assigned to him from the surviving records.

    A later source changes the directorship question. On April 29, 1906, the Atlanta Journal described Dr. E. A. Jelks of Quitman as a physician, capitalist, and director of the South Georgia Railway.[53] This verifies that he was identified as a director by 1906, but it does not establish when he joined the board, how long he served, or whether he owned stock.

    His interest in transportation was broader than that one company. In February 1899 he called a Quitman mass meeting to order and helped explain why the town needed another railroad running south to protect its trade.[49] That was a separate proposed line, not the South Georgia Railroad.

    In 1907 E. A. Jelks appeared among the petitioners for a charter for the proposed Quitman, Valdosta and Thomasville Electric Railway and Power Company.[50][51] The notices described a planned fifty-mile electric street railway. They prove participation in the petition, not that the railway was built. The listing appears to refer to the elder doctor, who was still alive, and separately names other Jelks family members where applicable.

    Final years, death, and burial

    The family biography says heart trouble forced Jelks to stop night calls around 1895 and that he retired fully in 1904.[9] Contemporary evidence shows at least some active practice in 1903, so his withdrawal was gradual rather than abrupt.[18]

    On July 28, 1908, the Valdosta Daily Times reported a telegram from Quitman: Dr. E. A. Jelks had died at 2 p.m. after about two weeks of illness. The report said he had suffered from heart trouble for years and had experienced a paralytic stroke about a year earlier. His daughter, Mrs. D. A. Denmark of Valdosta, and other relatives were present.[6]

    A follow-up memorial said he was buried on July 29 before a large gathering.[19] His photographed stone at West End Cemetery in Quitman reads "E. A. Jelks, M.D.," followed by October 1, 1836 and July 28, 1908.[8][10] The stone and same-day newspaper settle the death year as 1908. The 1909 date found in some catalog and web descriptions is wrong.

    The memorial portrayed him as a doctor who had grown with Quitman from its early years, a trusted adviser in civic affairs, a successful businessman, a Presbyterian elder, and a familiar friend to children and poor patients.[19] The language is affectionate and plainly local. Its value lies not in every superlative, but in the breadth of the life the town believed it had lost.

    Historical assessment

    Edwin A. Jelks practiced medicine in Quitman for more than forty years. Outside his practice, he served on boards or committees connected with schools, banking, mills, utilities, the library, construction, and transportation proposals. Medicine remained the center of his public identity, from his 1860 graduation through his state examining-board work more than forty years later. His Confederate service carried prestige in his own era, while his investments and board work tied him to many of Quitman's development efforts.

    Huxford identifies Jelks as a South Georgia Railroad charter petitioner. Contemporary organization reports show that he was not on the first board in 1896. By April 1906, however, the Atlanta Journal identified him as a South Georgia Railway director. His election date, length of service, and stockholding remain unresolved.

    Later accounts disagree about his middle name. One gives the wrong state for his 1860 residence, another gives the wrong year for his daughter's birth, and several descriptions give the wrong year for his death. The census entries, medical-school circular, marriage register, military indexes, contemporary newspapers, and gravestone resolve much of the basic chronology. The original military file, first marriage entry, church records, South Georgia Railroad petition, and probate estate remain unexamined or missing.

    Evidence limits and open questions

    • No primary record located here spells out Jelks's middle name. Augustine and Augustus are conflicting later expansions.
    • No Mercer degree is proved. The catalogue supports attendance in 1855-1856.
    • The medical-school circular proves his Charleston attendance and graduation, but the Jefferson Medical College phase still rests mainly on Huxford.
    • The original Confederate service cards and individual Appomattox parole were not accessible. Exact appointment dates come from a later compiled register.
    • No original Camden County image for the 1863 marriage was inspected.
    • No original Presbyterian membership or elder record was found.
    • No original South Georgia Railroad charter petition, subscription book, stock ledger, or company minutes were located. The 1906 newspaper verifies a later directorship as printed, but not Jelks's election date, length of service, or stockholding.
    • The Georgia Archives catalogue says its small Jelks collection includes a copy of his will, but the document is not digitized and was not inspected. No probate packet, inventory, or estate settlement was found. The catalogue also gives his death year as 1909, which the newspaper and gravestone disprove.[52]
    • West End Cemetery is supported by the memorial and a legible stone photograph, but no cemetery register was inspected.
