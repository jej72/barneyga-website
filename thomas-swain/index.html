    Thomas S. Swain

    Thomas S. Swain was a Telfair County public official, road superintendent, ferry proprietor, surveyor, livestock owner, and enslaver. Georgia's official 1823 report identifies him as one of the two men appointed to supervise the Alapaha-to-Florida road that became the southern part of Coffee Road. He spent his later years at Jacksonville on the Ocmulgee River and died in 1840.[1][2]

    Swain left a thinner paper trail than his fellow superintendent, General John Coffee. No personal correspondence, will, obituary, military record, or authenticated portrait was located online. Even so, official reports, statutes, newspapers, census material, estate notices, and a photographed grave marker allow much of his public and economic life to be reconstructed. They also show where the record stops.

    Identity

    The road superintendent was Thomas S. Swain of Telfair County. The controlling evidence is Georgia's 1823 legislative report, which says an executive order of December 24, 1822 appointed "General John Coffee and Thomas S. Swain Esqrs. of the county of Telfair" to superintend the road.[1]

    Later records follow the same man through a distinctive combination of name and place. Georgia authorized Thomas S. Swain to establish an Ocmulgee River ferry on his own land opposite Jacksonville in 1831. A sheriff's notice records a judgment in his favor in 1838. Estate notices then identify Thomas S. Swain as a deceased Jacksonville resident with Ocmulgee land and property spread across Telfair and Irwin counties.[5][8][9]

    The evidence does not support merging every Thomas Swain in early Georgia with him. A later Telfair debtor of that name was alive in 1856, sixteen years after this man's estate opened. An Emanuel County legislator listed only as "Swain" in 1827 has not been identified. Kenneth Swain, whom Montgomery M. Folsom named as a hunter for Coffee's road party, was a different person unless new evidence proves otherwise.[17][18]

    Birth, migration, and family

    A modern grave memorial with a photographed marker gives Swain's life dates as 1784 to 1840. The marker establishes the traditional birth year, but no birth, baptism, family Bible, or other contemporary birth record was located. A FamilySearch profile places his birth in Bertie County, North Carolina, and names Job Swain and Susanna Smith as his parents. The public sources attached to that profile do not prove either claim, so his birthplace and parents remain unresolved.[12][13]

    Julian Williams's Jacksonville history says Swain came from Warren County, Georgia, about 1808. That account was written long after the event and no migration deed or tax sequence was found online. It is useful local history, not direct proof.[14]

    Rebecca Swain served with Mitchell G. Willcox as administrator of Thomas's estate from 1840 into the 1840s. Family and cemetery sources identify her as Rebecca Pridgen, his wife, who lived about 1795 to 1865. No marriage entry was inspected. The relationship is strongly supported by the combined estate, family, and cemetery evidence, but the contemporary notices call her administratrix rather than widow.[9][12][15]

    The number of their children is not settled. A Telfair County family history says Thomas and Rebecca had six. The current FamilySearch tree attaches seven. Susannah, Martha, Elizabeth, and Mark have the strongest support from family histories, marriage indexing, linked memorials, and their placement with the family. Thomas Jr., Stephen, and Josiah appear in the online tree but were not tied to Thomas by an original record inspected for this project. A complete child list should wait for the probate distribution, family Bible, or comparable record.[12][13][15]

    Telfair County public service

    Swain held county office before the road project. An official resolution approved December 7, 1812 appointed Thomas Swain a justice of the Telfair County Inferior Court, replacing one removed justice and one who had resigned. Inferior Court justices handled county judicial and administrative business, including roads, ferries, estates, and local government.[3]

    The name, county, public standing, and chronology fit the later road superintendent. Because the 1812 resolution omits the middle initial, it is best read as very strong identity evidence rather than an entirely self-proving link.

    A local history also places Swain on the Telfair Inferior Court in 1814 and says he later conveyed Jacksonville's old courthouse lot to Methodist Episcopal trustees in 1834. The attached deed image requires a FamilySearch sign-in and was not inspected, so the land gift remains a well-supported local account rather than a verified deed finding.[14]

    Coffee Road

    Georgia approved the road measure on December 23, 1822. It authorized the governor to appoint two suitable people to supervise a road beginning at or near Cunningham's Ford on the Alapaha River. The route was to pass through specified Irwin and Early County land districts and continue to the Florida line near the Ochlocknee River. The state appropriated $1,500. The statute did not name the two appointees.[4]

    The next year's official legislative report supplies the names and dates. It says Governor John Clark appointed General John Coffee and Thomas S. Swain of Telfair County on December 24, 1822. Work began in August 1823 and ended November 15. The committee described nearly ninety miles of road, plus about seven additional miles needed to reach a suitable Alapaha crossing, several small bridges, a larger bridge over a stream whose printed name is difficult to identify, and four ferry flats.[1]

    The report does not explain how Coffee and Swain divided the fieldwork. It says Coffee received the full $1,500 appropriation. The governor's order supplied no instructions, contract, performance bond, or accounting method. Legislators therefore could not judge the work or determine how the money had been spent, and they ordered a detailed report within six months.[1]

    The accounting remained unresolved in the next published report. In 1824 Coffee sent the governor a letter and accounts, but the legislative committee said they still did not show enough about the work or the manner in which it had been completed. The legislature asked the governor to investigate. The report does not accuse Coffee or Swain of stealing money, and it does not say funds were missing. It shows an inadequately documented public project. No separate report from Swain or final settlement was located online.[6]

    Coffee Road later came to mean a longer corridor extending north toward the Ocmulgee. The 1822 state project began at the Alapaha, not at Jacksonville or Swain's Ferry. A published transcription of Irwin County minutes says county officials appointed Sion Hall, James Allen, and Thomas Townsend in July 1824 to lay out the northern Ocmulgee-to-Alapaha segment. Nothing located online shows that Swain supervised that separate extension.[7]

    Twentieth-century Georgia historical markers repeatedly name Coffee and Swain as the road's superintendents. Those markers preserve the correct names, but the 1823 official report is the earlier and stronger source.[16]

    Swain's Ferry and Jacksonville

    Legislative reporting in 1830 described a proposal allowing Thomas S. Swain to establish a ferry on his own land opposite Jacksonville. The enacted measure appeared in the 1831 passed-act list as an authorization for Thomas S. Swain to establish a ferry across the Ocmulgee River.[5]

    This is the best evidence for the origin of the name Swain's Ferry. It does not prove that a chartered ferry was operating during the 1823 road work. The authorization came eight years later. An earlier informal crossing is possible, but no ferry license, bond, deed, or court entry was located to establish one.

    The middle initial appears as L or J in a few newspaper renderings. The 1823 official report, the 1831 passed-act list, the 1838 court notice, and the 1840 estate notices use S. That repeated form in the stronger sources controls.[1][5][8][9]

    Surveying and other possible records

    Georgia Archives metadata identifies Thomas S. Swain as the surveyor of Cherokee County, Section 3, District 5, from May 15 through June 13, 1832. The name and initial match, and the timing is compatible with the Telfair man. The field notebook metadata does not give the surveyor's residence or another identifier, so this assignment remains probable rather than certain.[19]

    A Telfair election report from 1831 says Thomas J. Swain received six votes for district surveyor and lost to John Fitzsimons. The J may be a printing error, or the candidate may have been another man. It cannot safely be used to prove or disprove the identity of the Cherokee surveyor.[20]

    No verified militia commission, War of 1812 service, pension, bounty-land file, or muster roll was located for Thomas S. Swain. He should not be assigned a military rank. The title "Esquire" in the 1823 report indicates civil standing, not military office.[1]

    Land, livestock, and business

    By the end of his life Swain ran a large regional livestock operation. A sale notice dated April 15, 1840 advertised between five hundred and six hundred cattle at John Willis's place in Irwin County, between one hundred and two hundred at Woodson Willcox's place in Telfair County, and about two hundred at Jacksonville. The estate also held sheep, horses, and other stock.[9]

    Later notices advertised property at Swain's Jacksonville residence: three horses, three mules, a yoke of oxen, about eight hundred bushels of corn, a two-horse wagon, blacksmith tools, household and kitchen furniture, and farming equipment. His real estate included the western half of Telfair Land Lot 269 and all of Lot 321, about 429.9 acres on the Ocmulgee River.[10]

    A June 1838 sheriff's notice records an Irwin Superior Court execution in favor of Thomas S. Swain against Angus Finlyson and William Bradford. The notice does not explain the underlying debt, but it documents Swain's business activity beyond Jacksonville shortly before his death.[8]

    Estate administration continued for years. Rebecca Swain and Mitchell G. Willcox were still seeking authority to sell estate land in 1849. The long administration is consistent with the size and geographic spread of the property, though the notices do not explain the delay.[11]

    Slavery

    Swain was an enslaver. An 1840 administrator's notice said fourteen enslaved people belonging to the estate would be hired out for the following year. The notice does not name them, give their ages, or preserve their family relationships.[10]

    A Telfair family history reports that Sarah Pridgen sold an enslaved person to Swain on February 13, 1819 for his daughter Susannah. The original instrument was not accessible online, so that transaction should remain attributed to the family history rather than presented as an independently inspected deed.[15]

    The public 1830 census image identifies Thomas S. Swain as a Telfair County household head, but the accessible image did not provide a reliable complete slave-schedule count for this research. No names or additional numbers should be inferred. The probate inventory, appraisement, hire records, sale bill, and estate division are the best remaining sources for recovering the identities and family ties erased by the newspaper notices.[13]

    Death and burial

    Thomas S. Swain died before April 8, 1840. On that date Telfair clerk James H. McCall issued a citation on the application of Rebecca Swain and Mitchell G. Willcox for letters of administration. A later sale notice dated April 15 also calls Swain deceased. No obituary, funeral notice, medical account, or exact death date was located.[2][9]

    Find a Grave memorial 86291771 includes a photograph of a marker reading Thomas S. Swain, 1784-1840, in Swain Family Cemetery at Jacksonville. The memorial and marker give strong support for the burial place and life years. No cemetery register, church record, or contemporary burial record was found, so the exact dates and original placement of the marker remain unverified.[12]

    Portrait search

    No authenticated likeness of Thomas S. Swain was found. The search covered Wikimedia Commons, Library of Congress Prints and Photographs, the Digital Library of Georgia, Georgia Archives, Internet Archive metadata, Georgia Historical Society marker pages, Find a Grave, family-history pages, and general web indexes.

    Because Swain died in 1840, any photograph would require unusually strong provenance from the earliest years of American photography. No portrait, miniature, silhouette, engraving, or family-owned image located in this search could be tied to him. Images of General Coffee, unrelated men named Thomas Swain, or a generic pioneer should not be used as his likeness.

    Research limits

    The documented search covered stored Barney and Coffee Road research; Georgia's published acts and legislative reports for 1812, 1822, 1823, and 1824; Georgia Archives and Digital Library of Georgia records; Georgia Historic Newspapers; available Newspapers.com material; the 1830 federal census record; FamilySearch public records and trees; Find a Grave; Internet Archive; Library of Congress; Wikimedia Commons; Georgia Historical Society markers; Telfair family histories; cemetery compilations; and general web indexes.

    Not located online were Swain's birth or baptism record; proof of his parents; marriage record; family Bible; complete deed and tax sequence; the original December 24, 1822 executive order; road contract, vouchers, payroll, survey, or final settlement; Swain's correspondence; ferry license or bond; original 1832 surveyor appointment; military file; full probate packet; named inventory and hire records for the enslaved people; obituary; exact death date; cemetery register; or authenticated portrait.

    Those gaps do not weaken the official proof of Swain's road appointment, his Telfair County identity in 1822, his Jacksonville estate, or the scale and character of his property. They do require caution about his origins, complete family, precise role in the road work, ferry operations before 1831, military service, and exact burial history.
