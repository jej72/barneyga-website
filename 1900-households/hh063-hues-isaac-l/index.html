    Family Narrative

    The household is no longer limited to a single 1900 census entry. New records strongly suggest that Isaac L. Hues of Barney is the same man later recorded in Colquitt County under heavier surname distortion as Isaac L. Huker or Hues in 1910 and as I. Leonard Hughes in 1930, with a 1937 Colquitt County death certificate for Leonard Hughes completing the line.

    In 1900, Isaac L. Hues appears in Barney with wife Sallie and four children: Asa, George, Rachel, and infant Chester. The census gives his birth as October 1866, reports that the couple married in 1892, and shows Isaac as a farm laborer in a rented household. One useful detail in this record is the parental birthplace pattern: father born in North Carolina and mother born in Georgia.

    That same parental pattern appears again in the 1910 census of Warrior District, Colquitt County, where Isaac L. Huker, age 42, is living with wife Sallie E. and children James A., George M., Chester, and Sallie M. The age, wife's name, Georgia birthplace, and father-from-North Carolina and mother-from-Georgia pattern all fit the 1900 household well. The record also suggests that the family had shifted from Brooks County into neighboring Colquitt County by 1910. One unresolved point is the oldest son: James A., age 17, may be the same child listed as Asa in 1900, but that identification should remain provisional until another record ties the names together directly.

    By 1930, the likely same couple appears in Robinson District, Colquitt County, as I. Leonard Hughes, age 63, and wife Sallie Hughes, age 59, with daughter Dollie May Hughes, age 23. The household detail again reports a Georgia-born man with a father born in North Carolina and a mother born in Georgia. He was farming on rented land and was still unable to read and write. Dollie May may be the younger daughter recorded as Sallie M. in 1910, but the name difference means that point should also remain cautious.

    The death certificate for Leonard Hughes, who died in Moultrie on 14 Feb 1937, makes this identification still stronger. It gives a birth date of 20 Nov 1867, occupation farmer, burial in Sardis Cemetery, and parents Asa Hughes and Millie Wilkes. The informant was D. H. Hughes of Hahira. The age, location, and household pattern make this a strong fit for HH#63, though one detail does not line up cleanly: the death certificate places Leonard's father in South Carolina, while the census records consistently report North Carolina. That conflict is not unusual in late records, but it should be noted rather than smoothed away.

    A newly found marriage record for Asa Hughs and Emily Wilks, dated 4 Feb 1867 in Tattnall County, gives direct support to the parent names reported on Leonard's death certificate. The spelling differs slightly from the death record's Asa Hughes and Millie Wilkes, but the names are close enough to treat the marriage as strong corroborative evidence rather than a coincidence. It does not by itself prove that Leonard was their son, but it materially strengthens the case that the death certificate points to a real Hughes-Wilks couple in Georgia.

    A Florida death index entry for Sallie E. Hughes, a white woman who died in Dade County in March 1962, may be relevant to the same family. The name fits well, and the timing would be plausible for the widow of Leonard Hughes, but the index entry alone is too thin to prove the identification. Until a full death certificate or another linking record is found, this should remain a lead rather than a settled conclusion.

    Taken together, these records support a solid working identification for the household and show that the surname moved through several forms in records: Hues, Huker, and Hughes. The family appears to have moved from Barney in Brooks County into Colquitt County by 1910 and remained there through Leonard's death in 1937, with a possible later Florida death record for Sallie still needing confirmation.

    Open Research Questions

    • Parentage audit: Keep head-of-household and spouse parentage as unresolved unless a cited direct record names the parents or the entry explains the census chain supporting the identification.
    • Child census audit: Continue direct-record tracking for each child named in the 1900 household where later census, marriage, death, burial, obituary, or other parent-link proof is not already cited above.
    • Other household member audit: Verify every non-nuclear household member, including in-laws, boarders, servants, laborers, uncles, grandchildren, and collateral relatives, before treating the stated relationship as part of the proved family structure.
    • A marriage record for Isaac or Leonard Hues-Hughes and Sallie, likely about 1892, is still needed.
    • Leonard Hughes's death certificate should be weighed against earlier census evidence because it gives the father's birthplace as South Carolina rather than North Carolina.
    • The Asa Hughs-Emily Wilks marriage record supports the parent names on Leonard's death certificate, but Leonard still needs a direct record tying him to that couple.
    • James A. Hughes in 1910 may be the same person as Asa Hues in 1900, but that needs direct confirmation.
    • Dollie May Hughes in 1930 may be the same child as Sallie M. in 1910, but the name difference needs explanation.
    • Rachel Hues from 1900 has not been securely located in later records.
    • A 1920 census placement for the household is still needed.
    • The Florida death index entry for Sallie E. Hughes, died March 1962 in Dade County, should be tested with a full death certificate or other Florida records before being accepted.
    • Burial, cemetery, probate, land, and church records should still be gathered for Leonard, Sallie, and the children.

    Research Status

    Substantial census and death-record chain in hand. HH#63 can now be followed with good confidence from Brooks County in 1900 into Colquitt County in 1910 and 1930, with a strong likely death record in 1937. A possible Florida death index entry may point to Sallie's later life, but that still needs confirmation. The main remaining work is to tighten child identifications, find the missing 1920 household, document Sallie's later life and death, and push the family back into earlier records.
