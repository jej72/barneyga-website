    Family Narrative

    The 1900 census places John T. Morrow in Barney, Brooks County, Georgia, as a white farmer with wife Minerva "Minnie" and nine children still at home. By that date, the family was plainly well established in the Barney community.

    The census chain is now fairly solid. In 1880, John T. Morrow, age 31, was enumerated at Morven, Brooks County, with wife L. J. V. Morrow, age 29, and children John W., Eliza A., and Sallie. In 1900, the same couple appears at Barney with the household expanded to include William, Lillian, Eliza, Sallie, James, Thomas, Caddie, Charley, and Nancy. In 1910, John F. Morrow, age 60, and wife Laura J. Morrow, age 57, were still in Barney with James and Nancy in the home. Together, those three censuses show the same household across thirty years in the same part of Brooks County.

    The later widow entries also help identify Minerva "Minnie" Morrow as Laura J. V. Delk Morrow. A Brooks County marriage index entry shows "Liv Delk" marrying J. T. Morrow on 22 December 1871. In 1920, Mrs. L. J. V. Marrow, age 68, appears in Barney as mother-in-law in the W. E. Phillips household. In 1930, Laura J. Morrow, age 78, appears in the same family cluster in Brooks County as mother-in-law in the William E. Phillips household. Laura Jane Virginia Morrow's 29 June 1930 Brooks County death certificate ties the chain together directly. It names her as Laura Jane Virginia Morrow, widow of John Morrow, daughter of John Delks and Jane Hodges, with W. E. Phillips of Barney as informant. Read together with the 1880 wife recorded as L. J. V. Morrow and the 1900 wife recorded as Minnie, those records support the conclusion that the 1900 Minnie was Laura Jane Virginia Delk Morrow.

    Earlier material is less secure. A Laura J. V. Delk, age 8, appears in Brooks County in 1860 in the household of John W. Delk, which fits the later wife and supports the Delk maiden-family identification. A 1860 Heard County census entry places John T. Morrow, age 9, in the household of Joseph E. Morrow, age 46, and Eliza G. Morrow, age 45. That record fits HH#84 John T. Morrow reasonably well on name, age, place, and family structure, and it gives the Heard County origin theory more weight than it had before. Even so, it is still one step short of proof, because a direct bridge from that child in 1860 to the adult John T. Morrow in Brooks County has not yet been found. A possible 1870 John Marrow in Brooks County may point to the same line, but that identification remains uncertain. Find a Grave memorials for Joseph Ewing Morrow and Eliza Jane Walker Morrow are consistent with the 1860 Heard County household and with the broader family hypothesis, but they remain derivative support rather than proof of John T. Morrow's parentage. A memorial for Sarah Winfrey Walker McCoy, identified there as Eliza Jane Walker Morrow's mother, also fits the broader Walker and McCoy network, but it too remains a clue rather than direct evidence for HH#84.

    The daughter lines are clearer than they were at the start of this review. Laura's death certificate first pointed toward the Phillips household because W. E. Phillips served as informant. That link is reinforced by additional records: a Brooks County marriage record shows Ruth Morrow marrying Wm. Earnest Phillips on 19 April 1913; the 1920 Barney census shows Ruth Phillips, identified as Ruth Morrow, in the W. E. Phillips household with her mother listed as L. J. V. Morrow; and the 1930 Brooks County census again places Ruth Phillips with Laura J. Morrow in the same household cluster. Those records support the conclusion that Ruth, or Nancy Ruth Morrow, was the daughter who married William Earnest Phillips and remained in Barney.

    The Sallie line is also much better defined. A Brooks County marriage record shows Sallie Morrow marrying Jno. Delk on 7 December 1905. The 1910, 1920, 1930, and 1940 censuses track Sallie Delk in Barney and Militia District 1571 with husband John F. Delk and sons Carl and Hoke, then as a widow with son Hoke still at home in 1940. A World War II draft registration card for Joseph Hoke Delk of Barney names his mother as Mrs. Sallie Delk of Barney, which fits that census trail. Camp Ground Cemetery memorials for Sally E. Morrow Delk and John Flemming Delk match the same household and children shown in the census material, and a Florida death notice and SSDI entry point to Sallie's later death in Orange County, Florida, in April 1966. The cemetery and index material remains derivative, but the full set of records makes it likely that the Sallie Morrow in the 1900 household was the daughter who married John Flemming Delk and stayed rooted in the Barney community for decades.

    Camp Ground Cemetery memorials for Nancy Ruth Morrow Phillips and William Ernest Phillips fit the same family and burial cluster, but they should still be treated as derivative support unless backed by direct records. Camp Ground Cemetery memorials for J. W. Morrow and James E. Morrow remain plausible matches to sons from this household, but those identifications still need parent-linked support.

    Open Research Questions

    • Parentage audit: Keep head-of-household and spouse parentage as unresolved unless a cited direct record names the parents or the entry explains the census chain supporting the identification.
    • Child census audit: Continue direct-record tracking for each child named in the 1900 household where later census, marriage, death, burial, obituary, or other parent-link proof is not already cited above.
    • A death record, probate record, burial record, or obituary for John T. Morrow is still needed.
    • The identifications of J. W. Morrow and James E. Morrow as sons from this household still need direct supporting records that name parents.
    • The Phillips daughter question is now largely resolved in favor of Ruth Morrow, but a death certificate, obituary, or other direct record naming both parents would still strengthen the case further.
    • Sallie Morrow's Delk line is now strongly supported across marriage and four censuses, but a direct death certificate or obituary naming her parents would still tighten the proof.
    • Laura's 1930 death certificate should be paired with a clearly readable burial record or grave evidence if the burial place on the certificate cannot be read confidently from the available image.
    • The 1860 Heard County John T. Morrow entry is now a strong lead, but it still needs a direct bridge to the adult John T. Morrow in Brooks County.
    • The possible 1870 Brooks County John Marrow entry needs more corroboration before it can be treated as this same John.
    • The Joseph Ewing Morrow and Eliza Jane Walker Morrow memorials support the Heard County hypothesis, but they do not by themselves establish that HH#84 John T. Morrow was their son.
    • The Sarah Winfrey Walker McCoy memorial may help connect the line to the Walker and McCoy families, but it remains derivative support and does not close the case.
    • The wider Delk, Hodges, and Lashley connections should remain outside the core proof until they are tied in through marriage, death, probate, land, or church records.

    Research Status

    Well-supported census reconstruction, but not fully closed. The 1880, 1900, 1910, 1920, and 1930 censuses now give a strong multi-decade chain for John T. Morrow and Laura Jane Virginia Delk Morrow, and the Ruth Phillips and Sallie Delk evidence materially improves the child identifications. John's death and burial and several son identifications still need direct-record support.
