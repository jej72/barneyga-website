    Farming, livestock, and markets

    Although timber shaped the early town, the surrounding district remained heavily agricultural. The railroad gave farmers a practical route to outside buyers. Wheat reports in 1899 named growers around Morven and Barney and discussed Brooks County soils and rust problems. Watermelon acreage and shipments were prominent by 1899 and 1900. One report said 187 carloads had been shipped during the previous melon season. Growers also complained that freight charges left them little profit. The railroad opened distant markets, but its rates helped determine whether a crop paid.[17]

    Farm notices document more than cotton and watermelons. A 1901 report praised the ridges near Barney for peaches and named M. F. Jones among growers with bearing orchards. A Moultrie account described Sim Hires's cane, hogs, and losses during dry weather. An August 1902 article reported fine peaches on W. B. Gornto's farm, while a 1902 industrial list recorded local cotton gins.[18]

    In February 1903, an Adel report described I. B. Ingram near Barney as producing twenty-five bales of sea island cotton with three plows, along with food crops, cattle, and hogs for sale. The figures come from a contemporary newspaper profile, not independently audited farm accounts.[18]

    In March 1903, M. B. Williams announced plans to establish a cattle operation within a fenced 400-acre pasture. The article documents his plan, but no source cited here confirms that he completed the enterprise.[18]

    The 1900 census gives a broader view of work in the district. The reconciled enumeration counted farmers and agricultural laborers alongside workers in sawmills, lumber, turpentine, naval stores, stores, railroads, domestic service, and medicine. Farming and timber work existed side by side, supported by the railroad and local merchants.[19]

    Freight complaints keep the agricultural story honest. The station did not guarantee prosperity. A grower could raise a marketable crop and still lose much of its value to transportation charges, weather, spoilage, or distant prices. Barney's railroad access created opportunity, but it also exposed local farmers to decisions made outside the district.
