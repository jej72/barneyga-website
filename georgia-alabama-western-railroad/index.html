    The Georgia, Alabama and Western Railroad Company

    The Georgia, Alabama and Western Railroad Company was chartered in January 1912 to build an east-west line through Barney. The original proposal ran about 150 miles from Milltown to the Alabama line near Blakely. By June, promoters were describing a larger Brunswick-to-Dothan system.[1-6]

    The organizers solicited stock, held community meetings, pursued rights of way, and hired engineers. No surviving timetable, station list, equipment return, completed-mileage report, or train schedule shows that the company ever operated. Georgia's official 1916 railroad map shows no corresponding line through Barney as of June 30, 1915.[7-13]

    The company name

    The 1911 petition called it the Georgia Alabama and Western Railroad Company. Some 1912 newspapers used Georgia, Alabama and Western Railway Company, but no located amendment records a legal name change. This page follows the petition.[2][5][8-12]

    Two unrelated ventures used or proposed the same name: an Atlanta-based company listed in 1908 and a 1914 Atlanta, Birmingham and Atlantic reorganization plan.[14][15] The Barney venture was also separate from the Valdosta, Moultrie and Western, the South Georgia Railway, and the Waycross and Western.

    The original route

    The published petition estimated a line of about 150 miles. It would begin at Milltown in Berrien County and run west to the Alabama line near Blakely. The route named:

    Order in the petition Community or area County
    1 Milltown Berrien
    2 Hahira Lowndes
    3 Barney Brooks
    4 Pavo Brooks and Thomas
    5 Meigs and Coolidge Thomas
    6 Parts of Grady and Mitchell counties Grady and Mitchell
    7 Near Babcock and through Colquitt Miller
    8 Blakely and the Alabama line Early

    The petition proposed $200,000 in common stock, divided into $100 shares, with authority to increase the total to $3 million. It requested a fifty-year corporate term and placed the principal office in Waycross. Ten men signed as petitioners:[2]

    Community Petitioners
    Barney E. H. Beck, T. A. Rozier, Fleming Bates Walker (printed “F. B. Walker”)
    Hahira W. W. Webb, L. M. Stanfield, Robert Barfield
    Waycross J. E. T. Bowden, G. W. Deen
    Pavo J. Frank Harris, John A. Kennedy

    The notice authorized stock solicitation and construction; it does not prove that the money was raised or track laid.

    Financing and the charter

    Promotion was active by November 1911. A Quitman dispatch said E. H. Beck expected the organizers to place the stock not taken by the incorporators. Temporary treasurer T. M. Sweat of Hahira was receiving subscriptions, but no located record gives the amount paid.[3][4]

    After legal notices appeared along the route, Secretary of State Philip Cook granted the charter on January 25, 1912. The charter authorized the company to organize and build; it did not certify that financing or construction was complete.[2][5][6]

    Barney's part in the project

    Three Barney men signed the petition: Beck, Rozier, and Fleming Bates Walker. By June 1912, reports named T. A. Rozier and I. L. Folsom as directors, Beck as financial agent, and Walker as vice president. Those reports reversed Walker's initials as “B. F. Walker.”[2][8-12]

    Barney residents also organized locally. On April 19, 1912, the Valdosta Daily Times reported that Hahira citizens were taking subscriptions, obtaining rights of way, and making preliminary surveys from Hahira toward Barney. Barney residents had held their own meeting that week and were encouraging the extension.[7]

    The article also mentioned a separate railroad building west from Waycross toward a proposed connection at Milltown, apparently the Waycross and Western. Its account of subscriptions, right-of-way work, and preliminary surveys toward Barney does not prove that Georgia, Alabama and Western track was being laid.

    Had the line reached Barney, it would have crossed the South Georgia Railway and made the town a junction. No evidence shows that the crossing was built.

    The survey and the Brunswick-to-Dothan plan

    On May 31, 1912, the board met at Hahira and ordered president W. W. Webb to arrange an immediate survey and construction-cost estimate. Crook & Lanneau, civil engineers of Americus, were hired as chief engineers, with a field party to work under F. Lanneau.[8-12]

    The board also told Webb to seek an amendment extending the original Milltown terminus to Brunswick or another coastal point. Reports traced the larger route through Milltown, Ray's Mill, Hahira, Barney, Pavo, Coolidge, Meigs, Colquitt, and Blakely to Dothan. They placed the general offices in Hahira, although the petition had named Waycross.[8-12]

    Promoters claimed twenty-two railroad connections, no parallel line within eighteen miles, and profitable traffic. No located traffic study, engineering estimate, construction contract, or charter amendment confirms those claims.

    Was any part built?

    On June 4, the Brunswick News said the company “only consists of a few miles now.” This is the only located suggestion of completed mileage, but the article gives no endpoints, owner, construction date, stations, or service. It also says the survey and cost estimate had just been ordered and refers to the railroad as something that might be built.[11] The meaning of “a few miles” remains unresolved.

    Evidence against an operating line is stronger:

    • No located report identifies completed Georgia, Alabama and Western track between two named points.
    • No timetable, depot list, freight tariff, passenger schedule, locomotive, car roster, or operating return has been found.
    • The June reports still describe surveys, estimates, and the route as future work.
    • The Railroad Commission of Georgia's 1916 map, based on mileage in operation on June 30, 1915, shows no east-west railroad through Barney matching the proposal.[8-13]

    The company almost certainly never operated as a common-carrier railroad. Survey stakes, cleared right of way, grading, or short private track may nevertheless have existed.

    Why the project ended

    The last substantial reports date from June 1912. No located source gives an abandonment date, charter forfeiture, receivership, or reason for failure. The project may have faced weak financing, competition, or high construction costs, but the evidence does not identify a cause.

    Open questions

    • Where is the original Secretary of State charter packet, and was the Brunswick amendment ever filed or granted?
    • Do stock books, bank records, or T. M. Sweat's papers show actual subscriptions and payments?
    • Did Crook & Lanneau complete a survey, field book, estimate, or route plat?
    • Do Berrien, Lowndes, Brooks, Thomas, Grady, Mitchell, Miller, or Early County deed books contain Georgia, Alabama and Western right-of-way instruments?
    • What did the Brunswick News mean by "a few miles"?
    • Did any grading occur between Hahira and Barney or elsewhere on the route?
    • Was the charter formally dissolved, forfeited, or simply allowed to lapse?
    • Why did the effort stop after June 1912?
