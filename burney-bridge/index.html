    Burney Bridge on Little River

    Research dossier

    Prepared August 26, 2026

    Scope and conclusion

    Burney Bridge was a separate Little River crossing on the old road system between Tallokas and the Nashville, Sparks, and Berlin side of the river. It was northwest of Rock Bridge and Folsom Bridge. It was not an alternate name for either one.

    The road story begins in 1869, when Brooks County advertised a new route from Tallokas northeast across Okapilco Creek and through the Colter, Alderman, and Norman neighborhoods to the Nashville Road at Little River. That notice does not name Burney Bridge or prove that a bridge stood at its endpoint. The later road geography makes Burney Bridge the strong probable endpoint, but the connection is not explicit.[1-4]

    The bridge itself is directly named by 1887. Later reports show that it was an established regional landmark, a proposed gathering place, and the starting point of a Berrien County road toward the Sparks road. Brooks and Berrien counties agreed to rebuild it in 1903. By May 1904, high water had delayed the work for more than a year even though the crossing carried "a great deal of travel". The original newspaper page confirms that rains swept the bridge away in April 1908.[5-11]

    The record then goes quiet until 1941, when a child drowned near Burney Bridge. Federal bridge data now close part of the later gap. The National Bridge Inventory identifies a county bridge built in 1966 on Hempstead Church Road over Little River, approximately six miles northwest of Barney. Its coordinates are within about 15 meters, or 50 feet, of the locally identified Burney Bridge point. The 1966 structure was a seven-span concrete T-beam bridge about 253 feet long.[14-16]

    No located record identifies the Burney namesake, dates the first bridge, describes the nineteenth-century structure, proves completion of the 1903-1904 rebuilding, or explains the full replacement sequence between 1908 and 1966.

    Evidence grades

    This dossier uses four grades:

    • Verified: stated in a direct original, contemporary, or official record, or fixed by several independent records without a material conflict.
    • Strong probable: several specific facts agree, but no source states the entire conclusion.
    • Possible: plausible, but the evidence is incomplete or does not distinguish alternatives.
    • Unresolved: the evidence does not support a choice.

    Location and bridge identity

    James identifies Burney Bridge at 31.067920872044272, -83.56471852760521. That point began as a local geographic control, not an official survey coordinate. The federal inventory now supplies an independent check. It places the Hempstead Church Road bridge over Little River at 31.0680389, -83.5646697, about 15 meters away.[15]

    The federal location description reads "APP 6 MI NW OF BARNEY". The 1974 USGS Berlin East quadrangle, photorevised in 1988, independently plots the local road crossing Little River at the same place.[16]

    This fixes the modern crossing. It does not prove that every older deck occupied precisely the same line. Nineteenth-century timber bridges were often repaired, shifted, or replaced without a surviving plan. The historical evidence converges on the same road and river reach, making the 1966 bridge the strong probable modern successor at Burney Bridge.

    The surrounding county geography changed. Before Cook County was created in 1918, contemporary reports placed the bridge where the Brooks, Berrien, and Colquitt area met. The 1908 washout dispatch described Little River there as dividing those counties. The modern crossing remains close to the Brooks, Cook, and Colquitt corner.[11,16]

    Separation from Rock Bridge and Folsom Bridge

    The three bridges belonged to different roads:

    Crossing Historical road system Geographic control
    Burney Bridge Tallokas-side road to Little River, Berrien-side road to the Sparks road, and later Hempstead Church Road 31.0680389, -83.5646697 in the federal inventory
    Rock Bridge Adel-Quitman Road, later old GA 76, directly north of Barney 31.050137, -83.499369 local control, supported by the 1908 map and nearby USGS station
    Folsom Bridge Foster and the Hahira-facing road, later SR 122 30.999009, -83.456601 local control

    Burney Bridge lies roughly four straight-line miles northwest of Rock Bridge and about eight straight-line miles northwest of Folsom Bridge. The May 1904 newspaper item clearly concerns "Burney bridge over Little river". It should not be corrected to Barney or used in Rock Bridge history.[10]

    The Tallokas-to-Little River road

    The 1869 opening notice

    On July 5, 1869, Brooks County ordered notice of a proposed road "commencing at Tallokas, running Northeast". It would cross Okapilco Creek at or near John Colter's, pass the residences of John Alderman and John Norman, and intersect the Nashville Road at Little River. The notice appeared in the Quitman Banner on July 9 and July 30.[1]

    The sequence is unusually useful because it names a start, a creek crossing, three residents, a direction, and an established road at the river. It proves that county officials were trying to create a Tallokas-to-Little River connector. It does not name a bridge at the endpoint.

    The route can be tested against later land and map evidence. An original Tallokas tax image from the 1872-1877 volume group records John A. Colter with 735 acres in Land Lots 459 and 468. Lot 459 lies on the locally reconstructed approach from Tallokas toward Burney Bridge. The 1908 Brooks County map shows a developed public-road network through the same broad neighborhood.[3,12]

    The local reconstruction follows Lots 458, 459, 460, and 415, crosses Okapilco Creek in Lot 415, briefly enters Colquitt County, returns to Brooks near Lots 490 and 477, and reaches Little River at Burney Bridge. That reconstruction fits the period evidence, but no surviving road survey has yet proved every segment or centerline.

    The 1870 discontinuance proceeding

    The route's early legal history is not continuous on the present record. In July 1870, an application sought to discontinue the road from Tallokas by Colter, Alderman, and Norman to Little River. Work was suspended while the court invited objections through August. No final decision has been found.[2]

    The 1908 Brooks County map supplies later evidence that a Tallokas-to-Nashville road existed and appears to follow the 1869 directions through the same broad corridor.[12] This shows that a road connecting those places existed in 1908 despite the unresolved 1870 proceeding. What remains unproved is whether the 1908 road followed the exact 1869 path. The earlier route may have been retained, altered, discontinued and later reopened, or replaced by a road on a similar line. The 1869 notice therefore remains the strong probable beginning of the documented Burney corridor, but it does not prove one unchanged legal road or centerline.

    County maps and later highways

    B. W. Frobel's 1869 Brooks County map contains a faint, unlabeled route in the northern road network that broadly matches the Tallokas-to-Little River corridor. It supplies supporting geography but no bridge name or decisive legal status.[4]

    The 1908 Flippen and Hudgins map shows the mature road network around Tallokas, Colter, Alderman, Norman, Okapilco Creek, and Little River. The inspected image does not provide a legible Burney Bridge label at the crossing, so the map should be used to establish the network, not to date the bridge.[12]

    The rural delivery map from about 1930-1938 preserves the later northern Brooks road pattern but does not clearly label Burney Bridge or prove which postal route crossed it.[17]

    The 1939 state highway map places SR 33 on the old Moultrie-Quitman Road through Tallokas and Berlin and SR 122 through Rock Hill and Barney. The Tallokas-Nashville Road was a separate road that intersected SR 33; the two roads did not follow the same path. The map therefore documents the later numbered-road setting but does not identify the Tallokas-Nashville Road or Burney Bridge as part of SR 33.[13]

    The 1966 official highway map retains that broad state-route framework. The federal inventory is more specific about Burney Bridge itself: the crossing remained a county-owned and county-maintained bridge on Hempstead Church Road.[15,18]

    The bridge before 1900

    Earliest located name, 1887

    The earliest direct use found is a Valdosta Times article published September 24, 1887. In a discussion of a proposed railroad and possible depot sites, a writer called Sineath Crossroads or Boyd's "the only convenient point from W. R. Ryals to Burney's bridge". The same discussion referred to the Brooks-Colquitt corner and the Berlin post office.[5]

    The article does not say when the bridge was built. It does show that Burney's Bridge was established regional geography by 1887, familiar enough to serve as an endpoint in a public argument about roads, depots, and community access.

    Gathering place, 1892

    In September 1892, the Hempstead Democratic Club proposed a mass meeting at "Burney's bridge, or some other accessible point" and invited residents of Berrien and Brooks counties. The wording again treats the bridge as a known place and says something about its accessibility. It does not describe its structure or condition.[6]

    The Berrien-side road, 1894

    In May 1894, Berrien County ordered a new public road beginning at Burney Bridge on Little River and running northeast to the Sparks road at Paul Crosby's. The order named Caroline Flowers, B. George, G. W. Ingram, W. B. Crosby, Marcus Wright, S. J. Wright, W. W. Hancock, William Castleberry, James Castleberry, W. W. Giddens, and Paul Crosby along the proposed line.[7]

    This is the strongest early bridge-and-road anchor. The order begins at an existing named bridge, so Burney Bridge predates the new road. It also shows that the crossing was not merely a local Tallokas outlet. On the Berrien side, it connected directly to the Sparks road. A connection with the Nashville road system is probable from the wider road geography, but this order does not state it.

    The 1869 and 1894 records therefore describe complementary sides of a likely through route. One reached Little River from Tallokas. The other began at Burney Bridge and ran northeast toward the Sparks road. The geographic connection is strong probable. No single record joins the two as one continuously named road.

    Deep water and the Rowland near-drowning, 1898

    On September 3, 1898, B. A. Rowland and his wife nearly drowned while crossing Little River at Burney Bridge. They entered water deeper than expected but escaped. The report appeared six days later in the Tifton Gazette.[8]

    The article proves a water-related travel hazard. It does not say whether the danger was on the bridge deck, a flooded approach, or another part of the crossing. It also does not identify the storm or rainfall that caused the deeper water. The safest conclusion is that high or unexpectedly deep water made the crossing dangerous, not that the bridge itself failed.

    The 1903-1904 rebuilding

    In June 1903, the Tifton Gazette reported that Berrien County commissioners had agreed with Brooks County to rebuild Burney Bridge across Little River. The need for joint action fits a county-line bridge. The short item gives no reason for rebuilding and no cost, contractor, material, dimensions, or design.[9]

    The work did not move quickly. A May 1904 Adel News report said Burney Bridge was in very bad condition. Rebuilding material had been on the ground for more than a year, but water had stayed too high for the work until shortly before the article. The bridge carried "a great deal of travel", and the newspaper urged the commissioners to take advantage of low water and complete the job.[10]

    The chronology suggests that planning or material delivery began before the June 1903 public report. It does not prove when construction started or whether the counties completed that particular rebuilding. No completion notice has been found.

    The 1904 report also shows that Burney Bridge was part of an active travel corridor, not an obscure farm crossing. The long delay illustrates the practical problem of bridge work in the Little River floodplain. Water levels could prevent construction even when officials, materials, and public pressure were already in place.

    The 1908 washout

    A Macon newspaper dispatch dated April 5 and published April 6, 1908 says: "The recent rains in this section caused nearly all the streams to rise. The Burney bridge over Little River, which divides the counties of Berrien, Brooks and Colquitt, was swept away."[11]

    The original Newspapers.com page was visually read on August 27, 2026, confirming the saved OCR. The washout is verified from the contemporary page.

    The 1908 washout supplies an end point for the bridge then standing, but it does not settle the delayed 1903-1904 rebuilding cycle. The article does not tell us whether the lost bridge was the completed replacement, an older span still awaiting work, or an intermediate repair. It also does not say when another bridge opened.

    The 1941 drowning

    On May 29, 1941, twelve-year-old George Berry Croft of the Berlin community drowned while swimming with other children in Little River near Burney Bridge. Reports said he stepped into a deep hole and that his body was recovered about thirty minutes after he was missed.[14]

    This was a river drowning near the bridge, not a traffic accident, washout, or structural failure. It proves that Burney Bridge remained a recognized place-name in 1941. The article does not describe the bridge or expressly state that a bridge still stood there.

    A separate 1945 accident occurred at an unnamed bridge on Tallokas Road. The available report does not call it Burney Bridge. It cannot be assigned to this crossing.

    The 1966 county bridge

    The strongest later evidence comes from the Federal Highway Administration's National Bridge Inventory. Structure 000000002700470 carried Hempstead Church Road over Little River at the Burney Bridge location. The 2022, 2023, and 2024 Georgia inventory files all retain the record.[15]

    The coded record describes:

    Field Official value
    Facility carried Hempstead Church Road
    Feature crossed Little River
    Location Approximately six miles northwest of Barney
    Year built 1966
    Owner and maintenance responsibility County highway agency
    Main structure Concrete T-beam
    Main spans Seven
    Maximum span 14.3 meters, about 47 feet
    Total length 77.1 meters, about 253 feet
    Roadway width 7.3 meters, about 24 feet
    Deck width 9.2 meters, about 30 feet

    The 1974/1988 USGS Berlin East map confirms the road crossing at the same location. The NBI record grades the inventoried bridge as not eligible for the National Register under its historic-significance code. That classification applies to the 1966 structure, not to the historical importance of the older crossing.[15-16]

    The federal record verifies a 1966 successor. It does not reveal what stood there between 1908 and 1966, whether the 1966 bridge reused older abutments, or whether its centerline shifted slightly. Bridge files, county minutes, and aerial photographs are still needed to answer those questions.

    Name and namesake

    Both "Burney's Bridge" and "Burney Bridge" appear in contemporary records. The possessive form in 1887 and 1892 suggests a personal or family namesake, but no inspected source identifies that person. The surname alone is not enough to choose among Burney families or landholders elsewhere in south Georgia.

    The name should not be modernized to Barney. The independent 1887, 1892, 1894, 1898, 1903, and 1904 references form a consistent record for one separate crossing. Searches for "Burny Bridge" and "Birney Bridge" found no additional relevant event.

    What is proved

    Question Conclusion Grade
    Was Burney Bridge the same as Rock Bridge or Folsom Bridge? No. The roads, coordinates, and source sequences identify three separate crossings. Verified
    Did the 1869 Tallokas road end at Burney Bridge? It reached the Nashville Road at Little River in the correct corridor. Later geography points strongly to Burney Bridge, but the notice does not name it. Strong probable
    Did Burney Bridge exist by 1887? Yes. A contemporary article used "Burney's bridge" as established regional geography. Verified
    Was a road opened northeast from the bridge in 1894? Yes. The Berrien order began at Burney Bridge and met the Sparks road. Verified
    Did deep water endanger travel in 1898? Yes. B. A. Rowland and his wife nearly drowned while crossing there. Verified
    Did Brooks and Berrien agree to rebuild the bridge? Yes, by June 1903. Verified
    Did high water delay rebuilding through May 1904? Yes. Material had been present for more than a year, but water prevented work. Verified
    Was the bridge swept away in April 1908? Yes. The original newspaper page says recent rains raised the streams and swept away Burney Bridge. Verified
    Was a bridge at the crossing built in 1966? Yes. Federal inventory structure 000000002700470 fixes its location and construction details. Verified

    What remains unresolved

    • The Burney namesake.
    • The first bridge's construction date, builder, material, dimensions, and precise deck line.
    • Whether the 1870 discontinuance application succeeded.
    • The exact legal and physical continuity between the 1869 road, the later Burney road, and Hempstead Church Road.
    • Completion and details of the 1903-1904 rebuilding.
    • The replacement sequence between 1908 and 1966.
    • Whether the 1966 bridge reused an older approach, abutment, or deck line.

    Best next records

    1. Brooks County Ordinary and County Purposes Books A and B for the 1869 petition, reviewers' return, opening order, and final 1870 discontinuance decision.
    2. Brooks and Berrien commissioner minutes, bridge books, warrants, contractor accounts, and road-superintendent reports for 1903-1908.
    3. Brooks, Berrien, Colquitt, and later Cook county bridge records covering replacement after 1908.
    4. Georgia Highway Department inventory cards, plans, inspection files, and correspondence for NBI structure 000000002700470.
    5. A georeferenced comparison of the 1869 and 1908 county maps, the 1930s rural-delivery and highway maps, USGS sheets, and USDA aerials from the 1940s through 1966.
    6. Deeds, tax digests, and probate files for Burney landholders near the crossing to identify the namesake.

    Search coverage and limits

    Stored memory, bridge dossiers, road reports, map analyses, coordinate ledgers, saved newspaper OCR, and related genealogy files were searched before online sources. Georgia Historic Newspapers was searched from 1763 through 1960 using exact and proximity variants for Burney Bridge, Little River, Tallokas, Nashville, Sparks, Berlin, road, repair, rebuilding, flood, high water, washout, accident, and drowning. The focused exact-phrase search returned fifteen pages. Relevant original pages from 1869, 1870, 1892, 1894, 1898, 1903, and 1904 were checked.

    Preserved Newspapers.com results covering 1850 through 1960 supplied the 1887 article, the 1908 washout report, and the 1941 drowning reports. Fresh access initially failed after two browser timeouts, and public requests returned 403. The original 1908 page was subsequently opened and read on August 27, confirming the washout report. The Newspapers.com and Ancestry links in the source list require the appropriate subscription or account.

    Official records checked included Georgia Archives county maps, Digital Library of Georgia rural-delivery and highway maps, the USGS Historical Topographic Map collection, and FHWA National Bridge Inventory files through 2024. Complete county commissioner minutes, bridge books, contractor files, and Georgia Highway Department project files were not online and were not inspected.

    This search is broad and reproducible, but it is not exhaustive. The missing county records are likely to contain the first construction, rebuilding, and replacement details.
