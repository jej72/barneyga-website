    Lawrence Folsom

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Online research dossier

    Prepared: August 18, 2026
    Subject: Lawrence Folsom, early settler of southern Irwin and original Lowndes County, Georgia
    Research purpose: Identify the man later called Lawrence Armstrong Folsom, test the tradition that he belonged to Lowndes County's first four settler families, and document his land, family, public, religious, and community record without merging him with later Folsoms.

    Bottom line

    Lawrence Folsom was among the earliest documented settlers in the part of southern Irwin County that became Lowndes County in 1825. The first original record located for him is a recorded deed dated November 27, 1823. Newman Pounds sold Lawrence Folsom of Irwin County all 490 acres of Land Lot 187, 12th District, for $268. The deed was recorded December 31, 1823.[1-3]

    The familiar claim that he was one of Lowndes County's first four settlers is supported as an old local-history tradition, not as a proved 1821 event. The earliest inspectable quartet account found is William Harden's 1913 county biography. It groups Lawrence with James Rountree, Drew Vickers, and Alfred Belote, says they explored the region in 1815, and calls their families the first permanent white settlers of Lowndes, especially its northern portion.[4] Folks Huxford's 1922 quarterly gives the sharper date of 1821, but credits much of its information to then-recent newspaper sketches by Hortense W. Baker whose underlying article and sources were not located.[5] “First permanent white settlers” is the later sources' Euro-American settlement terminology; it is not evidence that the land was previously uninhabited.

    The safest conclusion is that Lawrence was one of four men repeatedly identified in early twentieth-century histories as the first permanent settler group of original Lowndes County. His early presence is independently verified by the 1823 deed and by his appointment as a county-site commissioner in December 1825. The exact 1815 scouting trip and 1821 family migration remain unverified.[1-5]

    The contemporary records reviewed call him Lawrence Folsom. The identification with the later-named Lawrence Armstrong Folsom, husband of Rachel Vickers and ancestor of the local Folsom family, is strong probable rather than strictly verified because no reviewed original record uses the middle name. His likely life span is about 1772-1775 to April 11, 1842, but the exact birth year and birthplace remain disputed.[4,6-10]

    Identity verdict

    Contemporary Lawrence and the later middle name

    The identity chain is persuasive:

    • The 1823 deed names Lawrence Folsom of Irwin County and places him on Lot 187 east of Little River.[1-3]
    • A public transcription of the 1830 Lowndes census lists a Lawrence Folsom household with three males and three females.[11-12]
    • Later local and family histories consistently describe Lawrence Armstrong Folsom as the Burke-to-Pulaski-to-Lowndes migrant who married Rachel Vickers, settled near Little River, and died in Lowndes.[4,6-10]
    • A contemporary 1893 account says James Folsom inherited his father's lands and that James's children later divided them. The 1938 genealogy identifies James as Lawrence's youngest son, creating a strong, though partly derivative, land-and-family chain.[8,13]

    No inspected deed, census entry, legislative record, or church deed gives Lawrence the middle name Armstrong. The dossier therefore uses Lawrence Folsom for original-record claims and treats Lawrence Armstrong Folsom as the strong-probable later identification.

    Namesakes and related men

    Lawrence must be kept separate from:

    • William "Uncle Billy" Folsom, a son in the compiled genealogy who became a large landholder west of Little River.[8]
    • Randal or Randall Folsom, another early local Folsom and later public figure.[8]
    • James Folsom, the son who reportedly inherited Lawrence's lands.[8,13]
    • Elijah, John, Maston, and other Folsoms appearing in the 1830 census and later histories.[8,11]

    Shared surname, neighborhood, or land use is not enough to merge these men.

    The first-four-settlers tradition

    What the sources actually say

    Harden's 1913 A History of Savannah and South Georgia is the earliest fully inspectable source located that names the quartet. In a biography of Ashley Lawson and his wife Cynthia Folsom, it says Lawrence Armstrong Folsom, James Rountree, Drew Vickers, and Alfred Belote explored southern Irwin County in 1815, acquired land, returned for their families, and became "the first permanent white settlers of Lowndes county, more especially of its northern portion."[4] Two details warn against reading it literally: its statement that they acquired “government land” conflicts with Lawrence's verified private purchase from Newman Pounds, and its statement that they returned to Brooks County uses a county name that did not exist until 1858.

    Huxford's 1922 South Georgia Historical and Genealogical Quarterly states that the same four men and their families moved in 1821 and were the first settlers of what it called the present county of Lowndes. The article says its data came chiefly from Hortense W. Baker's recent Valdosta Times sketches. No direct 1821 record or Baker source note was found.[5]

    These sources preserve a credible early-settlement tradition, but they were written roughly ninety years after the events. The 1823 deed is the earliest original proof located for Lawrence in the region.[1-5]

    Conflicting 1899 chronology

    R. E. L. Folsom's 1899 Valdosta Times "Historic Sketch of Lowndes County" gives a different sequence. It calls Washington Joyce's 1824 home and ferry the first white settlement in present Lowndes County and places Drew Vickers and Lawrence Folsom next.[14] That account conflicts with Lawrence's verified November 1823 land purchase.

    The geographic wording explains part, but not all, of the disagreement. The quartet tradition makes the most sense for original Lowndes County, which was much larger than today's county. The 1899 sketch explicitly discusses present Lowndes and Joyce's east-bank settlement at the later Miller Bridge. Even so, the conflict shows that none of the later narratives should be treated as a precise arrival calendar.

    Finding: strong for the existence and membership of the tradition; probable for permanent settlement in the early 1820s; unresolved for the 1815 trip and exact 1821 arrival.

    Origins, marriage, and children

    Elizabeth Knowles Folsom's 1938 Genealogy of the Folsom Family identifies Lawrence as a son of William Folsom and Sally Armstrong. It says he was born in Burke County, while preserving another informant's statement that he was a North Carolina native. The book gives his birth only as about 1775; modern collaborative profiles usually use 1772. No contemporary birth or parent-naming record was found.[8-10]

    The same genealogy says he married Rachel Vickers, daughter of Jonathan Vickers, in Burke County, and gives her death as December 18, 1849, in Lowndes County. Huxford reportedly described Rachel as Drew Vickers's sister, but other researchers have not independently confirmed that relationship. The marriage is strong probable; Rachel's precise parentage and her relationship to Drew remain derivative.[7-10]

    The 1938 genealogy lists eight children: William, Randal, John, Elijah, Maston, Cynthia, Elizabeth, and James.[8] Harden's 1913 account lists nine: William, John, Maston, Randall, James, Elijah, Cynthia, Rachael, and Betsy.[4] Collaborative online trees also disagree and sometimes duplicate children. The dossier does not flatten these into a definitive list.

    The cleanest working reconstruction is the 1938 eight-child list, labeled compiled and conflict-aware. James receives stronger contextual support from the 1893 inheritance article, although that article itself does not name his father.[8,13]

    Migration and household evidence

    The 1938 genealogy says Lawrence moved from Burke County to Pulaski County in 1808 and later to Lowndes County.[8] The 1913 history adds the 1815 exploration narrative.[4] Both are retrospective.

    The first firm date is November 27, 1823, when Lawrence was described as a resident of Irwin County in the Lot 187 deed.[1-3] Lowndes County did not yet exist; it was created from Irwin on December 23, 1825.[5,15]

    The public 1830 census transcription lists Lawrence Folsom - 3 M, 3 F.[11] It compresses the original schedule to sex totals and does not preserve age columns or household-level enslaved-person details. The NARA microfilm is freely available online, but it has no usable text layer and Lawrence's exact image was not identified during this investigation.[12] The transcription confirms a household only at a derivative level.

    The derivative land/tax KML places Lawrence in District 12 Lot 182 in 1830, 1834, 1835, 1838, and 1840, and Lot 187 in 1830, 1834, 1835, and 1838. It places Drewry Vickers on Lot 187 by 1838/1840 and James Folsom on Lots 182 and 187 in 1844.[16] The Lot 187 deed is original-record proof; the tax years and Lot 182 remain derivative until their digest images are inspected.

    Land and geographic setting

    The Lot 187 deed

    FamilySearch DGS 008032657, images 50-51, preserves the recorded instrument. Newman Pounds of Gwinnett County sold Lawrence Folsom of Irwin County all 490 acres of Land Lot 187, 12th District, for $268. The deed was executed November 27, 1823 and recorded December 31. It says Pounds's state grant was dated November 17, 1823. John Durham and W. B. McGehee, J.P., witnessed it; Pounds signed by mark.[1-3]

    A clerk's note says the instrument was transcribed from Book B, pages 65-66, on February 7, 1827. This corrects three derivative errors: the grantor was Newman, not Norman, Pounds; the deed date was November 27, not December 27; and the state grant date was November 17, not November 7.[1-3]

    Lots 182 and 187 lie east of Little River in the later Folsom Bridge neighborhood. The deed proves Lawrence's ownership of Lot 187, not the exact footprint of his house.[16-17]

    Folsom Bridge caution

    No contemporary source says the bridge was named for Lawrence. His verified and indexed landholdings, together with the later family-land chain through James Folsom's children, make the surrounding Folsom settlement the strongest geographic source of the name.[13,16-17]

    The Folsom Bridge history brings together the settlement, naming, map, road, and bridge evidence.

    The defensible conclusion is that the bridge probably took its name from the Folsom family settlement. Lawrence is the leading individual candidate, but the namesake remains unresolved.

    Public and religious roles

    County-site commissioner

    Lowndes County was created December 23, 1825. A separate organizing act approved December 24 appointed Lawrence Folsom, Sion Hall, William Blair, John J. Underwood, and Daniel McCranie to select a public site for the new county. Elections and courts were to remain at Sion Hall's house until a site was chosen.[15]

    This appointment shows that Lawrence was an established public figure by the county's creation. No reviewed source shows that he later served as sheriff, clerk, Inferior Court justice, legislator, tax officer, or county surveyor.

    Mount Zion

    Hamilton W. Sharpe's 1884 reminiscence names Lawrence among the seven first tenters at Mount Zion, with William Blair, William Hendry, John Hendry, Rev. Joshua Platt, Sharpe, and John Pike. The newspaper OCR reads the first meeting's year as 1829, while Huxford's later transcription reads 1828. The original image needs a close visual read before selecting between them.[18]

    Lawrence's trusteeship is firmer. Huxford printed a transcription he said he made from the original November 8, 1831 deed. Sion and Enoch Hall conveyed about six acres on Land Lot 271, including the existing camps, church, and burying ground, to Methodist Episcopal trustees William Blair, William Hendry, Lawrence Folsom, James J. Joyce, and William G. Hall. The title reverted if the property ceased to be used for worship. The county copy was lost in the courthouse fire and was never re-recorded.[15,19]

    Finding: verified through Huxford's original-based transcription that Lawrence was a Mount Zion trustee in 1831; strong for his participation among the first tenters, with the exact year unresolved.

    Militia and community logistics

    A letter written by Lasa Adams in 1893 and published in the Valdosta Times in 1895 recalled 1836 militia operations near Little River. Adams said men were sent to tell Capt. Jonathan C. Hawthorn to come down and draw supplies at Lawrence Folsom's place.[15] The account shows that the farm was established and capable of provisioning militia in the region. It does not prove that Lawrence held military command or a formal quartermaster office.

    A separate later claim says Lawrence was commissioned an ensign in the Burke County militia on January 23, 1799.[7] The underlying commission was not located. If correct, this was peacetime Georgia militia service. No War of 1812 record, pension, or bounty-land file for Lawrence was found, so he should not be described as a War of 1812 veteran.

    Death, estate, and burial

    The 1938 genealogy gives Lawrence's death as April 11, 1842, says he left his farm to youngest son James, and places both men in a family burial lot on the farm. These statements are attributed to family informant Mrs. Boyd and lack a will-book citation or marker transcription.[8]

    A FamilySearch collaborative-profile note reportedly says Lawrence's will was probated in 1850 with William Folsom as executor. No will, probate index entry, letters testamentary, inventory, appraisal, return, or executor deed was located online. The eight-year gap after the stated death and proximity to Rachel's reported 1849 death make the story possible but not record-proved.[9]

    The missing estate record may not survive, but this investigation did not locate a record-loss inventory establishing what happened to Lawrence's specific probate papers. Until a private copy, later deed recital, loose file, or church paper is found, statements about the devise must remain attributed to the 1938 genealogy.

    Find a Grave memorial 64843909 gives 1772-April 11, 1842 and cites Old Folsom Family Cemetery near Hahira.[10] The accessible memorial text did not expose a marker image. The cemetery name and date are useful derivative evidence, not original grave proof.

    Conflicts and cautions

    1. Birth: sources range from about 1772 to about 1775 and conflict between Virginia, Burke County, Georgia, and North Carolina.[4,8-10]
    2. Middle name: Armstrong appears in later histories, not in the reviewed contemporary records.[1,11,15,19]
    3. Settlement date: the 1913 history gives an 1815 exploratory trip; the 1922 history gives an 1821 family move; neither has been verified from an original record.[4-5]
    4. 1899 contradiction: R. E. L. Folsom placed Lawrence after Washington Joyce's 1824 settlement, while Lawrence's deed is dated 1823.[1,14]
    5. Children: the 1913 and 1938 lists differ, and collaborative trees add more conflicts.[4,8-9]
    6. Tax years: the public KML lists Lawrence on Lot 182 through 1840 but on Lot 187 only through 1838; the digest images remain unverified.[16]
    7. Estate: no original will or probate file was found, and the fate of Lawrence's specific probate papers is unknown.[8-9]
    8. Burial: the family-lot and Old Folsom Family Cemetery descriptions may refer to the same place, but no photographed marker or contemporary burial register was reviewed.[8,10]
    9. Slavery: the 1830 transcription omits household-level enslaved-person columns. This dossier does not assign Lawrence a specific number without the original schedule or tax image.[11-12]

    Research coverage and negative findings

    This investigation searched stored Barney, Folsom Bridge, Coffee Road, Mount Zion, road, genealogy, census, and newspaper evidence before broader online sources. The name forms included "Lawrence Folsom", "Laurence Folsom", "L. Folsom", and "Lawrence Armstrong Folsom", with Rachel Vickers, the proposed children, Lots 182/187, commissioner, trustee, militia, will, probate, cemetery, and first-settler terms.

    The online source ladder included original FamilySearch deed images, NARA's 1830 census roll, Georgia Archives custody records, official Georgia land-lot data, Internet Archive books and OCR, Genealogy Trails, Newspapers.com image IDs, Find a Grave, FamilySearch public and previously reviewed signed-in profile data, Ray City History Blog, Georgia Historical Society, and public catalog records.

    The managed logged-in browser timed out twice while attempting fresh Ancestry access; under the anti-loop rule the source was not retried. Existing project notes from prior signed-in FamilySearch and Ancestry work were used only with that provenance disclosed. The underlying Hortense W. Baker newspaper sketch, the original 1799 militia commission, exact 1830 census image, original Mount Zion manuscript, 1895 Lasa Adams page ID, Lawrence's will/probate papers, and a photographed grave marker were not located. These are access or survival gaps, not proof that the records never existed.

    Citation audit note

    Original records, contemporary reports, later recollections, compiled genealogy, collaborative profiles, and modern memorials are labeled separately. Later sources do not become independent proof merely because they repeat the same tradition. Conflicts in the arrival chronology, birth, children, tax years, estate, and burial remain visible rather than being silently harmonized.
