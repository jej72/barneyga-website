    Francis Rountree of early Lowndes County

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Research completed: August 19, 2026
    Geographic scope: original Irwin and early Lowndes counties, with Brooks County sources used for retrospective context, and a possible later migration to Madison/Taylor counties, Florida
    Controlled name: Francis Rountree
    Variants searched: Francis, Frances, and Frank; Rountree, Roundtree, Rowntree, Rauntree, and OCR forms including Kowntree

    Conclusion

    Francis Rountree was a white male landholder and enslaver in early Lowndes County. His 1840 household included five people employed in agriculture, but the schedule does not assign Francis an occupation.[5][7][8][9][10][11] Georgia's 1837 official digest says the county's temporary court-and-election place was moved from Sion Hall's house to Francis Roundtree's house in 1826. Nineteenth-century recollections also place an early Superior Court term there, on property later known as part of Remer Young's farm. The official digest proves the statutory designation; actual court use rests on the retrospective accounts. The evidence does not settle which early session should be called the first Lowndes Superior Court.[1][2][3][4][12][13]

    The original 1830 Lowndes census and tax records, followed by tax and census records through 1840, establish a strong continuous Lowndes identity. The household head aged from 30-39 in 1830 to 40-49 in 1840; the adult woman's age band and the younger females' age progression also fit. Tax digests place him in the districts of Captains Folsom, Godwin, Caswell, and Varner and tie him to Lowndes District 12 land lots 36, 56, 82, and 83. The records use Francis, Frances, Roundtree, Rountree, and Rowntree. Those are spelling variants, not evidence of different sexes or people.[5][6][7][8][9][10][11]

    Folks Huxford's 1948 county history reports that Francis was commissioned second lieutenant in the 658th Militia District on January 17, 1827, and captain on August 9, 1827. The dates and office sequence are specific, but no original commission-book image was located online. The militia claim is therefore strong secondary evidence, not newly verified original evidence. A different index names a Francis Roundtree as a private in Whitfield's Detachment during the Florida War; nothing in that index identifies him as the Lowndes man, and it cannot be used to prove the 1827 offices.[4][18]

    His origins, wife, named children, relationship to pioneer James Rountree, exact death, burial, and estate remain unresolved. A possible record chain continues to a Francis and Rebecca Roundtree in Madison County, Florida, in 1850 and to a 66-year-old Francis Roundtree recorded in Taylor County's 1860 mortality schedule as having died in December during the preceding census year, probably December 1859. The name, age-band compatibility, border geography, and household/property pattern offer a plausible match, but no located deed, probate record, or named family document bridges Lowndes to Florida. The candidate ages also conflict: fifty in 1850 versus sixty-six about nine years later. That continuation is possible, not strong probable or verified.[15][16][17]

    Identity and names

    The controlling 1830 record is the Lowndes County household on NARA roll 19, printed page 82, Ancestry record 1850862. Ancestry indexes the handwritten entry as "Frances Kowntree [Frances Rowntree]"; the manuscript surname is difficult, but the row is the local Rountree household. A separate 1830 record for Francis Rountree in Youngs Valley, Talbot County, is a different man and is excluded from this dossier.[5][20]

    The records do not support one rigid original spelling:

    • Georgia's 1837 digest prints "Francis Roundtree".[1]
    • The 1830 Lowndes census index reads "Frances ... Rowntree", while the manuscript is ambiguous.[5]
    • The 1830 tax index reads "Francis Roundtree".[6]
    • The 1834 and 1835 tax indexes read "Francis Rountree".[7][8]
    • The 1838 and 1840 tax indexes use "Frances Rowntree" or "Frances Rountree".[9][10]
    • The 1840 census index reads "Francis Rountree".[11]

    This dossier uses Francis Rountree as an editorial control name because Francis is the dominant male given-name form in the official digest and census/tax chain. "Frances" must remain in the search ledger because clerks and indexers used it.

    Several same-name records were rejected or separated:

    • The Talbot County household in 1830 had an adult couple, two boys, and one enslaved girl. It is not the Lowndes household, which had three girls and four enslaved people.[5][20]
    • A user-contributed Ancestry tree places a Francis Rountree born in Burke County in 1787, married to Henrietta Lester, and dead in Lowndes in 1836 while also attaching a Talbot residence. The death claim is contradicted for this subject by his original 1840 Lowndes census and tax records. The tree is a lead, not evidence.[19]
    • Later Francis/Frances/Frank Rountrees in Georgia newspapers, censuses, and cemeteries were not merged with the subject without a dated bridge.

    Court and election site

    Lowndes County was created from Irwin County in December 1825. Oliver H. Prince's official 1837 digest gives the controlling legal summary on printed page 955:

    "Courts and elections to be held at the house of Sion Hall, 1825 ... Removed to the house of Francis Roundtree, 1826."[1]

    A December 5, 1826 legislative report confirms passage of a bill amending the organizing act concerning the holding of courts and elections at Sion Hall's. That report corroborates the change but does not name the destination; Prince's 1837 digest supplies Francis Roundtree's house. Neither source says that Francis was a judge, justice, clerk, commissioner, or lawyer.[1][2]

    The session chronology is less certain than the legal designation:

    • Hamilton W. Sharpe wrote in 1884 that the first temporary organization and election took place at Hall's residence and that at least one Superior Court term later met at Francis Rountree's.[3]
    • Huxford reprinted William Smith's December 1880 recollection. Smith placed the first Superior Court at Sion Hall's on the first Monday in January 1827 and the next at Rountree's house in early 1828. He located Rountree's house in the old part of Remer Young's farm and said Rountree then owned the place.[4]
    • R. E. L. Folsom's 1899 historical sketch assigned the first Lowndes court to Rountree's place in 1827. A 1900 sketch and later reprints repeated related versions.[12][13][14]

    These accounts were written decades after the events and disagree about date and sequence. The safest conclusion is:

    Verified: Francis Rountree's house became the legally designated Lowndes court-and-election place in 1826.
    Strong probable: an early Superior Court term was actually held there.
    Unresolved: whether that term occurred in 1827 or early 1828 and whether it should be called the county's first or second Superior Court.

    No online deed was found tying the site to Remer Young or supplying a land lot for the house. "Remer Young's farm" is an important retrospective location clue, not a proved title chain.

    Militia service

    Huxford's officer table for the 658th Georgia Militia District lists:

    • "Francis Rountree, Jan. 17, 1827" among second lieutenants;
    • "Francis Rountree, Aug. 9, 1827" among captains.[4]

    The sequence is plausible and the dates are exact enough to suggest that Huxford worked from commission records, but the book does not reproduce the commissions on the cited page. No original Georgia militia commission, election return, muster roll, or pay roll was found online in this project. The defensible grade is strong secondary evidence.

    An Ancestry/NARA index separately lists "Francis Roundtree", private, Whitfield's Detachment, Georgia Militia, Florida War. It gives no residence, age, family, service dates, or county. It is possible that this was the same man, but the index does not prove it. It also does not corroborate the earlier captaincy because it names the soldier as a private in a different unit and context.[18]

    Household and slavery

    1830 Lowndes census

    The original schedule records a nine-person household:[5]

    • one white male aged 30-39;
    • one white female aged 30-39;
    • three white girls: one under five, one aged 5-9, and one aged 10-14;
    • four enslaved people: one boy under ten, two girls under ten, and one woman aged 24-35.

    The census names only the household head. It does not identify the adult woman as his wife, name the three girls as daughters, or name any enslaved person.

    1840 Lowndes census

    The 1840 schedule records twenty people:[11]

    • one white male aged 40-49;
    • one white female aged 40-49;
    • three white girls: one under five, one aged 10-14, and one aged 15-19;
    • fifteen enslaved people: two boys under ten, one man aged 36-54, seven girls under ten, three females aged 10-23, one woman aged 24-35, and one woman aged 36-54;
    • five people employed in agriculture;
    • one white person over twenty reported unable to read and write.

    The age progression strongly supports the same Lowndes household head in 1830 and 1840. It does not establish the identity or relationship of any other person.

    Tax snapshots

    Original tax-page images were inspected. They record separate annual snapshots, not a continuous biography of the enslaved people or proof that the same individuals remained in the household:

    Tax year District label Polls Enslaved people on original page Land evidence
    1830 Captain Folsom 1 blank or not legible; the index's 7 is not accepted over the page one visible District 12 Lowndes entry associated with Lot 56; columns require cautious reading
    1834 Captain Godwin 1 10 Lowndes District 12, Lots 56, 83, 82, and 36
    1835 Captain Caswell 1 11 same four district/lot entries
    1838 Captain Caswell 1 14 same four Lowndes lots plus an ambiguous fifth line that appears to concern 100 acres in Bulloch County
    1840 Captain Varner 1 15 same four district/lot entries

    The land columns use acreage/class or quality subdivisions that should not be flattened into simple total-acre figures. The legible raw tuples are:

    • 1834 and 1835 tax-digest entries were reviewed for the reported tracts.
    • The 1838 digest contains four relevant tract entries and an ambiguous Bulloch County line.
    • 1840: the 1834/1835 four-lot pattern returns.[7][8][9][10]

    The 1834 page associates the four tracts with grantee names W. B. Lester, C. Crimm, J. Roberts, and J. Stafford and identifies Little River. Those fields help distinguish the property rows but do not prove that Rountree received the land directly from those people.

    The difference between the 1840 tax total of fifteen enslaved people and the population-census total of fifteen is consistent. Earlier changes from four people in the 1830 census to ten, eleven, and fourteen in later tax years cannot be turned into a purchase history. No names, transfers, deaths, sales, hires, family connections, or individual continuity are shown.

    Land and economic position

    The tax chain proves substantial taxable interests in four District 12 Lowndes lots by 1834 and through 1840. It does not by itself prove that his residence stood on any one of them. The court-house site might have been on one of these tracts, on property held under a different title, or at the place later remembered as Remer Young's farm. A deed chain is needed before assigning the house to a particular lot.[7][8][9][10]

    The household and tax records show that Rountree farmed within an economy built on enslaved labor. The 1840 census reports five people employed in agriculture but does not identify which free or enslaved household members were counted as workers.[11]

    Family and relationship to James Rountree

    No located original record names Francis's wife, parents, children, or siblings. The adult woman in the 1830 and 1840 households was probably his wife, but the censuses do not state the relationship or her name.[5][11]

    Huxford's main text says sisters Harriet and Nancy Rountree, wives of brothers James and John McMullen, were daughters of Francis. Huxford's own corrections section reverses that statement and says they were daughters of James Rountree and Nancy Hendley. The correction controls the book's earlier claim. Harriet and Nancy therefore cannot be assigned to Francis from Huxford.[4]

    No deed, probate record, Bible record, obituary, or contemporary family notice was found proving that Francis was the brother, son, cousin, or other relative of pioneer James Rountree, who died in 1834. The men shared a surname and lived in early Lowndes County; that is not kinship proof. A prior local-history sentence calling Francis James's brother is unsupported and should not be repeated.

    The user-contributed Ancestry tree claim that Francis married Henrietta Lester is also unproved and appears entangled with the Talbot County namesake and the contradicted 1836 death claim.[19][20]

    Possible move to Florida and death

    An 1850 Madison County, Florida, census record identifies Francis Roundtree, age fifty, Georgia-born farmer, with $1,000 in real estate, and Rebecca Roundtree, also age fifty and Georgia-born. The corresponding slave schedule lists twenty-one enslaved people under Francis's name.[15][16]

    An indexed 1860 mortality-schedule entry records Francis Roundtree, age sixty-six, Georgia-born farmer, dying in Taylor County, Florida, in December. Mortality schedules for the 1860 census covered the year ending June 1, 1860, so this probably means December 1859, not December 1860. The original schedule was not inspected.[17]

    This is a plausible candidate continuation because:

    • the 1850 age is compatible with the 1840 Lowndes 40-49 band, but the mortality index's age sixty-six about nine years later is a material conflict;
    • the name, adult-couple household, border geography, and broad property pattern are compatible;
    • Madison and Taylor counties lie just south of the Georgia border;
    • the enslaved-property scale is broadly compatible with the 1840 Lowndes record.

    The chain is not verified. No located Lowndes sale deed, Florida purchase deed, tax roll, probate file, migration notice, or named relative links the Georgia and Florida households. Rebecca's relationship is indexed as inferred, not stated on the census. The 1860 mortality entry is itself a derivative index; the original mortality schedule was not inspected. Therefore:

    Possible: the Lowndes man was the Francis Roundtree in Madison County in 1850 and the Francis recorded as dying in Taylor County in December during the year ending June 1, 1860, probably December 1859.
    Unresolved: Rebecca's maiden name, marriage, children, exact death day and cause, burial, estate, and any heirs.

    Online research coverage and limits

    The investigation began with stored Lowndes, Brooks, Rountree, Sion Hall, William Blair, John Pike, census, newspaper, and local-book evidence. It then searched authenticated Ancestry census, tax, marriage, probate, military, mortality, slave-schedule, and cemetery collections; Newspapers.com; Georgia Historic Newspapers; Internet Archive; Huxford's county history; the 1837 Georgia law digest; FamilySearch/public genealogy leads; Find a Grave; and general web results.

    Searches used all controlled name variants with Lowndes, Irwin, Brooks, Burke, Talbot, Madison, Taylor, Remer Young, Sion Hall, first court, Superior Court, militia, captain, lieutenant, Florida War, Lot 56, Withlacoochee, Little River, wife, child, heir, marriage, probate, estate, administrator, death, burial, and cemetery. Newspapers.com searches covered 1800-1950. Georgia Historic Newspapers searches covered the available corpus with the same core variants. Results were adjudicated against place, age, household, date, and occupation before use.

    No pre-1850 contemporary newspaper item naming the Lowndes Francis was found in the documented full-text searches. Useful newspaper accounts begin with retrospective county histories in 1884 and 1899; later reprints are not independent proof. OCR limitations remain, especially for "Frances", "Rowntree", initials, damaged columns, and unindexed legal notices.

    "Fully researched online" means the accessible online collections and known leads were searched and reconciled. It does not mean undigitized courthouse and archival records were examined. The following remain unavailable or uninspected:

    • original 1825 and 1826 session-law pages behind the 1837 digest references;
    • original Lowndes Superior Court minutes or docket for 1826-1828;
    • original 1827 Georgia militia commission entries;
    • complete Lowndes deed indexes and books for the four District 12 lots and Remer Young chain;
    • Lowndes ordinary minutes, wills, bonds, inventories, sales, returns, and loose estates;
    • original Florida deeds, tax rolls, 1860 mortality schedule, probate, and cemetery records for the candidate continuation;
    • any family Bible, church record, or direct record naming Francis's wife, children, or relationship to James.

    Best next archival work

    1. Obtain the exact 1825 and 1826 Georgia session laws cited as volume IV, pages 128, 134, and 237; transcribe the full language and effective dates.
    2. Search surviving Lowndes Superior Court dockets, minutes, writs, jury lists, and clerk papers for 1826-1828 and the James D. Shanks perjury case.
    3. Request Georgia militia commission-book images for Francis Rountree's January 17 and August 9, 1827 entries in the 658th District.
    4. Trace District 12 Lots 36, 56, 82, and 83 through Irwin/Lowndes/Brooks deeds, tax digests, plats, grants, executions, and later conveyances; separately trace Remer Young's title.
    5. Search Lowndes probate volumes and loose papers after 1840 under every surname variant, then repeat in Madison and Taylor counties, Florida, through 1865.
    6. Inspect the original 1850 Madison population and slave schedules and the 1860 Taylor mortality schedule; search adjoining households and deeds for Rebecca and named relatives.
    7. Review cemetery surveys and marker photographs in southern Georgia and Madison/Taylor counties for a matching Francis and Rebecca.
    8. Search deeds, probate inventories, bills of sale, church records, and post-emancipation records for names and family continuity of people enslaved by the household.

    Preservation and citation audit

    The project preserves original images for the 1830 and 1840 Lowndes censuses, the 1830/1834/1835/1838/1840 tax pages, and the correct printed page 955 of Prince's 1837 digest. The initially downloaded Prince image was discovered to be printed page 986; it was moved to rejected-evidence and replaced before drafting. The Talbot County 1830 record is cited only as an excluded namesake.

    The newspaper pages, Huxford pages, 1850 Florida population and slave schedules, 1860 mortality schedule, and military index card are not preserved in this evidence folder. Their links and record IDs are retained, but subscription access or another repository may be needed to reopen them. This is a real preservation gap, not a complete offline source set.

    All narrative claims distinguish original/contemporary evidence, retrospective accounts, indexes, trees, and unresolved inferences. The exact first-court date, original militia commissions, family, James kinship, Florida continuity, death, burial, and probate remain open rather than being filled by assumption.
