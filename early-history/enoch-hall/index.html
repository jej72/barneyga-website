    Enoch Hall of old Lowndes County, Georgia

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Evidence-graded online dossier

    Subject: Enoch Hall, son of Sion Hall; early resident of original Irwin/Lowndes County and later Brooks County, Georgia
    Life dates: November 11, 1804–September 2, 1886, strongly supported by his photographed marker and Folks Huxford's county history; Telfair County as the birthplace remains a later family/local-history tradition
    Prepared: August 18, 2026
    Standard: Original and contemporary records control. Later recollections, cemetery pages, and family traditions are labeled and never counted as independent proof merely because they were reprinted.

    Bottom line

    Enoch Hall was a farmer, mill-associated landholder, county official, state representative, and militia commander in the Morven neighborhood. His public career is much clearer than the family legends make it seem. Huxford's commission-based tables place him on the Lowndes Inferior Court from 1832 through 1837, in the Georgia House in 1838, as a militia major in 1834, and as colonel of the 81st Regiment in 1838. A preserved 1836 general-militia return identifies him as lieutenant colonel commanding the 2nd Battalion of the 81st Regiment. Hall's own July 14, 1838 letter to Governor George R. Gilmer verifies his later colonelcy directly: he signed as colonel commanding the regiment, reported raising four volunteer companies, and described plans to guard Lowndes settlements and move against Indians believed to be in the Okefenokee Swamp.[1][2][3][33]

    The documentary record also ties Enoch and his father Sion to Land Lot 271. A transcription made by Huxford from the original 1831 Mount Zion deed names both men as grantors, while contemporary 1845–1846 sheriff-sale notices describe their interest in the lot and working saw and grist mills there.[2][7][8][9]

    Several later stories do not survive comparison with the official chronology. Enoch did not introduce the 1858 bill creating Brooks County; Senator James West introduced and handled it. Enoch's supported legislative term was for Lowndes County in 1838, not as Brooks County's second representative. No located record proves that he was a Mexican War colonel. A Civil War soldiers index does contain a same-name "Enoch Hall" in the 11th Georgia Cavalry (State Guards), but it gives no age, residence, company, or rank with which to identify him as the older Brooks County man. His militia title was documented before the Mexican War. The Confederate claim remains possible but unproved.[25][28][29][36]

    Identity and chronology

    This dossier concerns the Enoch Hall born in Georgia about 1804–1808, living in Lowndes County by 1840 and in the same neighborhood after it became part of Brooks County in 1858. The uncommon name, place continuity, census households, 1865 marriage, and 1886 estate bond form one reliable record chain.[10–14][19][20]

    He must not be merged with Enoch Hall Pike, born in 1836; Enoch H. Hall, the son born about 1878; later Brooks County men named Enoch Hall; or an Enoch F. Hall returned in Civil War indexes. None of those identities supplies military service for the older man.[2][14][18]

    Birth, parents, and arrival

    The federal schedules vary from about 1805 to 1808. Huxford gives November 11, 1804 in Telfair County, and Enoch's weathered marker bears the same date. The agreement is strong, but no contemporary birth, baptism, Bible, or parental probate record was found online.[2][10–14][21]

    Montgomery M. Folsom's memorial sketch, published within weeks of Enoch's death, calls him "the son of Sion Hall". Huxford identifies his mother as Bridget or "Beady" Hall. The 1831 joint deed, Lot 271 notices, adjacent 1840 households, and later testimony reinforce the Sion relationship. No birth or probate record directly names either parent. The publication-safe judgment is strong probable that Sion was his father; moderate that Bridget/Beady was his mother.[2–4][7–10]

    The move date cannot be fixed to one year. Huxford says Enoch came with Sion in 1822 or 1823. Widow Hannah told a reporter in 1928 that he came in 1824 at age twenty. An 1820 Irwin County census index has the likely Sion household under the OCR form "Dian Hall", but that aggregate schedule does not name Enoch or locate the household within the enormous county. The safest conclusion is that Enoch was in the Coffee Road/Morven settlement by 1823 or 1824; the exact sequence remains unresolved.[2][26][31][34]

    Coffee Road claim

    Huxford, the 1924 centennial account, and later retellings call Enoch an overseer in laying out the Coffee Road. No original contract, road return, pay record, or court order naming him was located. The 1822 legislation appointed John Coffee and Thomas Swain to superintend the state road. A separate July 1824 Irwin Inferior Court transcription names Sion Hall, James Allen, and Thomas Townsend to lay out a road from the Ocmulgee River to the Alapaha River; it does not name Enoch.[2][25]

    Enoch may have assisted on a crew or segment without appearing in the surviving summaries, but that is not proved. Coffee Road overseer is a longstanding secondary tradition, not a verified office. The 1824 Ocmulgee-to-Alapaha order may concern a northern connector to the broader Coffee Road route, but its exact relationship to the state road remains unresolved.

    Land Lot 271, Mount Zion, the mills, and the Hall place

    The property evidence is cumulative:

    1. Huxford printed a transcription he said he made from the original November 8, 1831 deed. Sion and Enoch Hall conveyed roughly six acres associated with the existing church, camps, and burying ground to Methodist Episcopal trustees. The description begins at the west north-south line of Lot 271, District 12. The title reverted to the Halls if the land ceased to be used for worship. The destroyed Lowndes Deed Book A copy had been recorded at pages 654–655 in 1833.[2][7]
    2. An April 8, 1845 sheriff-sale notice levied Lot 271 as the property of Sion and Enoch Hall in the judgment "Drury Vickers vs Enoch Hall, Sion Hall and John Hall, securities on stay".[8]
    3. A March 10, 1846 repeat called the lot well improved and described a saw and grist mill in operation.[9]
    4. Hamilton W. Sharpe wrote in 1884 that Sion's place was occupied by his son Col. Enoch Hall, then over eighty, on Lot 271.[5]

    This proves a Hall legal interest in Lot 271 and working mills on the levied property. It does not establish who built either mill, the final purchaser after levy, the full later title chain, or the exact location of the house. In 1902 Norman Campbell, an early resident, corrected a published account and placed the residence itself in adjacent Lot 270.[35] The property cluster is clear; the precise house parcel remains unsettled.

    Folsom's 1886 articles associate Enoch closely with the mill and portray him talking politics and telling stories while customers waited for their meal. Huxford says he continued Sion's travelers' inn for a time. Those descriptions are plausible and locally specific, but no mill ledger, inn license, guest register, or advertisement was located. Mill association is strong; personal daily operation and the inn are secondary-supported.[2–6]

    The 1844 Lowndes tax digest also indexes Enoch in Captain Blair's District. The entry confirms taxation and residence but does not, by itself, identify Lot 271 or transcribe his entire assessment.[17]

    County office and legislative service

    Huxford's officer tables identify Enoch as a Lowndes Inferior Court justice commissioned January 18, 1832, recommissioned January 17, 1833, and serving through 1837. The same roster identifies him as Lowndes County's representative in the House for 1838.[2] Folsom wrote shortly after Enoch's death that his neighbors sent him to the legislature, without giving the county or year.[3][4]

    Taken together, these are strong evidence of real public service. The underlying commission book, 1838 election return, and House journal page were not found online, so the precise table remains one step removed from the original record. The proper wording is: Huxford's commission-based roster identifies Hall as an Inferior Court justice from 1832 to 1837 and Lowndes representative in 1838; Folsom independently confirms the legislative tradition near Enoch's death.

    The Brooks County bill

    The May 1924 centennial article says Enoch followed Sion in the legislature and introduced the bill forming Brooks County. A 1933 Hannah Hall obituary stream changed the story again, calling Sion Brooks County's first representative and Enoch its second.[24][25]

    The official record contradicts both Brooks-specific versions:

    • Brooks County did not exist until December 11, 1858, nine years after Sion's death.
    • Senator James West introduced the Brooks County bill on November 4, 1858, moved its amendments, and obtained Senate passage. William A. Lane had drafted it after the Lowndes division convention.[28]
    • The 1859 official journals identify J. T. Edmondson in the House and Griffin of Brooks in the Senate, the first legislative session after the county's creation.[29]

    Enoch's supported legislative service was the separate 1838 Lowndes House term. No inspected source gives him a role in drafting, introducing, amending, or passing the Brooks bill. Private support cannot be excluded, but the sponsor claim is contradicted.

    Militia command and the Indian conflicts

    Huxford's tables give a promotion sequence: major of the 158th Battalion on September 3, 1834, then colonel of Lowndes County's 81st Regiment on May 21, 1838.[2] The digitized manuscript of a 1836 Lowndes general-militia return fills part of the interval. It identifies "Lieut Col Enoch Hall — Comd 2d Bat 81st Reg" under Colonel Henry Blair. Valdosta State's repository notes that the document was preserved through the Brooks County Historical Society and that these were general-militia returns, not active-service rosters.[33]

    The decisive original is Enoch's July 14, 1838 letter from Lowndes County to Governor George R. Gilmer. He wrote that he had raised four volunteer companies to keep a constant guard between the settlements and the Okefenokee Swamp. He believed Indians were gathering there, intended to move into the swamp once provisions could be obtained, and asked for instructions. He signed "Enoch Hall Col. Comd. 81 Regt G.M." The wrapper identifies the same date and sender.[1]

    That manuscript verifies his 1838 colonelcy, regiment, frontier command, and organization of four companies. The 1836 return separately verifies his lieutenant-colonel rank in the general militia. Neither item proves that he entered the swamp or fought at Brushy Creek.

    Brushy Creek and 1836

    Huxford says Enoch served through the 1836 and 1838 campaigns. Hannah Hall's 1928 interview preserves elaborate family stories about his role at Brushy Creek, an Indian-language ability, a warning of an attack, and a battlefield exchange with Hamilton Sharpe.[2][26] The interview is valuable oral history, but it dates Brushy Creek to 1826 instead of 1836 and gives Enoch a colonel's title two years before the documented commission.

    The official Brushy Creek report names John Pike, James A. Newman, Hamilton W. Sharpe, and Michael Young in command roles, not Enoch. The separate 1836 general-militia return proves that Enoch was then an 81st Regiment lieutenant colonel, but the repository description expressly warns that the return is not an active-service roster. Huxford says the campaign company rosters are lost. His 1836 general-militia command is strongly supported; active campaign service is secondary-supported; personal combat at Brushy Creek remains possible/traditional.[2][27][33]

    Mexican War and Civil War claims

    The 1924 centennial article calls Enoch a Mexican War colonel and says he enlisted in the Confederate army at age sixty.[25] The documented 1834–1838 Georgia militia progression already explains the title "Colonel"; the authenticated searches described below did not locate a Mexican War record tied to this man.

    Folsom's 1886 sketch says Enoch's sons Lyman and Boling went to the Civil War and that Boling returned with an injured leg; it does not say their father enlisted.[3][4] Huxford's Confederate roster names Lyman but not the elder Enoch.[2]

    An 1864 Georgia census for reorganizing the militia lists an Enoch Hall, age fifty-five, in Brooks County. The name and place make him the probable subject despite the low age. That census enumerated men available for home defense; it does not establish enlistment or active service.[18]

    The National Park Service-derived Civil War soldiers index adds an unresolved lead: an "Enoch Hall" served in the Confederate 11th Regiment, Georgia Cavalry (State Guards). The entry has no middle initial, but it also has no age, residence, company, or rank. A second result, "Enoch J. Hall" of the 13th Battalion Georgia Infantry (State Guards), is a distinct name. The exact-name 11th Regiment entry could be the elder Brooks County man, especially beside the 1864 home-defense census, but it does not establish identity.[36] Mexican War rank remains unverified. Confederate State Guard service is possible, not proved; the specific claim that Enoch enlisted at sixty still lacks an identifying service record.

    Wives and children

    Sarah or Sallie Self

    Huxford identifies Enoch's first wife as Sallie, daughter of Thomas Self, and their sons as Lyman and Bolen G. Hall. The 1850 schedule independently places Sarah, age thirty-four, with Enoch, Lyman, and Bolen. The 1860 schedule again places Sarah, age forty-six, in Enoch's household. "Sallie" is a familiar form of "Sarah", so the fit is close.[2][11][12]

    Huxford's smallpox account, drawn from Brooks Inferior Court expenses, says Sallie Hall, wife of Col. Enoch Hall, died during the 1860 epidemic and that the county paid for her coffin and medical treatment. The original minute image was not located. No marriage record, grave marker, memorial, or probate file for Sarah/Sallie was found. Sarah/Sallie as first wife is strong probable; Self surname and Thomas Self parentage are moderate; her exact death date and burial remain unresolved.[2]

    The additional Sarah Hall, twenty-three, and Nancy Hall, two, in the 1860 household must not be assigned automatically. The schedule gives no relationships. They could be Lyman's wife and child, but that remains unresolved.[12]

    Hannah Elizabeth Gornto

    The Brooks County marriage record identifies Enoch Hall and H. E. Gornto marrying November 23, 1865. The 1870 household includes Enoch, Hannah, Sion, and infant Joel. The 1880 schedule explicitly calls H. E. Hall Enoch's wife and identifies Sion, Joel, and Enoch H. as sons.[13][14][19]

    Hannah's 1933 death certificate names her as Hannah Elizabeth Hall, gives her father as Joel Gornto and mother as Mary Blair, and records burial in Hall Cemetery. The informant was her son J. L. Hall. Her contemporary obituary names the three surviving sons and repeats several family traditions assessed separately in this dossier.[23][24]

    The estate evidence is even better. On December 6, 1886, Hannah E. Hall executed an administration bond after being appointed administrator of Enoch Hall's estate. The manuscript proves the estate proceeding and Hannah's role; it is not a will and does not name heirs or distribute property.[20]

    Publication-safe child structure

    • Lyman Hall, born about 1835: in the 1850 and 1860 households; Huxford names him as a son of the first marriage. Strong probable son.[2][11][12]
    • Bolen/Bolin G. Hall, born about 1838: in the 1850 household; Huxford names him as the other first-marriage son. Strong probable son.[2][11]
    • Sion L. Hall, born about 1868: age two in Enoch and Hannah's 1870 household and explicitly a son in 1880. He later became a Barney merchant, the first recorded justice of the peace for the 1571st (Barney) District, and Barney's first mayor. Huxford's commission list begins the district with S. L. Hall on February 16, 1899, and a newspaper that November independently called him justice of the peace when his store, the post office inside it, and his legal papers burned. The mayoral evidence is explained below. Verified son; first recorded Barney-district justice of the peace and first mayor strongly supported.[2][13][14][37][38][39][40]
    • Joel L. Hall, born April 11, 1870: a two-month-old infant in 1870, explicitly a son in 1880, and living with mother Hannah in 1900. His Find a Grave page incorrectly says 1880, but the photographed marker reads April 11, 1870 and agrees with both censuses. Verified son.[13][14][22][32]
    • Enoch Hitch Hall, born about 1878: explicitly a son in 1880 and living with Hannah and Joel in 1900. Verified son.[14][22]

    The list is not complete. In 1900 Hannah reported six children born and three living. Sion, Joel, and Enoch Hitch were all alive. The natural reading is that three additional children, probably of her marriage to Enoch, had died before 1900 and remain unidentified; the census does not name their father. Huxford's three-son list therefore describes the identifiable or surviving sons, not every child Hannah bore.[22]

    Sion L. Hall is strongly supported as Barney's first mayor. The town charter, approved August 6, 1903, scheduled Barney's first municipal election for the first Wednesday in December 1903. Barely a month later, the January 8, 1904 Adel News reported: "Mayor S. L. Hall, of Barney, was a visitor to Adel Sunday." The original image reads S. L., although an earlier research index misread the first initial as J. A second notice on June 24 referred to "Mayor Hall of Barney." Neither notice calls Hall the first mayor or substitutes for the missing election return. Taken together, however, the charter and the two notices strongly support identifying him as the inaugural mayor. W. E. Hiers, elected in December 1905, was the next documented mayor rather than the first.[38][39][40][41]

    Farming, property, and enslaved people

    The federal records show Enoch's changing household economy:

    • 1840: three enslaved people: one male aged ten to twenty-three, one female under ten, and one female aged ten to twenty-three.[10]
    • 1850: farmer with $2,000 in real estate; slave schedule records a woman aged twenty and a boy aged one.[11][15]
    • 1860: farmer with $1,750 in personal property; slave schedule records a woman aged thirty and boys aged eleven and nine.[12][16]
    • 1870: farmer with $1,000 real estate and $610 personal estate.[13]
    • 1880: farmer, age seventy-five, recorded as blind.[14]

    The 1850 woman aged twenty and the 1860 woman aged thirty may be the same person. The 1850 infant boy may be the eleven-year-old in 1860. Those age progressions are suggestive, not proof. The schedules omit personal names. This dossier does not invent identities for the people Enoch enslaved or describe the population-schedule wealth figures as a complete estate valuation.

    Later life and character

    Sharpe described Enoch in 1884 as over eighty and still living on Lot 271.[5] Folsom visited the neighborhood in 1886 and wrote about the old Hall house, well, mill site, Enoch's declining sight, and his habit of recognizing friends by voice. His posthumous sketch remembers a high voice, political talk, practical jokes, and phrases that became local sayings.[3][4][6]

    These details are valuable as a near-contemporary portrait, but their exact source is not always stated. They should remain Folsom's recollections rather than be rewritten as documentary facts. Hannah's 1928 interview likewise records family relics she attributed to Enoch and Sion; the reporter saw the objects, but their age and chain of ownership rest on Hannah's testimony.[26]

    Death, estate, and burial

    Enoch's photographed Hall Cemetery marker reads "COLONEL ENOCH HALL / BORN NOV. 11, 1804 / DIED SEP. 2, 1886". Huxford independently gives the same dates and says Enoch died at home near Morven. The December 6 administration bond is consistent with a September death.[2][20][21]

    Two September 14 statewide briefs say he died "Thursday last", which, read literally on that publication date, would mean September 9.[30] The phrase may have been copied unchanged from an earlier weekly or local report. The photographed inscription plus Huxford's exact agreement outweigh the stale relative wording, but the conflict should remain visible. The marker inscription is verified; November 11, 1804–September 2, 1886 as the actual dates is strong probable. Death near Morven and burial in Hall Cemetery are also strong probable.

    No will, inventory, appraisal, year's support, estate sale, heir petition, distribution, or final settlement was found in the authenticated Georgia Wills and Probate Records search (Ancestry collection 8635) under Enoch Hall, death 1886, Brooks County, or in the opened record's linked material. Hannah's bond proves that an estate proceeding existed, not that the surviving online packet is complete.

    Claims graded

    Verified or strongly supported

    • One Enoch Hall remained in the same Lowndes/Brooks neighborhood from 1840 through 1880.[10–14]
    • Sion was his father, on the combined joint-property and near-contemporary biographical evidence.[3][7–9]
    • He held an interest in Lot 271 and was associated with operating saw and grist mills.[7–9]
    • Huxford's official rosters identify him as a Lowndes Inferior Court justice and Lowndes representative in 1838; Folsom independently supports the legislative service, but not the court office.[2–4]
    • A preserved 1836 manuscript return identifies him as lieutenant colonel commanding the 2nd Battalion of the 81st Regiment; his 1838 letter proves his later colonelcy and organization of four volunteer companies.[1][33]
    • He married Hannah E. Gornto in 1865, and she administered his estate in 1886.[19][20]
    • He enslaved people in 1840, 1850, and 1860.[10][15][16]

    Probable or traditional, with limits

    • November 11, 1804 as the birth date and September 2, 1886 as the death date near Morven.[2][21][30]
    • Telfair County as the birthplace, based on Huxford and later family tradition rather than a contemporary birth record.[2][26]
    • Bridget/Beady Hall as his mother.[2]
    • Sarah/Sallie Self as first wife and Thomas Self as her father.[2][11][12]
    • Active service in the 1836 regional Indian campaign is secondary-supported; personal combat at Brushy Creek remains possible/traditional.[2][26][27][33]
    • Work as a daily miller, continuance of Sion's inn, and the exact route/date of his move.[2–6]

    Unproved or contradicted

    • Coffee Road overseer: repeated later, but no original road record names Enoch.
    • Brooks County bill sponsor: contradicted; James West introduced and carried the 1858 bill.[28]
    • First or second Brooks County representative: contradicted by chronology and the 1859 journals.[29]
    • Mexican War colonel: not found in the documented searches; the title is documented in Georgia militia before the Mexican War, and the failed collection probe is disclosed below.
    • Confederate enlistment at sixty: a same-name 11th Georgia Cavalry (State Guards) index entry is possible but lacks identifying details; the 1864 militia census proves enumeration for home defense, not enlistment.[18][36]
    • Complete child list: contradicted by Hannah's report of six births when only three second-family sons have been identified.[22]

    Online research coverage and limits

    Local evidence was searched first across the Sion Hall dossier, early-settlement reports, Barney household work, retained Huxford text, memory files, newspaper result sets, deed/source extracts, and prior user corrections.

    Authenticated Ancestry searches covered the 1830–1900 federal censuses, 1850 and 1860 slave schedules, Georgia marriage records, the 1844 tax digest, 1864 militia-reorganization census, Georgia probate records, and Georgia death records. The 1840–1880 population chain, 1865 marriage, and 1886 administration bond carry exact collection, record, image, and page references below. The 1870 manuscript image was reopened: it shows Enoch, Hannah, Sion/Lion, and infant Joel together; the 1870 schedule itself records no relationships, so the 1865 marriage and 1880 relationship fields control the family interpretation.[13][14][19]

    Newspapers.com searches used "Enoch Hall", "E. Hall", "Col. Enoch Hall", "Colonel Enoch Hall", and combinations with Lowndes, Brooks, Morven, legislature, died, estate, Hannah, Gornto, Lyman, Boling/Bolling, Indian, militia, Mexican, and Confederate across authenticated Georgia coverage from 1800 to 1950. Georgia Historic Newspapers supplied the 1845–1846 sheriff-sale notices. The important located newspaper streams were Sharpe's 1884 recollection; Folsom's July and September 1886 articles and the September death briefs; the 1924 centennial; the 1928 Hannah interview; and Edna Cain Daniel's 1933 Hannah obituary and reprints.

    The Georgia Archives File II name collection produced Hall's four-image July 14, 1838 letter. Valdosta State's South Georgia Folklife Collection supplied the digitized 1836 Lowndes general-militia return; its repository description and roster page were inspected.[33] Georgia Archives colonial/headright plat indexes also return an older Enoch Hall in Montgomery County in 1799, 1801, and 1803; those records predate the subject's birth and were excluded rather than folded into his land history.

    Military searches were rerun on August 19, 2026, through authenticated Ancestry. Exact/broad searches used "Enoch Hall", birth 1804 Georgia, residence Brooks/Georgia, and the keywords "war", "Mexican War", and "Confederate". The U.S. Civil War Soldiers collection (1138) was then searched directly for Enoch Hall in Georgia; records 1816806 and 1795992 were opened. The first is the unresolved exact-name 11th Georgia Cavalry State Guards entry; the second is "Enoch J. Hall". The Civil War Pension Index (4654) was searched for Enoch Hall with Georgia residence and returned no candidate tied to Brooks County. The dedicated military-category URL and attempted Mexican War collection probe returned Ancestry page-not-found errors, so no claim of an exhaustive Mexican War collection search is made. Newspaper searches covered "Mexican", "Mexican War", "Confederate", "enlisted", and "age sixty" within the documented 1800–1950 Georgia search window. The safe conclusion is "not found in the documented search", not "no record exists".[36]

    The official 1858 Brooks County act, Senate passage sequence, and 1859 House/Senate journals were checked. The public FamilySearch profile and Find a Grave pages were used only as derivative leads unless a photographed marker controlled.

    "Fully researched online" here means the accessible online collections, saved subscription material, and identified leads were searched and reconciled. It does not mean every courthouse or manuscript record is digitized. The 1838 House journal/election return, original Inferior Court commissions and minutes, 1836 active-service muster/pay rolls, full Brooks estate packet, complete deed/tax chain, Hall family Bible, and named records for the people Enoch enslaved were not found online. Those are disclosed access gaps, not evidence that the records never existed.

    Best next archival work

    1. Obtain the 1832–1838 Georgia Executive Department commission entries, 1838 House journal/election return, and original Lowndes Inferior Court minutes.
    2. Search active-service pay rolls, muster rolls, requisitions, and governor correspondence for Enoch in 1834–1838, especially Brushy Creek and the four companies mentioned in his July 1838 letter.
    3. Browse Brooks County estate volumes and loose papers from September 1886 forward for inventory, appraisal, sale, annual returns, heir petitions, and final settlement.
    4. Build the complete deed and tax chain for Lots 270 and 271, including the result of the 1845–1846 levy and the later Hall succession.
    5. Locate Sarah/Sallie Hall's marriage, 1860 death entry, coffin-payment minute, and grave; seek records identifying the three deceased children Hannah reported in 1900.
    6. Use estate, deed, church, Freedmen's Bureau, and post-emancipation labor records to seek the names of people enslaved by the Hall household.
