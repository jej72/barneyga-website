    James Rountree

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Online research dossier

    Prepared: August 18, 2026
    Subject: James Rountree, early settler of southern Irwin and original Lowndes County, Georgia
    Research purpose: Identify the man later counted among Lowndes County's first four permanent white settlers; test that tradition against original records; and document his family, land, household, public role, death, estate, and connection to later Brooks County and Barney families.

    Bottom line

    James Rountree was an early South Georgia landowner whose record trail runs from Pulaski County into the portion of Irwin County that became original Lowndes County in 1825. An original 1820 census image places his household in Pulaski County. Reported deed-book citations say he bought Irwin County Land Lots 497 and 516, District 9, in 1821. The deed images were not available for fresh inspection, so those purchases remain precise, high-value leads rather than fully reverified facts.[1-4]

    The claim that Rountree, Lawrence Folsom, Drew Vickers, and Alfred Belote were Lowndes County's first four settlers is a genuine local-history tradition, but not a contemporary 1821 finding. The earliest fully inspectable source found that names all four is William Harden's 1913 history. It says they explored southern Irwin County in 1815 and later returned with their families as the first permanent white settlers of Lowndes, especially its northern portion. Folks Huxford repeated the quartet in 1922 and supplied an 1821 settlement date, relying chiefly on then-recent newspaper sketches by Hortense W. Baker that have not been located.[5-6]

    Earlier newspapers show that the tradition was already circulating. An 1888 Valdosta Times reminiscence called Rountree the builder of the first house in Lowndes County, while an 1890 article said an elderly formerly enslaved woman named Clara had come to the county with its first settlers as a person enslaved by him.[7-8] Those recollections strengthen the tradition's age, but they were published more than fifty years after Rountree's death. An 1899 county sketch gave a conflicting chronology, naming Washington Joyce's 1824 place as the first white settlement in present Lowndes County.[9]

    The careful conclusion is that James Rountree was one of four men repeatedly identified in old local histories as the first permanent Euro-American settler group of original Lowndes County. His presence in the region by the early 1820s is strongly supported, but the exact 1815 expedition and 1821 family migration remain unverified. “First permanent white settlers” is the later sources' terminology; it does not mean the land was previously uninhabited and should not erase the Native peoples who occupied South Georgia before American settlement.[5-6]

    James married Nancy Hendley in Pulaski County on March 6, 1823, as shown by the original license and return.[10-11] Contemporary newspapers establish that he was murdered on March 26, 1834, while camped on the Thomasville-Tallahassee road in Leon County, Florida. A Lowndes estate-administration notice followed in July.[12-17] His likely birth year, parents, first wife's identity, complete child list, final burial place, and estate distribution are not established by original records located online.

    Identity verdict

    The pioneer and his name forms

    The records use several spellings: James Rountree, Roundtree, Rowntree, and Rauntree. Place, date, associates, and family context—not spelling alone—control the identification. The subject of this dossier is the adult Pulaski County householder of 1820, Nancy Hendley's husband in 1823, the early Lowndes juror of 1833, and the Lowndes resident killed in Florida in 1834.[1,10-17]

    Later genealogies commonly give his life span as about 1787 to March 26, 1834 and call him a Burke County son of William F. Rountree and Rachel. No birth record, Bible entry, baptism, probate record, or parent-naming deed was found to verify the year, birthplace, or parents. The original 1830 census cannot settle his age because the entry long indexed as James is actually Jesse Townsend.[18-20]

    People who must not be merged with him

    • James Lester Rountree (1823-1905), usually identified as his son, lived long after the pioneer died.[21-22]
    • James Rountree Folsom (1831-1896) was a different man in the related Folsom family.
    • Francis Rountree, enumerated separately in Lowndes County in 1830, may have been related, but the census states no relationship.[19]
    • Later Brooks and Lowndes County Rountrees cannot be attached to the pioneer by surname alone.

    The middle name Lester should not be assigned to the pioneer. It belongs securely to the younger James in later records and appears for the elder James only in derivative online trees.

    The first-four-settlers tradition

    Source lineage

    Harden's 1913 county biography is the earliest fully inspectable source located that names the quartet. It says James Rountree, Lawrence Armstrong Folsom, Drew Vickers, and Alfred Belote explored southern Irwin County in 1815, obtained land, returned for their families, and became the first permanent white settlers of Lowndes County.[5]

    Huxford's 1922 quarterly names the same four families but dates their move to 1821. The article says its information came chiefly from Hortense W. Baker's Valdosta Times historical sketches. No Baker article, interview notes, or earlier manuscript was located, so the sharper 1821 date cannot be treated as original evidence.[6]

    The 1888 Valdosta Times reminiscence predates both books and says the first house in Lowndes County was built by James “Roundtree” on land later owned by West and James. It does not name the quartet or date the construction.[7] The 1890 Clara article likewise places Rountree among the first settlers, but its principal subject was Clara's life, not a formal county chronology.[8]

    R. E. L. Folsom's 1899 sketch conflicts with the quartet chronology by identifying Washington Joyce's 1824 home and ferry as the first white settlement in present Lowndes County.[9] Geography explains part of the disagreement: original Lowndes was much larger than today's county. The conflicting accounts still show why a precise “first” ranking cannot be proved from the later narratives alone.

    Finding: strong for an old and repeated tradition; probable for Rountree's permanent settlement in the region by the early 1820s; unresolved for the 1815 scouting trip and exact 1821 household move.

    Origins, land, and migration

    A modern synthesis preserving Robert Jeffries's research reports that Rountree bought Telfair County Lots 318 and 319, District 14, on June 27, 1808; appeared in the 1811 Pulaski tax digest with land in several counties and four enslaved people; and held 405 acres and eight enslaved people in 1818. The cited deed and tax images were not obtained, so these entries remain reported abstracts.[3]

    The original 1820 census establishes a James Rountree household in Pulaski County.[1-2] Jeffries's abstracts then place “James Rountree of Pulaski County” in two Irwin County purchases:

    • March 6, 1821: Land Lot 497, District 9, from Kinchen P. Tyson of Jones County for $220, reported as Irwin Deed Book A, page 27.
    • October 16, 1821: Land Lot 516, District 9, from Joseph Barr of Franklin County for $200, reported as Irwin Deed Book A, page 25.[3-4]

    These exact citations make the deeds reproducible courthouse targets. They support an early move toward original Lowndes, but they do not by themselves prove when James built a house or brought his family. His March 1823 Pulaski marriage shows that he retained an active connection there after buying the Irwin land.[10-11]

    The 1830 Lowndes tax digest reportedly taxed James for Lots 451, 497, and 516, District 9, with his home plantation on Lots 497 and 516.[3] The digest image was not located. The lots lie in old Lowndes County, not the later town of Barney; their relevance to Barney is genealogical through descendants, not because James lived in the future town.

    Household and slavery

    1820 Pulaski County census

    The original image shows James Rountree as the head of a Pulaski County household. The clearest fresh reading of the column marks is:

    • one free white male age 26-44;
    • one free white male under ten;
    • three free white females under ten;
    • no adult free white female;
    • nine enslaved people: two males under 14, two males 14-25, three females under 14, one female 14-25, and one female 26-44;
    • six people engaged in agriculture; fourteen people total.[1-2]

    Published and derivative readings sometimes give ten enslaved people. Ancestry also exposes a duplicate or misaligned index entry that produces a different total. Because the original image's tightly spaced tally marks are difficult and the derivative indexes conflict, nine is the best fresh image reading, not an unquestionable total. The dossier preserves the disagreement rather than silently choosing the most repeated number.[1-3]

    The absence of an adult white woman is consistent with the family tradition that James's unidentified first wife had died by 1820. It does not prove widowhood or name the four children.

    1830 census correction

    A derivative Lowndes census transcription long printed household 257 as “James Rowntree - 4 M, 7 F.” High-resolution inspection of the original NARA schedule shows that the name is Jesse Townsend, not James Rountree.[18-19] The supposed 1830 James household must be removed from the evidence chain.

    The original schedule does show Francis Rountree at household 84, but it gives no relationship to James.[19] James's absence as a clearly identified head remains unexplained. Tax evidence, if the original digest is obtained, may clarify whether he was omitted, counted elsewhere, or represented under a damaged or misread name.

    Clara

    The 1890 Valdosta Times article about “Auntie Clara” said she came to Lowndes with the first settlers while enslaved by James Rountree. After his death, the article said, she was “hired” by one of his daughters, whose married name appears in the OCR as Mrs. Linton. Clara later went to Palatka, Florida, after emancipation, bought land and an orange grove, and was described as independent in her eightieth year.[8]

    This account is important because it remembers an enslaved person as an individual rather than only a census tally. It is still a late newspaper recollection; Clara's full name, the daughter's identity, and the exact wording of the faint original article need further verification. The apparent daughter “Mrs. Linton” also does not fit the standard compiled child lists and must not be forced into them.

    Marriages and children

    Unidentified first wife

    No marriage record or name was found for James's first wife. Later family reconstructions assign five children to her: John, Harriet, Nancy, Weston W., and Henrietta. They usually explain the four young children in the 1820 household by saying John died before enumeration. The census names nobody, and no located estate or guardianship record proves that reconstruction.[1-3]

    Nancy Hendley

    The original Pulaski County marriage license and return are clear. The license authorized the marriage of James Rountree and Nancy Hendly on March 5, 1823; W. B. McGehee, justice of the peace, solemnized it on March 6, 1823.[10-11]

    This corrects two derivative errors. Some indexes turn March 6 into May 6, and a 1976 compiled family article gives 1811. The original record controls.[10-11,23]

    Later accounts identify Nancy as a daughter of William Hendley and give her birth as April 22, 1793. The marriage record does not name her parents. Children commonly assigned to James and Nancy are James Lester, Annie B., and Georgia Ann. Their placement is repeated across compiled genealogies and collaborative profiles, but no complete estate distribution or guardianship packet was found naming all of them.[21-23]

    The 1976 family article is especially cautionary: it gives James's death as 1837 and his marriage as 1811, both contradicted by contemporary or original evidence. Its child list is useful as a research lead, not proof.[23]

    Civic record

    Direct newspaper reprints of the 1833 Lowndes County grand-jury presentments include James Rountree among the jurors.[24-26] This is the strongest located proof of a public role. No reliable evidence was found that he held elected office, militia rank, church trusteeship, or War of 1812 service. Those roles should not be borrowed from the other early settlers.

    Murder on the Tallahassee road

    James Rountree was killed in Leon County, Florida, on the night of March 26, 1834. The contemporary reports form a short but coherent chain:

    1. The Georgia Courier of April 4 reported that a Mr. Rountree's body had been found near the Georgia line and that robbery was suspected.[12]
    2. The Southern Recorder of April 16 named him as James Rowntree, a Lowndes County resident. It said he was murdered at night while camped on the road from Tallahassee to Thomasville and that three Black men were suspected, one already apprehended.[13]
    3. The Georgia Constitutionalist of April 18 said Florida's governor offered $200 and Tallahassee citizens another $200 for two fugitives charged with the murder.[14]

    These reports verify the death, general place, travel route, and reward. They do not prove the suspects' guilt. They also do not say that James was returning from salt works, despite that detail in later retellings.

    A later historical quotation from an 1837 Tallahassee Floridian item names Joe and Crittenden and gives robbery as the supposed motive.[27] The original 1837 newspaper page was not located. Later accounts connect the killing to Florida's Regulators and vigilante violence; those retrospective narratives should not be treated as a proved trial record.[28]

    No conviction, acquittal, execution, pardon, or complete court file was found. The safest account stops where the contemporary evidence stops.

    Estate, death date, and burial

    The Federal Union published a Lowndes legal notice on July 23, 1834, stating that James McMullin had applied for letters of administration on the estate of James Rountree, deceased. William Smith, clerk of the court of ordinary, had issued the notice July 8; it was repeated through August.[15-17]

    The notice verifies that James was dead and that administration was being opened. It does not name a widow, children, land, enslaved people, creditors, or distributees. No full probate packet, inventory, appraisal, sale, guardian return, or final distribution was located online.

    The contemporary death date is March 26, 1834.[12-14] Find a Grave and Ancestry's derivative memorial index repeat a Tallahassee-area death but provide no photographed marker or cemetery register for James.[29] His burial place remains unresolved.

    Connection to later Brooks County and Barney families

    The best-supported route is:

    "James Rountree (d. 1834)" → "Weston/West W. Rountree (about 1815-1895)" → "Henrietta Elizabeth Rountree Joyce and other children" → later Brooks County and Barney Rountree/Joyce households.

    The critical first step is now strong probable. A transcription of West Rountree's February 15, 1895 Tifton Gazette obituary explicitly calls James his father and says West and his brother James lived on their father's Lowndes place. The same obituary names West's daughter Mrs. Elizabeth Joyce and sons James W., George F., Noel L., William F., and Hardy V. Rountree.[30] The original newspaper image has not been obtained, so the transcription cannot yet carry a fully verified grade.

    The 1850 Lowndes census independently places Weston with Eda/Edith and children Henrietta E. and James W.[31] This supports the household structure, while later records identify Henrietta Elizabeth as the wife of William Washington Joyce. Their descendants connect directly to the Joyce family around Barney. William F. Rountree also appears among successful farmers near Barney in the 1899 town profile.[9,30-31]

    Thus James is a strong-probable ancestor of the later Barney Rountree-Joyce cluster. The missing controlling document is James's own probate, guardian, or parent-child deed naming Weston.

    Conflicts and cautions

    1. Birth and parents: about 1787, Burke County, William F. Rountree, and Rachel all come from later genealogies, not located original proof.[3,23,29]
    2. First-settler date: Harden gives an 1815 exploration; Huxford gives an 1821 move; neither cites a contemporary record.[5-6]
    3. 1820 slavery count: the original image is difficult; the best fresh reading is nine, while published accounts say ten.[1-3]
    4. 1830 census: the alleged James entry is Jesse Townsend. Any dossier or tree using “4 males, 7 females” for James should be corrected.[18-19]
    5. Marriage: March 6, 1823 is original-record proof; May 6 and 1811 are derivative errors.[10-11,23]
    6. Children: the standard lists are reconstructions. No located estate record names a complete set, and the 1890 Clara article creates an unresolved daughter-name conflict.[8,21-23]
    7. Murder: contemporary sources prove the death and accusations, not guilt, trial outcome, a salt-works destination, or later vigilante narratives.[12-14,27-28]
    8. Burial: no verified grave or marker was found.[29]
    9. Barney connection: strong probable through Weston's 1895 obituary transcription; James's own probate remains the missing direct link.[30-31]

    Research coverage and negative findings

    The search covered official records, newspapers, and published local histories. Name variants included Rountree, Roundtree, Rowntree, Rauntree, and “Jas.” paired with Pulaski, Irwin, Lowndes, Telfair, Burke, Nancy Hendley, Weston, Francis, Lots 497/516, probate, jury, murder, grave, and Tallahassee.

    Fresh logged-in Ancestry work inspected the 1820 census, the 1823 marriage, a 1834 tax-index entry, the Find a Grave index, and a 1976 compiled family-history image. Internet Archive supplied original federal schedules and the Harden and Huxford volumes. Georgia Historic Newspapers supplied direct 1833-1834 newspaper pages. Newspapers.com supplied the 1890 and 1899 Valdosta Times images; the 1888 item was available only through a public transcription. FamilySearch, WikiTree, Find a Grave, Ray City History, Georgia Archives, and JSTOR were used for record leads and derivative continuity, with their limits stated.

    No original image was obtained for the 1808 Telfair deeds, 1811/1818 Pulaski tax digests, 1821 Irwin deeds, 1830 Lowndes tax digest, 1895 Weston obituary, James's probate packet, a grave marker, or a court disposition in the murder case. No verified military, church, officeholding, or War of 1812 record was found.

    Best next records

    1. Obtain Irwin Deed Book A, pages 25 and 27, and verify the grantors, dates, consideration, recording dates, witnesses, and exact lot descriptions.
    2. Inspect the original 1811/1818 Pulaski and 1830/1834 Lowndes tax digests, including enslaved-person columns and estate timing.
    3. Locate James's Lowndes probate/administration packet, inventories, sales, guardian returns, and final distribution. This is the best route to his children and the names of enslaved people.
    4. Obtain the February 15, 1895 Tifton Gazette image for West Rountree's obituary.
    5. Search Leon County and territorial Florida court, jail, reward, governor, and clemency files for Joe, Crittenden, and the unnamed third suspect.
    6. Identify Hortense W. Baker's Valdosta Times sketches and determine whether she used family papers, interviews, or an earlier manuscript.
    7. Reopen the 1890 Clara article at full resolution and trace Clara and the apparent Mrs. Linton in Palatka records.
    8. Search for a cemetery register, grave survey, or contemporary burial reference for James.

    Citation audit note

    Original records, contemporary reports, later recollections, compiled genealogy, collaborative profiles, and modern memorials are labeled separately. Repetition does not turn a later tradition into independent proof. The 1830 census error, 1820 tally conflict, uncertain parentage, incomplete child proof, unadjudicated murder accusations, missing probate, and unresolved burial remain visible rather than being silently harmonized.
