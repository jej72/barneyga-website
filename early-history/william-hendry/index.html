    William Hendry of Liberty and Lowndes Counties, Georgia

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Research conclusion

    William Hendry can be followed with strong confidence from Liberty County in 1820 to Lowndes County in 1830 and 1834. Original census and tax images, an official Georgia law, contemporary newspapers, and an estate sale establish the core of his public life. He was a Mount Zion Camp Ground trustee, served on the May 1833 Lowndes grand jury, owned land in both Liberty and Lowndes Counties, and was dead by September 23, 1840. His estate included Lowndes District 12 Land Lots 517 and 518.[1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8]

    The familiar biography is less secure than that record chain. Folks Huxford, drawing on a Hendry family compilation attributed to Milton D. Wilson, says William was born in New Hanover County, North Carolina, on February 12, 1783; married Nancy McFail in Liberty County on December 7, 1807; moved to Lowndes about 1825; and died of typhoid on June 6, 1840.[9] Those details are consistent with the census, estate, and cemetery tradition, but no original birth, marriage, family Bible, death, or probate-distribution record was located online. They should be credited as family tradition, not repeated as independently proved facts.

    No contemporary record located in this project gives William a middle name. The modern form “William Tillman Hendry” comes from a contributor-maintained memorial and later derivative material.[10] This dossier therefore calls him William Hendry.

    Identity and record chain

    Liberty County, 1820

    The 1820 federal census lists William Hendry in Liberty County. His household contained one male and one female age 26 through 44, five boys under sixteen, and two girls under ten, nine people in all. Three people were engaged in agriculture. No enslaved people were enumerated.[1]

    The household fits the age and child pattern in the later Huxford-Wilson family account, but census tick marks do not identify a wife or children by name. The image proves a Liberty County household headed by William; it does not by itself prove the exact birth date, Nancy McFail’s identity, or the eleven-child list.

    Lowndes County, 1830-1834

    The 1830 Lowndes census lists William Hendry with eleven free white household members and no enslaved people. The oldest male and female were each age 40 through 49. John Hendry appears in the next household on the same page.[2]

    William’s 1830 tax row places him in Captain Pike’s district with one poll and no enslaved people entered. The difficult land columns include Liberty and Lowndes property and District 12 Land Lot 512. Some acreage and ownership notations are hard to distinguish, so the preserved image is more reliable than an exact modern transcription of every column.[3]

    The 1834 digest is clearer. It again lists William with one poll and no enslaved people entered. His land includes District 12 Lots 517 and 512 in Lowndes County and additional Liberty County acreage. John Hendry follows him in the digest.[4] The recurring Liberty interests, adult-age pattern, surname, and move into Lowndes make the 1820 and 1830-1834 records a strong-probable chain for the same man.

    Death and estate

    A Southern Recorder legal notice dated September 23, 1840 sought authority to sell the real estate of William Hendry, late of Lowndes County, deceased. William H. Hendry was administrator.[5] Repeated notices advertised a November 20 sale at William’s late residence of hogs, cattle, horses, household and kitchen furniture, and other movable property.[6]

    The 1840 tax digest independently distinguishes the two men: it lists living taxpayer William H. Hendry and identifies him separately as administrator of the estate of William Hendry, deceased.[7] William H. was therefore not merely the elder man recorded with a middle initial.

    An April 13, 1841 notice advertised District 12 Lots 517 and 518 for sale at Troupville as the deceased William’s property. William H. again signed as administrator.[8] The notices prove that William died before September 23, 1840, that William H. administered the estate, and that the estate owned the two Lowndes lots. They do not state William’s exact death date or the administrator’s relationship to him.

    Home tract and location

    The best defensible location for William’s Lowndes home is the estate tract formed by District 12 Land Lots 517 and 518. Georgia’s official land-lot GIS places the approximate lot centroids at 30.902353, -83.657136 for Lot 517 and 30.889638, -83.657372 for Lot 518.[11] The tract is in present-day Brooks County, near Mule Creek and roughly between Pavo and Quitman. That agrees with Huxford’s later description.[9]

    This is a land-lot reconstruction, not an identified house footprint. The perishable-property sale was to occur at the late residence, while the later land notice names both lots, but neither notice says which lot contained the dwelling. No deed chain, survey, plat showing a house, or archaeological identification was found online.

    Georgia Archives’ online headright-plat index also has five Liberty County entries under William Hendry: two 200-acre entries in 1816, 2.9 acres in 1817, 150 acres in 1818, and 196 acres on the Canoochee River in 1818.[12] They are useful leads for the Liberty-to-Lowndes man, but the index names alone do not prove that all five belong to him. Original plats, grants, and conveyances are needed before treating them as his parcel history.

    Mount Zion Camp Ground

    Mount Zion is William Hendry’s best-documented public role.

    The first tenters and the Hardy/Hendry problem

    Hamilton W. Sharpe, a participant, wrote in 1884 that William Hendry and John Hendry were among Mount Zion’s first seven tenters. His article dates the first meeting to fall 1829.[13] That statement was made about fifty-five years later, but it is direct participant recollection and specifically names both men.

    The same original page says the three-man site committee consisted of “William Hardy,” William Blair, and Sharpe. A 1941 reprint preserves “Wm. Hardy” for the committee while again naming William Hendry among the tenters.[14] Huxford later printed the committee member as William Hendry and dated the meeting to 1828.[9] The later deed and statute make Hendry’s Mount Zion leadership clear, but they do not authorize silently changing the newspaper’s "Hardy" to "Hendry". The committee identity and the 1828/1829 year remain unresolved.

    Norman Campbell’s 1903 recollection broadly included William, John, Eli, and James Hendry among Mount Zion’s founders.[15] Campbell moved into the area in 1829 and admitted difficulty remembering names. A 1926 centennial account likewise placed Hendry settlers near the camp ground but warned that minor details might be inaccurate.[16] Both are useful local memory, not independent kinship proof.

    The 1831 deed

    Huxford printed a transcription that he said he copied from the original November 8, 1831 deed. Sion and Enoch Hall conveyed about six acres associated with the west line of District 12 Lot 271, including the camps, church, and burying ground, to Methodist Episcopal trustees William Blair, William Hendry, Lawrence Folsom, James J. Joyce, and William G. Hall. John Morrison proved the deed on October 3, 1832, and clerk William Smith recorded it on February 26, 1833 in Lowndes Deed Book A, pages 654-655.[17]

    The courthouse copy was later lost in the fire and was not re-recorded. Huxford’s original-based transcription is strong evidence, but an image of the manuscript deed was not located.

    The 1833 incorporation act

    The official session law gives direct proof. Section 10 incorporated “William Blair, John Jordan, William Hendry, James J. Jordan, and Randal Folsom” and their successors as the Trustees of the Mount Zion Camp Ground in Lowndes County.[18] This list differs from the deed trustees except for Blair and Hendry. The two documents served different legal purposes and need not contain identical boards.

    The statute verifies William’s position without relying on Huxford or later oral history. It does not prove that every Hendry named in later founder lists was related to him.

    Civic record

    The Georgia Times and State Rights’ Advocate printed the May 1833 Lowndes grand-jury presentments with William Hendry among the jurors and William Blair as foreman. The panel addressed road enforcement, ferry charges, county records, and poor-school funds.[19] This is direct evidence of William’s civic participation, though it supplies no age, occupation, or family relationship.

    Huxford says William became a commissioner to contract for a Lowndes courthouse and jail in December 1835.[9] The official law does not print his surname that way. It names "William Henry", along with William Folsome, Jarrel Johnson, Samuel M. Clyet, Henry Strickland, John Knight, and John Lindsey, and was assented to December 23, 1835.[20] Huxford gives "Hendry" and December 28.

    The printed "Henry" may be an error for Hendry, but no original bill, journal annotation, commission, or executive minute was located to prove it. William’s appointment is therefore unresolved and contradicted by the official printed text. He should not presently be called a courthouse or county-site commissioner as an established fact.

    No online road petition, viewers’ return, overseer appointment, bridge contract, county road minute, or other direct record of a road role was found.

    Military claims

    An index to War of 1812 compiled service records contains a William Hendry who served as second lieutenant in the 2nd Regiment, Pray’s Georgia Militia.[21] He is a plausible candidate because the dossier subject was in Georgia by 1820 and later family history says he served. The index supplies no age, residence, wife, or relative, however, so it does not complete the identity bridge. A second indexed William Hendry served as a private in North Carolina militia, which adds to the ambiguity.[21]

    The modern cemetery marker calls the Lowndes settler a veteran of 1812.[10] That is evidence of later commemoration, not a contemporary military record. The safest conclusion is that Georgia service by a man of the same name is documented, while its assignment to this William is possible to probable, not proved.

    The downloaded bounty-warrant register does not help. Warrant 996 cross-references William Samuel Hendry and the year 1806, before the War of 1812; it is not evidence for the subject.[22]

    No original record was found for the reported 1807 ensign appointment or 1825 Liberty County justice-of-the-peace appointment. The 1836 Lowndes militia return does not name William among the 81st Regiment field and staff. Official Brushy Creek reports identify Captain John Pike and other commanders but do not list William, and no company roster was located.[23] William Hendry’s participation at Brushy Creek is unproved.

    Huxford’s officer table lists "Wm. H. Hendry" as an ensign in 1837.[9] That is a younger man, not proof of the elder William’s service.

    Family reconstruction

    Huxford’s Wilson-derived account calls William the third son of Robert Hendry and Ann Lee and says he married Nancy McFail in Liberty County on December 7, 1807. It lists eleven children:[9]

    1. James Edward, born September 29, 1808
    2. Eli Harrison, born September 26, 1810
    3. Harriet, born March 28, 1812
    4. Nancy Jane, born March 26, 1814
    5. Robert McFail, born February 29, 1816
    6. William Hamilton, born May 9, 1818
    7. Neil, born April 6, 1820
    8. John McFail, born July 5, 1822
    9. David Lee, born June 18, 1824
    10. Mary Winn, born July 2, 1826
    11. Elizabeth C., born December 30, 1830

    The 1820 household structure fits the Wilson-derived list through that year, but the 1830 schedule is only partly consistent with it. The census has nine people below the oldest male and female, while the list supplies ten children born by the census date if all were still alive and at home, and the sex and age bands do not align exactly. The census therefore neither proves nor cleanly corroborates the full child list. No will, probate distribution, guardianship, family Bible, or original marriage return naming the whole family was located online.

    William H. Hendry is a strong-probable match for the listed son William Hamilton. In 1840 he administered the estate, and an 1850 Lowndes household headed by Wm. H. Hendry, age 31, fits the reported 1818 birth year.[7][24] The estate notice itself never calls him a son.

    John Hendry is likewise a strong-probable brother, not a proved one. Huxford calls him William’s brother; the two appear adjacent in the 1830 census and tax material, and the older John remained in Lowndes through 1850, when he was recorded as age 72 and born in North Carolina.[2][4][9][25] Adjacency and a compiled family account are persuasive, but no located deed, probate, Bible, or contemporary statement expressly establishes the sibling relationship.

    Death and burial tradition

    Huxford reports that typhoid killed son Eli H. Hendry on June 4, William on June 6, and Nancy on June 27, 1840, and says they were buried on Mule Creek.[9] Find a Grave memorial 37017171 and a modern replacement marker repeat the dates and identify the burial place as Hendry Cemetery.[10]

    The estate record confirms that William was dead by September 23, 1840, but no contemporary obituary, death notice, epidemic report, burial entry, or original gravestone was found. The exact dates, typhoid diagnosis, Nancy’s June death, and Mule Creek burial are strong family and cemetery tradition. The marker is modern and should not be treated as a surviving 1840 stone.

    Enslavement evidence

    The 1820 and 1830 federal censuses enumerate no enslaved people in William’s household.[1][2] His 1830 and 1834 tax rows have no enslaved people entered.[3][4] The 1840 perishable-property notice lists livestock and household goods but does not advertise enslaved people.[6]

    These records justify the narrow statement that no enslaved people appear in the inspected census and tax entries. They do not prove that William never held an interest in enslaved labor at any point in his life or through another estate or arrangement.

    What is proved, probable, and unresolved

    Proved

    • A William Hendry headed the 1820 Liberty household and the 1830 Lowndes household.
    • The Lowndes William was taxed in 1830 and 1834, with District 12 land and no enslaved people entered.
    • He was an incorporated Mount Zion trustee in 1833.
    • He served on the May 1833 Lowndes grand jury.
    • He was dead by September 23, 1840.
    • William H. Hendry administered his estate.
    • The estate owned District 12 Lots 517 and 518.

    Strong probable or strong tradition

    • The Liberty and Lowndes records describe the same man.
    • Nancy McFail was his wife.
    • William H. was his son William Hamilton.
    • John Hendry was his brother.
    • The eleven-child Huxford-Wilson list substantially represents his family.
    • The estate tract was the general location of his home near Mule Creek.

    Unresolved or unproved

    • "Tillman" or any other middle name.
    • Original proof of the February 12, 1783 birth and December 7, 1807 marriage.
    • Exact death date, typhoid diagnosis, and exact grave location.
    • Whether the 1884 site-committee member printed "William Hardy" was Hendry.
    • Whether the first Mount Zion meeting occurred in 1828 or 1829.
    • Whether the 1835 act’s "William Henry" was a printing error for Hendry.
    • Identity of the War of 1812 Georgia officer and any Brushy Creek service.
    • Original proof of the reported ensign, justice-of-the-peace, and road roles.

    Online research coverage and limits

    Local project files and previously preserved books were searched first. The online search then covered Ancestry census, tax, military, marriage, family-history, and cemetery indexes; FamilySearch catalog and result leads; Find a Grave; Georgia Historic Newspapers; Newspapers.com; Digital Library of Georgia session laws; Georgia Archives plat indexes; Internet Archive; HathiTrust; Google Books metadata; and general web results. Searches used "Hendry", "Henry", "Hardy", "Hendrie", and "Hendrey" with Liberty, Lowndes, Brooks, Mount Zion, Mule Creek, estate, death, military, and family terms.[26]

    The best remaining targets are the original Lowndes administrator bond and probate packet; the full Wilson manuscript; Liberty marriage, church, deed, tax, and plat records; Georgia executive commission books; the original 1835 bill and legislative journals; and a War of 1812 compiled service or pension file with residence or kinship data.
