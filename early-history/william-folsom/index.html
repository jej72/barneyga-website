    William Folsom of old Irwin, Lowndes, and Brooks counties, Georgia

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Evidence-graded online dossier

    Subject: William Folsom, early South Georgia settler and reported son of Lawrence Folsom
    Life dates: born about 1795 in Georgia; died November 16, 1872, at his Brooks County residence
    Prepared: August 19, 2026
    Standard: Original records and contemporary newspapers control. Later county histories, compiled genealogies, collaborative trees, and cemetery pages are identified as such. Repetition among derivative sources is not treated as independent proof.

    Bottom line

    William Folsom was an early Irwin County resident who remained in the same general South Georgia neighborhood as county boundaries changed from Irwin to Lowndes and then Brooks. Later courthouse-based histories identify a William Folsom on Irwin County courthouse-and-jail commissions in 1823 and 1825. A published transcription of an 1845 Lowndes grand-jury list also names William Folsom, and Brooks County's first petit-jury list in 1859 includes the name. Age and location make the subject a plausible to strong-probable match in these civic records, but the records as reviewed do not identify his wife, parent, or land.[1–4]

    His death is unusually well documented. Hamilton W. Sharpe's obituary says Folsom died at his Brooks County residence on November 16, 1872, in his seventy-eighth year. It describes a long-established Methodist, generous to poor people, ministers, and church causes, and says that an aged wife survived him. The complete original page appeared in the Southern Christian Advocate on February 19, 1873.[5][6]

    The family structure is clearer than earlier compilations suggested, but it is not complete. FamilySearch's Pulaski County marriage record/index reports William's 1813 marriage to Elizabeth Folsom. Elizabeth's 1876 will directly calls Edith Rountree and Nancy Bradford her daughters; identifies W. R., C. W., A. C., and Ruth Ann Ryall as her grandchildren and the children of Rachel Ann Ryall; and names C. W. Ryall and William Harrell as grandsons. Those clauses make the child reconstruction firmer. They do not prove a complete child list, William's parentage, or the relationships of Green, George, Janie, and Noah. Lawrence and Rachel as William's parents remain strong probable.[7–10][34][35]

    A newly supplied photograph corrects an important point about the burial. William and Elizabeth did have an inscribed marble grave monument. The surviving stone is broken and weathered, but both names are visible. William's panel preserves the year 1872; Elizabeth's preserves 1877. The image does not establish where the photograph was taken, and the smaller date lines are not clear enough for a full independent transcription.[36] Field documentation places the gravesite at approximately 31.042122, -83.519596. The Georgia Geospatial Information Office's official land-lot layer independently places that coordinate in Land Lot 520, District 9, Brooks County, within original Irwin County.[27][37]

    Administration of William's Brooks County estate was secured by a $30,000 bond. William R. Ryall and Charles W. Ryall served as administrators, with West Rountree, Green Lawson, and Nicholas M. Reddick as sureties. Newspaper notices document a Colquitt County beef-cattle sale and an application to sell William's land for the heirs. None of these records names the heirs, describes the land, establishes an estate value, or proves the later Rountree-to-J. E. Moody title chain.[11–13]

    Identity and chronology

    This dossier concerns the William Folsom born in Georgia about 1795, living in old Lowndes County by 1850, in Brooks County after its 1858 creation, and dying there in 1872. His age, wife Elizabeth, neighborhood, associates, 1870 household, obituary, and probate form a coherent identity chain.[5][9][14–16]

    He must not be merged with Lieutenant William Folsom of Revolutionary-era Burke County; William I. Folsom, a younger Brooks County Confederate soldier; later judges or lawyers of the same name; William Folsom Ryall; or William Folsom Harrell. The surname cluster is dense, and title or location alone is not enough to transfer a record from one man to another.[7][10]

    Birth, parents, and migration

    The obituary places William in his seventy-eighth year at death, implying birth between November 17, 1794, and November 16, 1795. The 1870 census gives age seventy-five, also pointing to about 1794–1795. The frequently repeated year 1797 comes from later genealogy and cemetery/tree profiles, not from a contemporary birth record. A publication-safe birth statement is about 1795 in Georgia. Burke County remains plausible but unproved.[5][7–10][16]

    The 1938 Genealogy of the Folsom Family calls him the son of Lawrence Armstrong Folsom and Rachel Vickers. That Georgia section openly depends on family informants, principally Mrs. Boyd and Horace B. Folsom. FamilySearch, WikiTree, Find a Grave, and other online trees repeat the relationship. Contemporary 1849–1850 legal notices add important independent support: a William Folsom acted as executor of Lawrence Folsom's Lowndes County estate, sought leave to sell its enslaved people, and advertised estate property at James Folsom's house. The notices do not call William Lawrence's son, and the original will or probate file was not located. The executor's identity with this subject is strong probable from name, county, chronology, and family context; Lawrence and Rachel as his parents remain strong probable, not directly verified.[7–10][17][28][29]

    William's migration has often been told too simply. Mrs. D. H. Purvis wrote in 1948 that he moved from Irwin County into the later Barney district in the 1830s. Yet the courthouse-commission traditions already place a William Folsom in Irwin civic affairs in 1823 and 1825, and the family genealogy assigns several children to Lowndes County before or during the 1830s. The surviving evidence supports continuous residence in the broader Irwin-to-Lowndes region, but it does not supply one exact arrival date at the later Brooks plantation.[1–3][7][18]

    Public and community roles

    Irwin County courthouse commissions

    Folks Huxford's county history and his 1950 newspaper history say the General Assembly first appointed William Folsom, James Crum, Silloway McCall, Joshua Griffin, and Alexander McDonald as Irwin courthouse-and-jail commissioners on December 13, 1823. They apparently did not complete the work. The December 24, 1825 organizing act then named John B. F. Dixon, James Wilcox, Emanuel Knowles, William Folsom, and Elijah Beasley as replacement Irwin commissioners.[1–3]

    Huxford distinguishes the 1825 Irwin appointment from the simultaneous Lawrence Folsom commission for newly created Lowndes County. The two names should not be merged: his account puts Lawrence on the Lowndes commission and William on the Irwin commission. The target William was old enough and geographically plausible, and the repeated name in the two Irwin appointments supports a single local man. Neither the original act pages nor an identifying residence, relative, or tract was recovered, however. The subject's service is therefore possible to strong probable, not verified.[1–3][30]

    Jury service

    A published transcription of a June 1845 Lowndes Superior Court report names William Folsom among the grand jurors at Troupville. The transcription is strong secondary evidence, but the underlying newspaper image was not independently inspected in this project. Even if the reading is correct, the report establishes only jury service, not foremanship, office, party, or a specific verdict.[4]

    Huxford's transcription of Brooks County's first jury lists names William Folsom as petit juror number forty in 1859. Age, location, and prominence make the pioneer the strong-probable identity, but the list itself provides no age or residence. No record found online makes him a judge, justice of the peace, sheriff, legislator, militia officer, road commissioner, or elected county officer.[1]

    Wife and children

    Elizabeth Folsom

    FamilySearch's Pulaski County marriage record/index reports William and Elizabeth Folsom's marriage on December 11, 1813. Their 1850 and 1870 households and the 1873 obituary continue the identity chain and establish that Elizabeth survived William. The 1938 genealogy identifies her as a distant Folsom cousin, daughter of George Folsom and Edith Penny. No inspected birth, Bible, or parent-naming record proves that ancestry.[5][7][9][14][16][34]

    Elizabeth executed her will on January 28, 1876, and it was recorded August 13, 1877, disproving the genealogy's statement that she died about 1875. A memorial gives March 16, 1877. The newly supplied monument photograph visibly supports 1877, but damage and weathering prevent a confident reading of the smaller month-and-day line from this image alone. Judgment: Elizabeth as William's wife is verified, and the 1813 marriage date has strong indexed record evidence. Her George/Edith parentage is strong compiled tradition. Her death year is now verified by the photographed monument; March 16 remains probable but not independently verified from the image.[7][34–36]

    Publication-safe child structure

    The 1938 genealogy lists eight children. The list is useful, but it is not an heir return and should not be called complete or proved merely because modern trees copy it.[7]

    • Green Folsom: said to have died as a small boy. Compiled-family-history only. FamilySearch's Green profile points to different preferred parents, a material conflict.
    • Rachel Ann Folsom Ryall: Elizabeth's will identifies W. R., C. W., A. C., and Ruth Ann Ryall as her grandchildren and as Rachel Ann Ryall's children. The 1938 genealogy explicitly calls Rachel a daughter of William and Elizabeth, and the Ryall sons' later administration of William's estate reinforces the relationship. Because the will does not use the exact phrase “my daughter Rachel Ann,” the conservative grade is strong probable, bordering verified.[7][11][19][35]
    • Edith Folsom Rountree: Elizabeth's will directly calls Edith her daughter and the wife of West Rountree. William and Elizabeth appear as a married couple across the census chain, with no evidence of stepchildren or another marriage. Verified daughter of Elizabeth; strong-probable daughter of William.[7][11][20][35]
    • George Folsom: listed in the genealogy, but nearby men of the same name create serious confusion. The 1860 slave schedule has a separate George entry. Possible/compiled; identity and child structure unresolved.[7][21]
    • Nancy Folsom Perry Bradford: Elizabeth's will directly calls Nancy Bradford her daughter and the wife of John Bradford. The 1938 genealogy says Nancy first married a Perry, while descendant material reports a later Andrew Jackson Bradford marriage dated after the will was executed. Verified daughter of Elizabeth; strong-probable daughter of William. The Perry/Bradford spouse chronology and the will's “John Bradford” wording remain unresolved.[7][9][35]
    • Martha Folsom Harrell/Horrell: an 1892 obituary for John S. Harrell calls his late wife Martha the “youngest daughter of William Folsom, Esq.” That explicit statement, published twenty years after William's death, joins the compiled genealogy, Martha's placement in William's 1850 household, Elizabeth's identification of William Harrell as a grandson, and the younger William Harrill's presence in William's 1870 dwelling. Strong probable daughter and likely youngest daughter; the article does not identify her mother. Later sources conflict between 1861 and 1895 death dates; Elizabeth's 1876 will is consistent with an earlier death but does not prove 1861.[7][14][16][22][31][35]
    • Hardy Vickers Folsom: Hardy appears in William and Elizabeth's 1850 household; the genealogy identifies him as a son, and a Brooks County marriage record links Hardy V. Folsom and Tempy Ann Wells in 1859. Strong probable son; the census does not state relationships.[7][9][14]
    • Janie/Jane Folsom: the genealogy says James Perry's wife; FamilySearch repeats the marriage but attaches no source. Possible daughter / tree-and-genealogy claim.[7][9]

    FamilySearch adds Noah Folsom, born about 1832, as another son and identifies Julia Drawdy as his wife. Noah belongs to the same local Folsom cluster, but no record found in this research names William and Elizabeth as his parents. He is therefore a possible additional son, not a ninth verified child.[9][10]

    Farming, land, livestock, and wealth

    The 1948 Barney history portrays “Uncle Billy” as a prominent planter near Little River who produced brown sugar for Savannah and Tallahassee and sent large cattle herds to Savannah and Charleston. It says his land later became the Rountree place owned by J. E. Moody. Sharpe's obituary independently supports a comfortable living and generous use of wealth, while the 1873 cattle notice proves that the estate owned beef cattle in Colquitt County. These points give the later account a real economic foundation, but they do not verify every production figure or annual cattle-drive claim.[5][12][18]

    A derivative tax/land KML assigns William Lots 492, 493, 519, 520, and 521 in District 9 and Lots 322 and 323 in District 12, with 3,630 acres in 1869 and 3,670 acres in 1871–1872. The mapped cluster begins north of Barney and lies west of Folsom Bridge. The original digest images and page citations have not been found. Seven full 490-acre lots total 3,430 acres, not either reported total, so the index is internally incomplete or inconsistent.[23]

    The September 1873 notice verifies that William's estate owned land and that the administrators intended to sell it for the heirs. It does not name a lot, acreage, heir, purchaser, or administrator's deed. Purvis's Rountree-to-Moody story remains a credible local tradition awaiting the Brooks deed chain.[13][18]

    Geographic judgment: William's homeplace and William and Elizabeth's gravesite were north of Barney, in the Land Lot 520 area west of Folsom Bridge. They were separate from Foster's Land Lot 227 and Barney's postal Land Lot 320. Field documentation identifies the grave marker in Land Lot 520, District 9, and the official Georgia land-lot map confirms that location. The exact house site, plantation boundaries, acreage, Rountree transfer, Moody transfer, and legal cemetery parcel remain unresolved.[18][23][24][27][37]

    Folsom Bridge

    No located naming record identifies William as Folsom Bridge's namesake. Contemporary 1885 and 1893 evidence and early maps point instead to the older Lawrence–James–Andrew Jackson Folsom settlement at the east approach. William's reported plantation lay several miles west. The safest wording is that the bridge was named for the Folsom family settlement; Lawrence is the leading individual candidate, and William is geographically disfavored as the specific namesake.[24]

    See the Folsom Bridge history for the complete naming and crossing evidence.

    Enslaved people and the post-emancipation household

    A public transcription of the 1860 Brooks County slave schedule lists "Wm. Folsom" with one enslaved male aged eleven and one slave house. A separate George Folsom entry lists ten enslaved people. The entries must not be combined. The original schedule image was not recovered in this pass, and no name can be assigned to the boy.[21]

    The 1870 population schedule places three Black residents in William and Elizabeth's dwelling: Dicy Roberts, twenty-five, a domestic servant; Louis Roberts, four; and John Roberts, three. The census stated no relationships. In 1880, Dicea Roberts headed a Brooks County household with sons Lewis, fifteen, and John, fourteen, plus daughter Harriet, nine. The matching name forms, county, and ages verify postwar continuity for Dicy/Dicea and the two sons.[15][25]

    Nothing in those records proves that Dicy had been enslaved by William. Her age and sex exclude her from the unidentified eleven-year-old boy; Louis and John were born after emancipation. Employment, housing, kinship, or some other association could explain the shared 1870 dwelling. No person has been identified responsibly across the slavery-to-freedom divide.

    The contrast between the one-person 1860 schedule and Purvis's later “large plantation” worked by enslaved labor is unresolved. It may reflect different dates, hired labor, property held by relatives, earlier holdings, or exaggeration in the retrospective account. The evidence does not permit choosing among those explanations.[18][21]

    Mount Zion and Methodist life

    William was not among the seven original tenters named in Hamilton W. Sharpe's 1884 recollection, nor among the trustees in the 1831 Mount Zion deed. Lawrence Folsom held the trustee role. Sharpe did list William among deceased early residents of the Halls/Mount Zion neighborhood, and Purvis wrote in 1948 that “old settlers” credited William with doing more than anyone else toward the later church building.[18][26]

    The obituary supplies the firmest personal evidence. Sharpe, who said he had known William about forty years, described William's conversion at a camp meeting, hospitality to traveling ministers, generosity to church causes and poor people, and standing in the Morven Circuit. This verifies long Methodist affiliation and support. It does not prove the later comparative claim that he contributed more than everyone else, a construction date, a payment amount, or a formal trustee office.[5]

    Death and estate

    The Southern Christian Advocate obituary states that William died at his Brooks County residence on November 16 “last,” in his seventy-eighth year. Because the issue is February 19, 1873, the year is 1872. Georgia Historic Newspapers supplies the complete original page, visibly numbered 28 and carrying the newspaper masthead and volume information. No numbered columns are printed. The writer was H. W. Sharpe.[5]

    On July 7, 1873, William R. Ryall and Charles W. Ryall executed a $30,000 administration bond, with West Rountree, Green Lawson, and Nicholas M. Reddick as sureties. It was recorded August 12. The facing John Tillman bond is unrelated; Sharpe's signature on that facing page must not be attributed to William's estate.[11]

    The August 23 notice sold estate beef cattle at Moultrie. The September notice states that the administrators would seek leave to sell William's lands for the heirs. Later notices directed creditors to present claims and debtors to pay. In September 1875 the Ryalls represented that they had fully administered the estate and sought discharge on the first Monday in December; repeated citations document the closing stage, but no final discharge order was located.[11–13][32][33]

    No online inventory, appraisal, land-sale order, sale return, administrator's deed, heir list or receipts, final return, distribution, or discharge order was located. The estate associates materially support the Ryall and Rountree family traditions, but none of these records directly names a child.[11–13][32][33]

    Elizabeth's later will supplies the kinship language missing from William's estate file. It directly identifies Edith and Nancy as her daughters, the four named Ryalls as her grandchildren and Rachel Ann's children, C. W. Ryall and William Harrell as grandsons, and Charles W. Ryall as executor. The will is powerful family evidence but is not a complete list of William's heirs or children.[35]

    Burial and grave location

    Find a Grave assigns William and Elizabeth to Old Folsom Family Cemetery near Hahira and describes the graves as unmarked. That description is wrong. A user-supplied photograph shows a broken marble monument inscribed for both William and Elizabeth Folsom. William's name and the year 1872 are visible on the upper panel; Elizabeth's name and the year 1877 are visible below. The photograph verifies that a named monument existed, though it does not by itself identify the cemetery or geographic location.[6][36]

    The 1938 genealogy says they were buried on the old Folsom homestead, and Purvis placed William's grave near his old home on the later Rountree/Moody land. Field documentation identifies the gravesite at approximately 31.042122, -83.519596. A point query against the official Georgia land-lot layer places that coordinate in Land Lot 520, District 9, Brooks County, within original Irwin County.[7][18][27][37]

    The grave marker is on William's old homeplace north of Barney. The photograph proves that the named monument survives; field documentation establishes where it stands; and the state GIS confirms the land-lot designation. Find a Grave's placement in Lowndes District 12, Land Lot 187, east of Barney, is incorrect for William and Elizabeth's gravesite. A survey, deed, or burial register has not been found, so the legal cemetery parcel and the full plantation boundary remain unresolved.[6][27][36][37]

    Claims graded

    Verified or strongly supported

    • William lived in the old Irwin/Lowndes/Brooks region and died at his Brooks County residence on November 16, 1872.[5][14–16]
    • A surviving photograph verifies a broken marble monument bearing William and Elizabeth Folsom's names and their respective death years, 1872 and 1877.[36]
    • Field documentation identifies the gravesite at approximately 31.042122, -83.519596; the official Georgia land-lot layer places the point in Land Lot 520, District 9, Brooks County, within original Irwin County.[27][37]
    • FamilySearch's county-marriage record/index reports that William married Elizabeth Folsom in Pulaski County on December 11, 1813; the household and obituary evidence establish that she survived him.[5][14][16][34]
    • A published transcription of an 1845 newspaper names William Folsom as a Lowndes grand juror; the subject's identity is strong probable.[4]
    • Sharpe had known him for about forty years and described him as a generous Morven Circuit Methodist.[5]
    • His estate included land and beef cattle; administration was secured by a $30,000 bond.[11–13]
    • William R. and Charles W. Ryall administered the estate; West Rountree, Green Lawson, and Nicholas M. Reddick were sureties.[11]
    • Elizabeth's will directly identifies Edith Rountree and Nancy Bradford as her daughters and identifies the four named Ryalls and William Harrell as her grandchildren.[35]
    • The 1870 dwelling included Dicy Roberts and her sons Louis and John, who continued together in 1880.[15][25]

    Strong probable or secondary-supported

    • Lawrence Folsom and Rachel Vickers were William's parents.[7–10]
    • The William named on the 1823 and 1825 Irwin courthouse commissions was this man.[1–3][30]
    • The William who executed Lawrence Folsom's estate was this subject.[28][29]
    • Rachel Ann Ryall, Martha Harrell, and Hardy Vickers Folsom were William and Elizabeth's children; Edith Rountree and Nancy Bradford are verified daughters of Elizabeth and strong-probable daughters of William.[7][9][11][14][19][20][22][31][35]

    Unresolved, unsupported, or contradicted

    • Exact birth day/year, Burke County birthplace, and the full parent-naming chain.
    • A complete and record-proved child list; Noah's parentage; the duplicated Edith/George tree profiles; Martha's exact death date.
    • Any office as judge, legislator, justice, sheriff, militia officer, or road overseer.
    • Exact plantation acreage, home site, deed chain, heirs, land-sale buyer, Rountree transfer, and Moody transfer.
    • The reported large plantation west of Folsom Bridge and north of Barney rests on an internally inconsistent derivative land/tax KML and remains unresolved.[18][23]
    • Sugar production and exceptional support of Mount Zion are retrospective local traditions; no contemporary production or church-finance record was located.[18][26]
    • Identification of the unnamed boy enslaved in 1860 or any cross-emancipation identity.
    • William as the specific namesake of Folsom Bridge.
    • Elizabeth's exact death date; the smaller month-and-day inscription remains unreadable in the supplied photograph.

    Online research coverage and limits

    Online checks covered authenticated Ancestry census/probate and obituary indexes, FamilySearch collaborative profiles and record pointers, Find a Grave images and cemetery pages, WikiTree, Internet Archive's Folsom genealogy, Georgia Historic Newspapers, Newspapers.com, Georgia Archives maps/land lots, Ray City History Blog, Wiregrass Region Digital History Project, and broad web searches. Logged-in or image-level access is stated where used. Search snippets and collaborative trees support leads only.

    The following material was not found online: original birth record; Lawrence's will or probate file; original 1823 and 1825 act pages; 1820–1840 William-specific census chain; original 1850/1860 slave-schedule images; William's full Brooks estate file; his land deeds and tax pages; the administrator's land-sale return or deed; heir receipts; Mount Zion local records; and a burial register, cemetery deed, or survey. The monument photograph and field identification were supplied after the online search was completed. This dossier covers the accessible online evidence and those later field sources. It does not imply that undigitized courthouse and church records do not exist.

    Best next archival work

    1. Obtain the original 1823 and 1825 Georgia acts naming Irwin commissioners and compare exact spelling, powers, and dates.
    2. Request William Folsom's complete Brooks County loose estate file and every inventory, appraisal, sale, annual/final return, voucher, distribution, and order/minute entry, 1872–1885.
    3. Search Brooks deed indexes and books from 1859 forward for William/Elizabeth, W. R. and C. W. Ryall as administrators, West/Weston Rountree, Folsom heirs, and J. E. Moody.
    4. Obtain original Lowndes/Brooks tax pages behind the reported 1869–1872 acreage and the original 1850 and 1860 slave schedules.
    5. Search Mount Zion/Morven Circuit trustee, membership, financial, cemetery, and burial records for William and Elizabeth.
    6. Locate a parent-naming record for each proposed child rather than relying on the compiled eight-child list.
