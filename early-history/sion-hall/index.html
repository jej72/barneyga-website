    Sion Hall (c. 1784–1849)

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    An evidence-based dossier on an early settler of original Irwin and Lowndes counties, Georgia

    Research completed: August 18, 2026
    Geographic scope: original Irwin County, original Lowndes County, and the Hall–Mount Zion neighborhood north of present Morven in Brooks County
    Name forms searched: Sion, Sian, Zion, Lion, Simon, Sim, and Dian Hall; Hall's and Halls

    Conclusion

    Sion Hall was in original Irwin County by 1820. Later accounts place his move to the Coffee Road neighborhood north of later Morven in 1823 or early 1824. Georgia law designated his house as the temporary Lowndes County court-and-election place in 1825, then moved those functions to Francis Roundtree's house in 1826. The law also made Hall's house the 1829 justice-court place for Captain Pike's district. These records prove that Hall's home was an early public center. They do not prove that Hall was a judge, justice, military captain, or legislator.[1][2][3][4][19][20]

    Hall and his son Enoch held an interest in Land Lot 271, District 12. Their 1831 Mount Zion deed and 1845–1846 sheriff-sale notices tie them to the lot; the 1846 notice describes operating saw and grist mills there. Later accounts place Hall's home, travelers' lodging, mill, grave, and Mount Zion campground in the same small neighborhood. A credible 1902 correction says the residence itself stood in adjoining Lot 270. The property cluster is clear, but the house footprint is not.[4][5][6][7][8]

    The family record is thinner. Census records trace Hall from Irwin in 1820 to Lowndes in 1830 and 1840. An indexed Telfair County entry records his 1817 marriage to Elizabeth Coaker. Nineteenth-century and later sources identify Colonel Enoch Hall as his son, but no located probate or Bible record establishes a complete family. Claims about Sion's parents, three wives, all children, exact birth date, and exact death day remain derivative or unresolved.[9][10][11][12][13][14]

    Identity and namesakes

    The record chain concerns the older Sion Hall who headed households in original Irwin County in 1820 and Lowndes County in 1830 and 1840. The unusual name, county continuity, public-site records, Hall family land, and the later Enoch Hall accounts point to one man. Census indexes obscure him as "Dian Hall" in 1820 and "Sim Hall" in 1830. The original 1830 schedule was inspected and reads "Sion Hall".[9][10][11]

    He must not be merged with Sion L. Hall, a grandson born about 1868, or with his son Enoch Hall, his brother John Hall, or other Hall men in the neighborhood. Later honorifics such as "Hon." and "Judge" are not office records. No located document proves that Sion held judicial office, served in the militia, or sat in the legislature.[1][2][15]

    Origins, marriage, and family

    The modern Hall Cemetery marker gives the years 1784–1849. Find a Grave adds North Carolina as the birthplace, November 14 as the death date, and Enoch Hall and Nancy Ann Jackson as parents. Those details may be correct, but the photographed marker does not contain them and the memorial cites no original birth, Bible, probate, or death record. The safest birth description is about 1784, place unproved.[13]

    Three Ancestry collections report that Sion Hall married Elizabeth "Coaker" in Telfair County on April 27, 1817. The entries probably descend from the same county record, so they count as one line of evidence, not three independent confirmations. The original marriage-book image was unavailable. "Coaker" should remain the record spelling; "Coker" is a later variant.[12]

    Folks Huxford's sketch of Colonel Enoch Hall identifies Enoch, born in Telfair County in 1804, as the son of Sion and Bridget "Beady" Hall. An 1886 profile independently calls Enoch "the son of Sion Hall", without naming his mother. This makes the father-son relationship strong. Bridget's identity as Enoch's mother rests on the later compiled sketch rather than a located birth or marriage record.[4][14]

    Find a Grave, FloridaRoots, public trees, and family stories expand the family considerably. They name Bridget or Beady Hall, Elizabeth Coker or Coaker, and Polly or Pollie Morrison as wives; identify Sion's parents as Enoch Hall and Nancy Jackson; and offer varying child lists. FloridaRoots candidly says its compiler had not done much Hall research. No original record found in this project proves the alleged Bridget or Polly marriages, an 1816 separation, Sion's parentage, or a complete list of children. These are research leads, not a publication-safe pedigree.[13][16]

    Presence in original Irwin County

    Huxford identifies Sion as an 1820 Irwin County householder. The matching federal census index appears as "Dian Hall": eighteen people in the household, eight of them enslaved. The original image was not reopened, so the name identification and totals are strong probable rather than fully image-verified.[4][9]

    A published transcription of Irwin court records says Sion was drawn for the first petit-jury panel at the September 21, 1820 Superior Court. The court met at David Williams's house on Lot 147, District 4. The account says the panel was selected for the next term but was not called in March 1821. This distinction matters: Sion's jury selection does not make his home the first Irwin court site.[17]

    A second court-record transcription says that in July 1824 the Irwin Inferior Court appointed Sion Hall, James Allen, and Thomas Townsend to lay out a road from the Ocmulgee River to the Alapaha River. The original minute images were not located for either item, so both remain strong secondary evidence.[18]

    The Coffee Road settlement date

    The census and jury evidence place Sion somewhere in the large original Irwin County by 1820. They do not place him on Lot 271. The move to the Coffee Road neighborhood is remembered in two versions:

    • Huxford says Hall moved when the Coffee Road opened in 1823.[4]
    • The Valdosta Times stated in 1899 that Hall first cut timber in the area on January 20, 1824; a 1900 account gives 1824 without the exact day.[19][20]

    No located 1823 or 1824 deed, tax entry, diary, or road record settles the difference. The defensible conclusion is that Hall moved within original Irwin County to the Coffee Road settlement in 1823 or early 1824. January 20, 1824 is a precise but unsourced retrospective claim.

    Hall's house as the temporary Lowndes public center

    Lowndes County was created from Irwin on December 24, 1825. Georgia's official 1837 digest summarizes the relevant laws:

    "Courts and elections to be held at the house of Sion Hall, 1825 ... Removed to the house of Francis Roundtree, 1826."[1]

    A contemporary legislative report from December 1826 confirms passage of the amendment concerning courts and elections at Sion Hall's. One House-column rendering says "Levi Hall", but the Senate report and official digest identify Sion. The same digest says that in 1829 Hall's house was designated as the justice-court place in Captain Pike's district. "Captain Pike's district" identifies the militia district; it is not Sion's rank.[1][2]

    Huxford says the 1825 act appointed Lawrence Folsom, Sion Hall, William Blair, John J. Underwood, and Daniel McCranie to select the permanent county site. The exact session-law page naming the commissioners was not located, so the commissioner list is strong secondary evidence rather than a newly verified original.[4]

    The date of the first Lowndes Superior Court remains unsettled. A 1900 retrospective places it at Hall's in spring 1826. Huxford quotes William Smith's 1880 recollection that it met there on the first Monday in January 1827. The latter date sits awkwardly beside the 1826 law moving courts and elections to Roundtree's. Original court minutes or a docket are needed. It is safe to say that Hall's house was the statutory temporary site and hosted early county business; it is not safe to assign one exact first-court date.[1][4][20]

    Later accounts describe a large double-pen hewn-log house used for court, lodging, and meals, with some jurors deliberating outdoors. The earliest detailed version located is Montgomery M. Folsom's 1885 travel-history account. An 1886 Atlanta profile repeats its distinctive details and is probably derivative; it should not be counted as independent confirmation.[14][21]

    Several later histories mistakenly call Hall's house the site of Irwin County's first court. That claim is contradicted by the court-record transcription placing the 1820 Irwin court at David Williams's house. The Lowndes and Irwin claims must remain separate.[17][20]

    Land Lots 270 and 271, Mount Zion, and the mills

    The Georgia land-lot service places Lot 271 in the Twelfth District of original Irwin, now Brooks County. The map proves the lot's location, not Hall ownership.[22]

    The Hall interest in Lot 271 rests on three cumulative lines of evidence:

    1. Huxford printed a transcription he said he made from the original November 8, 1831 deed. Sion and Enoch Hall conveyed about six acres connected with camps, church, and burying ground to Methodist Episcopal trustees. The description begins at the west north-south line of Lot 271. The lost Lowndes Deed Book A copy had been recorded at pages 654–655 on February 26, 1833.[4]
    2. An April 1845 sheriff-sale notice levied Lot 271 as property of Sion and Enoch Hall to satisfy a Lowndes Superior Court judgment in "Drury Vickers vs Enoch Hall, Sion Hall and John Hall, securities on stay".[5]
    3. A March 1846 repeat calls Lot 271 well improved, with a saw and grist mill in operation. This proves operating mills on the levied Hall property by 1846. It does not prove who built or personally ran them.[6]

    Hamilton W. Sharpe's 1884 recollection placed Enoch at the Hall center on Lot 271. The 1908 Brooks County map later marked Mount Zion/Camp Ground and a J. L. Hall residence in that lot.[3][8]

    The house itself may have stood next door. In 1902 Norman Campbell, an early resident, corrected a published account and said the first-court residence stood on Lot 270. Lots 270 and 271 adjoin, and a home, mill, campground, burying ground, and larger plantation need not occupy one lot. The best present conclusion is: Hall's court house, lodging place, mills, and Mount Zion campground formed a compact Coffee Road neighborhood at or adjoining Lot 271; Hall's Lot 271 interest is proved, but the residence footprint and its placement in Lot 270 or 271 remain unresolved.[7]

    Inn, merchant activity, and local tradition

    Huxford says Sion kept a public inn on the Coffee Road, lodging twelve to fifteen travelers, and that Enoch continued it after Sion's death. The nineteenth-century court narratives support a large house that lodged officials and travelers. No tavern license or guest register was found, so "public inn" is strongly supported but not proved by a business license.[4][21]

    Sharpe wrote in 1884 that he was "merchantising at Mr. Sion Hall's" when he helped establish an early mail route and Sharpe's Store. This proves merchant activity at the Hall settlement, but it does not show who owned the stock, store, or building. Later claims that Sion owned the store or that his brother John ran a liquor bar remain possible and unverified.[3]

    The 1885 account described old mill sills and a dam near the former Hall home and repeated a comic story about the slow grist mill. The writer expressly declined to vouch for the story's authenticity. The physical mill claim is consistent with the 1846 sale notice; the anecdote remains folklore.[6][21]

    Census households and slavery

    The federal census indexes give the following household totals:[9][10][11]

    Year Indexed name and county Total household Enslaved people Evidence boundary
    1820 "Dian Hall", Irwin 18 8 Strong probable identification; original image not reopened
    1830 "Sim Hall", Lowndes 8 3 Original schedule inspected; manuscript name reads Sion Hall; totals retained from index
    1840 "Sion Hall", Lowndes 14 4 Index verified; original image not reopened

    These schedules do not name wives, children, or enslaved people. They support only numerical statements. No names or relationships can be reconstructed from the aggregate entries.

    The decline from eight enslaved people in 1820 to three in 1830 and four in 1840 is real at the indexed-household level, but the schedules do not explain transfers, sales, deaths, hiring, residence changes, or household composition. No estate inventory was found to connect particular individuals to Hall at his death.

    Death, burial, and probate

    Huxford gives Sion's death year as 1849. The modern Hall Cemetery marker also reads "SION HALL / 1784-1849". Find a Grave supplies November 14, 1849, but that day does not appear on the photographed stone and no contemporary notice or probate record was found. The year is strong; the exact date is unresolved.[4][13]

    The earliest located burial descriptions are from 1885 and 1886. They place Sion's grave under large trees or in a grove by his mill creek. Together with the Hall Cemetery memorial, they make burial in the Hall home-and-mill neighborhood near Morven strong probable. They do not establish that the modern marker occupies the exact original grave.[14][21]

    Searches found no will, administration, inventory, estate division, obituary, death notice, or administrator advertisement. The Ancestry Georgia wills-and-probate index displayed no Lowndes County result for Sion, but that was a name-index search, not a page-by-page review of county volumes. Newspapers.com searches for 1840–1870 also produced no death or estate notice. These are documented negatives, not proof that no estate proceeding existed.

    Public roles that are proved, probable, or unsupported

    Proved or strongly supported

    • Irwin petit-jury panel selection in 1820, from a published court-record transcription.[17]
    • Irwin road commissioner in 1824, from a published court-record transcription.[18]
    • Member of the 1825 Lowndes public-site commission, from Huxford's specific act-based account.[4]
    • His house's statutory use for Lowndes courts and elections in 1825–1826 and as a justice-court place in 1829.[1][2]
    • A legal/property interest with Enoch in Lot 271 and operating saw and grist mills on the levied property by 1846.[4][5][6]

    Unsupported or contradicted

    • Judicial office. The court met at Hall's house; no record found makes Hall a judge or justice.
    • Militia rank or Indian-war service. No commission, muster, pay roll, pension, bounty file, or roster naming this Sion was located.
    • Legislative service. The May 1924 centennial article says Sion "was a member of the Legislature" and that Enoch "followed him"; it does not name a county, office, or term. Service for Lowndes County sometime between its creation in 1825 and Sion's death in 1849 is therefore chronologically possible, but no election return, legislative roster, or official journal located in this project proves it. Hamilton W. Sharpe's 1884 recollection instead names William A. Knight and Benjamin Sermons as Lowndes County's first legislative delegation, so Sion was probably not one of the county's first legislators. Three derivative September 1933 articles changed the story by calling the elder Sion Brooks County's first representative and Enoch the second. That version is impossible: Sion died in 1849, nine years before Brooks County was created.[3][4][13][23][24]
    • A Hall-owned store, liquor bar, or licensed tavern.
    • A personal 1820 land-lottery draw. Lot 271's survey history and Hall's 1820 census presence do not establish how he acquired the property.

    Evidence summary

    Verified or strong

    • Sion Hall was in original Irwin County by 1820 and in Lowndes County in 1830 and 1840.[9][10][11]
    • His house was the legally designated temporary Lowndes court-and-election site.[1][2]
    • He and Enoch held an interest in Lot 271, which had operating saw and grist mills by 1846.[4][5][6]
    • Enoch was his son.[4][14]
    • An 1817 Telfair County marriage entry names Sion Hall and Elizabeth Coaker.[12]
    • Sion died in 1849 and was remembered as buried near the Hall home and mill.[4][13][21]

    Probable or moderate

    • The move to the Coffee Road/Lot 271 neighborhood occurred in 1823 or early 1824.[4][19][20]
    • Bridget or Beady was Enoch's mother.[4]
    • Hall's large house functioned as a public inn or travelers' lodging place.[4][21]
    • The current Hall Cemetery memorial identifies the original burial neighborhood.[13][14][21]

    Unresolved

    • Exact birth date and place; parentage; every spouse and child.
    • Whether the Coffee Road move occurred in 1823 or on January 20, 1824.
    • Whether the residence stood in Lot 270 or Lot 271.
    • The exact first Lowndes Superior Court date.
    • Exact death day, probate, estate distribution, and exact grave continuity.
    • Tavern or store ownership, military service, judicial office, and legislative service.

    Online research coverage and limits

    The project searched stored Brooks/Lowndes research first, then used authenticated Ancestry census, marriage, probate, and cemetery collections; Newspapers.com; Georgia Historic Newspapers; Internet Archive; Georgia Archives maps; the state land-lot service; Huxford's county history; public FamilySearch profile pages; Find a Grave; and selected image-backed local-history transcriptions. Searches used the listed name variants with Irwin, Lowndes, Brooks, Morven, Coffee Road, Mount Zion, Lot 270, Lot 271, court, election, jury, road, commissioner, mill, inn, tavern, store, militia, marriage, death, probate, estate, and cemetery.

    "Fully researched online" means the accessible online groups and known leads were searched and reconciled. It does not mean all courthouse or manuscript records are digitized. The 1820 and 1840 census images, original 1817 marriage entry, original Irwin jury and road minutes, exact 1825 and 1826 session-law pages, Lowndes probate volumes, and tax/deed chains for Lots 270 and 271 remain uninspected or unavailable. FamilySearch record images required authentication or were blocked, and some Ancestry and Newspapers.com links require subscriptions.

    Best next archival work

    1. Inspect the original Irwin Superior and Inferior Court minutes for the 1820 jury panel and July 1824 road order.
    2. Locate the original Georgia session laws of December 24, 1825 and November 30, 1826, including the commissioner list and exact court-removal language.
    3. Review Lowndes Inferior Court ordinary minutes, wills, bonds, inventories, appraisements, annual returns, and loose estates for 1848–1855, including filings under Enoch or another administrator.
    4. Trace Lots 270 and 271 through tax digests, deeds, plats, execution files, and the Vickers judgment to identify acreage, the sheriff-sale purchaser, the mill, and the house site.
    5. Search tavern licenses, merchant licenses, daybooks, and court expense accounts for Hall's inn and any store.
    6. Search Georgia militia commission books and 1836 rosters before assigning Hall any rank or service.
    7. Obtain a cemetery survey, church burial register, or older grave record for the Hall home-and-mill neighborhood.
    8. Use probate, deeds, church records, and later labor or Freedmen's Bureau records to look for names of people enslaved by the Hall household.
