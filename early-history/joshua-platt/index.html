    Rev. Joshua Platt of Early Lowndes County, Georgia

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Research scope and conclusion

    This report concerns the Reverend Joshua Platt whom Hamilton W. Sharpe remembered as one of the first seven tent occupants at the Mount Zion Methodist camp meeting in fall 1829. The accessible record supports a strong-probable identity chain from Bulloch County, Georgia, in 1820, through Lowndes County in 1830-1840, to Alachua County, Florida, in 1850. No single record explicitly joins all three places, so the chain is not presented as absolute fact.[2-6]

    The public life is clearer than the private genealogy. Original censuses and tax digests place Joshua in early Lowndes County and document changing landholdings and enslaved labor. Sharpe's participant recollection calls him "Rev. Joshua Platt"; a later Methodist biographical register identifies him as a local Methodist Episcopal preacher and reports an 1839 deacon election.[7-14] Original Florida newspaper notices prove that a Joshua Platt of Hernando County was dead before January 27, 1855, that John Platt administered his estate, and that five unnamed enslaved people belonging to the estate were advertised for sale in January 1857. The attribution of that estate to the earlier Lowndes and Alachua man is strong probable, not directly stated in the notices.[15-16]

    The often-repeated 1770 birth, Adam Platt parentage, marriages to Margaret Fussell and Amy Newell, complete child list, March 1855 death, and Williams Cemetery dates remain derivative or unproved. The March 1855 death claim is contradicted by administration notices dated January 27, 1855.[15,19-20] A photographed marker clearly reads "REV. JOSHUA PLATT", but its lower inscription is not legible enough in the preserved image to verify dates.[18]

    Identity and migration

    The 1820 Bulloch County census index records Joshua Platt as a household head and reports five free people in all. Its extracted age fields appear to describe six people: one man forty-five or older, one man sixteen through eighteen, three girls under sixteen, and one woman twenty-six through forty-four. The preserved manuscript page is too faint to resolve that mismatch confidently. One household member was reported in agriculture. The name and place support a possible or strong-probable earlier appearance, but this conflicted household extraction is not treated as a clean identity bridge.[2]

    In 1830, Joshua headed a four-person Lowndes County household: a man and woman each aged fifty through fifty-nine, a girl ten through fourteen, and a young woman fifteen through nineteen. No enslaved person was recorded.[4]

    The 1840 Lowndes schedule advances the older couple by one decade. It records a man and woman each sixty through sixty-nine, one man twenty through twenty-nine, two young women fifteen through nineteen, and one enslaved Black woman aged twenty-four through thirty-five. Two household members worked in agriculture.[5]

    The 1850 Alachua County, Florida, population schedule records Joshua Platt, eighty, North Carolina-born farmer, and Amy Platt, seventy-five, also born in North Carolina, in dwelling and family 92.[6] The age progression, rare exact name, occupation, household pattern, and southward movement make the Lowndes-to-Alachua identification strong probable. The census did not state relationships, so Amy is a strong-probable wife, not a relationship directly proved by the schedule.

    An 1810 Duplin County, North Carolina, Joshua Platt headed a nine-person household, and an 1804 Duplin land-entry card records warrant 1485 for ten acres on a drain of Island Creek. The card says "Entered 13th Feb. 1804"; its grant-number and issue fields are blank.[1,3] Those are plausible earlier appearances, but neither record connects its Joshua to Bulloch or Lowndes. The unresolved 1820 extraction also prevents a clean household-composition comparison. These appearances remain possible, not settled.

    Namesakes and rejected mergers

    The elder must remain separate from Joshua Platt, about twenty-two and Georgia-born, in John and Eliss Platt's Putnam County, Florida, household in 1850. That younger man continues in later Florida records as Joshua A. or Joshua Aaron Platt. Records and family trees attached to the younger man cannot be assigned automatically to the minister.[21]

    Samuel Platt also headed a separate Lowndes household in 1830. Peter and John Platt appear near Joshua in census or later estate contexts, but proximity and surname do not prove their relationships.[4-5]

    An 1827 Georgia land-lottery index names a Joshua Platt of Underwood's District, Carroll County, as the drawer of District 12, Lot 43 in Irwin County. That Carroll residence is not explicitly connected to the dossier subject, and Lot 43 does not appear in the minister's inspected Lowndes tax sequence. The lottery winner therefore remains unresolved rather than assigned to him.[17]

    Mount Zion and Methodist ministry

    Hamilton W. Sharpe's "Scenes and Incidents of Our Early History", printed April 12, 1884, is the closest located narrative witness to Mount Zion's beginning. Sharpe wrote, "It was I think as early as the fall of 1829", and named "Rev. Joshua Platt" among the seven first tent occupants with William Blair, William Hendry, John Hendry, H. W. Sharpe, John Pike, and Lawrence Folsom.[7]

    Sharpe wrote about fifty-five years after the event and explicitly signaled uncertainty with "I think". His original newspaper page reads 1829. Folks Huxford later printed 1828, and a modern historical marker repeats that year.[22] The dossier therefore reports fall 1829 as Sharpe's wording and preserves 1828 as a later variant.

    Sharpe proves only how a participant remembered Platt: a reverend and early tenter in the Methodist Episcopal gathering. The article does not say Platt selected the site, preached the first sermon, owned the campground, or held office there. Platt is absent from Huxford's original-based transcription of the November 8, 1831 Mount Zion deed, whose trustees were William Blair, William Hendry, Lawrence Folsom, James J. Joyce, and William G. Hall.[22] He should not be called an 1831 Mount Zion deed trustee; any later trustee service is unresolved.

    Harold Lawrence's 1984 biographical register gives the clearest ministerial entry: "PLATT, JOSHUA (MEC) (L) GEORGIA CONFERENCE; 1839 Deacon (E)." Lawrence defines MEC as Methodist Episcopal Church before the 1844 division, "L" as local preacher, and "E" as elected.[8] This is strong secondary evidence that Platt was a local Methodist Episcopal preacher elected deacon in 1839.

    The original conference action or ordination certificate was not located. Searches of the published Methodist annual-conference minutes for 1773-1839 found no Joshua Platt appointment.[9-11] Lawrence explains that local preachers seldom appeared in printed conference minutes before 1850. The combined evidence distinguishes local ministry from an itinerant circuit appointment; no specific circuit or annual appointment has been proved.

    Taxed land and local setting

    Original Lowndes tax digests place Joshua in the county from 1830 through 1840, but a tax return does not by itself identify a dwelling or complete deed chain.[12-14]

    • In 1830 he returned one poll, 632.5 acres of pine land and sixty acres of first-quality oak-and-hickory land, including District 12, Lot 107 in Lowndes and a Carroll County tract described as District 12, Lot 143.[12]
    • In 1834-1835, under "Joshua Plate" in one index, he returned a Lowndes tract associated with District 12, Lot 335 and a forty-acre Cherokee tract. The 1835 Cherokee district numeral is difficult to read and should not be treated as a perfected legal description.[13]
    • In 1838-1840 he returned a Lowndes tract associated with District 12, Lot 339 and another forty-acre Cherokee entry.[14]

    Georgia's official land-lot GIS places Lots 107, 335, and 339 within the present Brooks County landscape.[23] That locates the taxed parcels broadly. It does not prove which tract contained his house, when title passed, or whether every acreage line represented full ownership.

    No located record names Joshua as a Lowndes justice, sheriff, clerk, tax officer, legislator, militia officer, or soldier. A Savannah newspaper does place a Joshua Platt among Bulloch County grand jurors in April 1821; if the identity chain is correct, this is a strong-probable civic appearance, not a separate proof that the juror later moved to Lowndes.[24]

    In June 1847, the Southern Recorder printed an equity notice in "Thomas J. S. Farr v. Joshua Platt". The Lowndes sheriff reported that Platt could not be found in the county, and the court ordered publication.[25] This directly establishes the suit and his absence when service was attempted. It does not establish permanent residence elsewhere, but it is consistent with departure before the 1850 Florida census.

    Enslaved people

    The 1830 census records no enslaved person in Joshua's household. The 1840 census records one unnamed enslaved Black woman aged twenty-four through thirty-five.[4-5]

    The 1850 Alachua slave schedule records six unnamed Black people under Joshua Platt's ownership:[6]

    • a woman aged thirty-five;
    • a man aged twenty;
    • a girl aged thirteen;
    • boys aged ten and eight; and
    • a girl aged three.

    The inspected Lowndes tax rows do not supply enslaved-person counts, including the 1840 return. That difference should not be used to erase the federal census entry; tax-day and census-day records were separate instruments.[12-14]

    The estate advertised five unnamed enslaved people for sale in January 1857: "Two likely young men, one girl, and two children."[16] The newspaper does not identify them or explain why the estate count was one lower than the 1850 schedule. No names or defensible person-to-person linkage can be recovered from these records.

    Family

    Amy Platt's 1850 co-residence and similar age make her Joshua's strong-probable wife.[6] The original schedule calls her only Amy Platt. "Newell" appears in derivative trees and alternate indexing, not in the census manuscript.[19-20]

    John Platt directly appears as administrator for the Hernando County Joshua in the 1855-1856 notices.[15-16] Those notices do not call him a son, and their attribution to the dossier subject is strong probable rather than explicit. Find a Grave links John Wesley Platt and Peter Platt as children, while public trees add other names.[19-20] Without an original heirs list, will, Bible, marriage record, parental probate, or relationship-bearing church record, John and Peter should not be promoted from derivative or circumstantial kinship to proved sons.

    No located original record proves Joshua's parents, Amy's maiden name, an earlier wife, either marriage, or a complete child list. The popular "Joshua Adam Platt" construction is a tree label, not a name established in an original record inspected for this report.

    Death, administration, and estate

    The Florida Peninsular of February 17, 1855, printed two notices signed by John Platt and dated January 27. One sought authority from the "Judge of the Southern Circuit Court ... sitting in Hernando County" to sell the real estate of "Joshua Platt, late of said County, deceased"; the other directed creditors and legatees of "Joshua Platt, late of Hernando County, deceased" to present authenticated claims. Both identify John as administrator.[15]

    These notices prove that the Hernando County decedent died before January 27, 1855. His identification as the earlier Lowndes and Alachua man is strong probable. The notices do not give an exact date, cause, or relationship to John. They also contradict the March 1855 death attributed to this same man in derivative sources.[19-20]

    On November 1, 1856, the newspaper announced a final accounting before the Hernando probate judge and a public sale at the old Hernando courthouse on the first Monday in January 1857. The sale notice offered the five unnamed enslaved people belonging to Joshua's estate.[16] An adjacent debt notice instead called Joshua "late of Hillsborough County". The newspaper itself therefore contains a Hernando/Hillsborough conflict. Because the real-estate, accounting, and sale notices use Hernando, the safest conclusion is that the estate was administered there while preserving the contrary wording.

    No online will, full estate packet, inventory, heirs list, distribution, or complete deed proceeding was located. The notices prove administration and advertised actions, not that every proposed sale occurred exactly as planned.

    Burial

    Find a Grave memorial 65687643 places Rev. Joshua Platt in Williams Cemetery near Dade City and reports 1770-1855.[18-19] The preserved marker photograph clearly shows "REV. JOSHUA PLATT". Its lower inscription is too faint to read confidently, so the image does not independently verify the dates.

    The photographed marker and memorial location make Williams Cemetery a strong-probable burial identification. The exact birth and death dates remain unproved. A cemetery placement is also not proof that modern Dade City was the historical place of death.

    Chronology

    • 1804: a possible Joshua Platt entered warrant 1485 for ten acres in Duplin County, North Carolina; the preserved card has no grant number or issue date.[1]
    • 1810: possible appearance as a Duplin household head.[3]
    • 1820: strong-probable appearance as a Bulloch County, Georgia, household head.[2]
    • April 1821: a Joshua Platt served on a Bulloch grand jury; strong probable if the identity chain holds.[24]
    • Fall 1829: Sharpe later remembered Rev. Joshua Platt among Mount Zion's first seven tenters.[7]
    • 1830: Lowndes household of four; no enslaved people; original tax return includes District 12, Lot 107.[4,12]
    • 1834-1835: tax returns associate him with District 12, Lot 335 and a forty-acre Cherokee tract.[13]
    • 1838-1840: tax returns associate him with District 12, Lot 339 and a Cherokee tract.[14]
    • 1839: Lawrence's register reports election as a local Methodist Episcopal deacon.[8]
    • 1840: Lowndes household of five free people and one enslaved woman.[5]
    • May-June 1847: sheriff could not find Joshua in Lowndes for service in an equity case.[25]
    • 1850: Joshua, eighty, and Amy, seventy-five, in Alachua County; six unnamed enslaved people.[6]
    • Before January 27, 1855: The Hernando County Joshua died; John Platt was administrator. Attribution to the dossier subject is strong probable.[15]
    • November 1856: Final accounting and a January 1857 estate sale for that decedent were announced.[16]
    • January 1857: scheduled sale of five unnamed enslaved people at the old Hernando courthouse.[16]
    • 1884: Sharpe published his Mount Zion recollection.[7]
    • 1984: Lawrence published the local-preacher and 1839 deacon entry.[8]

    Search coverage and remaining targets

    No original birth, parental, marriage, ordination, complete child, will, estate-distribution, exact death-date, or fully legible grave-date record was found. No itinerant appointment, military service, or Lowndes public office was located. Google Books quota exhaustion and unavailable web/browser handlers restricted parts of the broad web layer; those failures mean "not found in the accessible search", not "no record existed".

    The main remaining targets are:

    1. the original 1839 Georgia Conference local-deacon action or ordination certificate;
    2. complete Duplin, Bulloch, Lowndes, Alachua, Hernando, and Hillsborough deed/probate packets;
    3. an heirs list, Bible, marriage record, or church register proving family relationships;
    4. a readable high-resolution marker image or cemetery register;
    5. a complete title chain for Lots 107, 335, and 339; and
    6. a record explicitly linking the Georgia and Florida appearances.

    The findings describe accessible online evidence checked through August 19, 2026. They do not imply that undigitized courthouse, church, cemetery, conference, family, or private records do not exist.
