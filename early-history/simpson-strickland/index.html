    Simpson Strickland

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Evidence assessment

    Simpson Strickland is documented in Lowndes County by 1826. Original tax returns place land under his name in the part of Lowndes that became Brooks County, and the 1860 census gives his post office as Tallokas. His exact arrival year, parents, marriages, and death remain incompletely documented. He is a possible namesake of Strickland Road, not a proved one.

    Early record and public service

    An original deed copied into Brooks County Deed Book A records a June 16, 1826 conveyance from Miles or Milus Thigpen to John Hendry. Simpson Strickland and Simeon Strickland witnessed it. On July 23, 1837, Simpson swore that he saw Thigpen execute the deed and saw Simeon sign. The deed establishes their association, but does not call them brothers.[1]

    Contemporary Lowndes grand-jury presentments for the May 1833 term name Simpson among the jurors. The June 19 newspaper printing has an 1833 heading and a June 12 footer, supporting the date independently of a conflicting footer in a later transcription.[2]

    Folks Huxford identifies Simpson as a Lowndes Inferior Court justice in 1829–1830 and as captain of the 790th Militia District beginning September 3, 1834. Huxford identifies the later district as Tallokas, but cautions that the original district was larger and its boundaries cannot be recovered from the surviving county minutes. The commission itself remains uninspected. A tax-book heading reading “Capt Stricklands” supplies neither a first name nor independent proof of the commission date.[3][7]

    Households and family

    The original 1830 Lowndes census lists a Simpson Strickland household with six free white members; the 1840 front schedule lists eight. These schedules name only the household head, and their reverse sides have not been inspected.[4]

    In 1850, Simpson was a 44-year-old South Carolina-born farmer in Lowndes County, reporting $1,000 in real estate. Household 838 includes Mary, 40; Susan, Henry, Esther, Redden, Simpson S., Joel P., Mary, William, and Nancy. The younger Simpson S. is 10, not 15 as indexed. The final three names appear on the continuation page. The census does not state their relationships to the household head.[5]

    The 1860 Brooks census places Simpson, 53, a South Carolina-born farmer, in household 547 under Tallokas post office. Martha, 33, also born in South Carolina, appears next, followed by Joel, Mary, William, Nancy, Sarah A., and Louiza. The household continues with Ella, Eunice I., and Stephen Godwin, a 17-year-old hireling. The first page is dated August 10; the continuation bears July 7. That heading discrepancy does not obscure the household continuation, but should not be silently corrected.[6]

    Tallokas is a postal designation here, not a land-lot description or proof that the family lived in the village or at modern Pavo. The change from Mary in 1850 to Martha in 1860 does not itself prove either marriage or Mary's death.

    Later accounts identify Mary as Mary Wooten, married to Simpson in 1829, and name Archibald and Luander Strickland as Simpson's parents and Simeon as his brother. These relationships remain secondary-supported. A derivative marriage index repeats the 1829 marriage but is not an original county marriage record. Later Simpson S. and Simpson J. Stricklands must not be merged with the older man without connecting evidence.[3][13]

    Land in the tax returns

    Original Lowndes tax volumes provide several distinct property references:[7]

    • 1834 and 1835: land in District 12, Lots 465 and 466.
    • 1838: 490 acres in Lot 233, District 12.
    • 1840: 490 acres each in Lots 274 and 233, District 12. The heading is Captain Davis's District. This return supports Lot 233, not Lot 234.
    • 1844: 1,470 acres in District 12, with the lot-number column blank. The acreage cannot safely be assigned to three particular lots.

    The returns also list 250-acre entries in District 8 of Early or Baker County. Their numbers differ: 372 in 1830, 1834, 1838, and 1840; a probable 373 in 1835; and 272 in 1844. The differences may involve separate parcels or recording problems, but no explanation has been established. The 1830 row also combines Lowndes and Lee county references with district and lot numbers whose allocation is ambiguous. These entries should not be treated as a continuous title chain.

    Tax returns establish that land was returned under Simpson's name. They are not deeds and do not prove that he lived on every tract.

    Debt proceedings and later deed references

    The Southern Recorder of November 30, 1852 advertised levies on Lots 66 and 120 in District 15, each described as 490 acres with improvements, and separately on Lot 274 in District 12, described as well improved. The notices identify the land as Simpson Strickland's property and cite two Lowndes Superior Court executions in favor of Mitchell B. Jones, executor. The common debtor, creditor, court, and Lot 274 connection strongly support identifying the debtor as this Simpson, although the notices do not call him captain.[8]

    These are advertisements, not proof that the sheriff completed the sales. The outcome matters because a reconstructed deed index later records Simpson conveying 580 acres in Lots 233 and 274 to James R. Edmondson and Thomas M. Heath on July 15, 1858. That instrument is in Lowndes reconstructed Deed Book A, pages 88–89, not Brooks Book A. Its full text remains uninspected; an index note mentioning promissory notes does not by itself establish a mortgage.[9]

    A separate index reference, Lowndes Book A, page 357, names Simpson as grantor to John S. Pinkston of Columbia County, Florida, for District 15 land and $200. The available extraction gives neither date nor lot number. It remains a lead, not a transaction securely assigned to either of the two District 15 lots.[9]

    Enslaved people and the Swain estate

    The original 1850 slave schedule lists four unnamed enslaved people under Simpson: a woman aged 21, girls aged five and one, and a boy aged four. It supplies no names or relationships. A later account's prose count of three conflicts with the four entries in the original.[10]

    The November 1852 newspaper also advertises a levy on Jane, an enslaved girl about seventeen, as Simpson's property under the same creditor's executions. Her later fate is not established.[8]

    In an August 23, 1851 notice, Simpson applied for letters of administration on the estate of Susannah Swain, a deceased minor of Lowndes County. A later notice, dated July 27, 1852, names Redden Wooten as administrator and advertises the estate's sale of an enslaved girl named Jane. Simpson's application therefore cannot be treated as proof that he ultimately administered the estate. Neither notice proves kinship. Nor does the shared name establish that the estate's Jane was the girl later levied on as Simpson's property.[11][12]

    Strickland Road and Barney

    The 1838 and 1840 tax entries, the 1852 Lot 274 notice, and the 1858 deed index establish a substantial land association in District 12 before the documented use of the road name in 1876. They make Simpson a plausible candidate for the name, but none says that the road was named for him. Other Stricklands cannot be excluded. See Early roads before Barney for the road evidence and its separate western and eastern references.

    The records do not establish his residence at the later Barney townsite, a specific tract near Pavo, or a proved line of descent to a Barney census household.

    Remaining questions

    The original militia commission, marriage records, parent-naming evidence, exact death and burial records, full Lowndes deeds, and final sheriff-sale returns remain to be examined. No confirmed 1870 record has been linked to this Simpson; a suggested Florida household should not be accepted on a similar name alone.
