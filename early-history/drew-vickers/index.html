    Drewry “Drew” Vickers of Early Lowndes County, Georgia

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Research dossier prepared: August 18, 2026
    Subject: The man recorded as Drury, Drewey, Drewy, and Drew Vickers
    Scope: Comprehensive online survey as of August 18, 2026, covering digitized newspapers, census schedules, land and court evidence, local histories, genealogy claims, namesake controls, and unresolved research questions

    Executive finding

    Drewry “Drew” Vickers was an early settler of the part of original Irwin County that became Lowndes County, Georgia. Later histories consistently include him among the county’s first four permanent white settlers. The earliest located account that could be examined directly is William Harden’s 1913 history; Folks Huxford repeated the attribution with an 1821 date in 1922. This is a secondary-supported traditional attribution. No located contemporary record proves the quartet claim, the reported 1815 scouting expedition, or an exact 1821 family move.

    Contemporary records nevertheless provide a substantial outline of his life. A Drury Vickers was a fortunate land-lottery drawer from Montgomery County in 1820. A probable identity chain traces a man of this name from Montgomery County that year to Lowndes County in 1830, 1840, and 1850. Newspapers document old-Irwin land, Lowndes Superior Court judgments, and a transaction connecting Drew with Lewis Vickers between 1833 and 1848. In 1850, Drew was about seventy-four, born in North Carolina, farming in Lowndes County, and living with Sarah Vickers. The census and slave schedules establish that he enslaved people in 1820, 1830, and 1850.

    His exact death date is unknown. The frequently copied 1845 date is false for this man because he appears in the 1850 census and in an 1848 court notice. No probate file, death notice, cemetery register, or photographed grave marker was found. The defensible conclusion is that he died sometime after the 1850 enumeration.

    Evidence standards used in this dossier

    • Verified: read in an original or indexed-original image, or in a contemporary newspaper page.
    • Strong probable: supported by several compatible records, but identity or interpretation still requires an inference.
    • Secondary-supported: stated in a later history, transcription, memorial, or compiled genealogy with useful detail but no inspected original proof.
    • Unresolved: plausible, but the evidence located online does not establish it.
    • Contradicted: inconsistent with a stronger original record.

    Negative findings mean only that no record was found in the documented searches. They do not prove that a record never existed.

    Identity and name variants

    The early records use several spellings:

    Form Record context
    Drury Vickers 1793 militia-roll transcription; 1820 land-lottery list; 1820 census alternate reading; 1833 tax sale; 1845-1846 court notices
    Drewey Vickers 1820 census indexing
    Drewy Vickers 1840 census and land/tax indexing
    Drew Vickers 1830 and 1850 censuses; 1848 court notice; later histories
    Drewry Vickers Later genealogies, memorials, and land-index references

    The name, geography, age, and chronology make these records a coherent probable identity chain. No located record calls the pioneer Andrew Vickers, and “Drew” should not be expanded to Andrew. No evidence supports a first initial J; the subject named in the historical sources is Drew or Drury Vickers.

    Several later men had the same or similar name. In particular, Florida probate and Indian War records belong to a Drewry Vickers born about 1820, not to the North Carolina-born pioneer who was already about seventy-four in 1850. A Drew Vickers born about 1831 in the 1860 Berrien County census and William Drew Vickers and other later Hahira-area men must also be kept separate.

    1899–1922 R. E. L. Folsom, Harden, and Huxford publish differing settlement chronologies. Essential historical traditions, but written decades after the events.

    Was he one of Lowndes County’s first four settlers?

    Later histories consistently include Drew among the four settlers, but no located contemporary record proves the quartet claim or fixes its date. The appropriate grade is secondary-supported traditional attribution; the exact group, permanence, geography, and dates remain unproved.

    William Harden’s 1913 A History of Savannah and South Georgia is the earliest located account that could be examined directly and names the quartet. In a biography of the Folsom family, Harden says Lawrence Folsom joined James Rountree, Drew Vickers, and Alfred Belote on an 1815 exploration. It says the men invested in government land, returned for their families, and became the first permanent white settlers of Lowndes County, especially its northern portion. Harden gives no contemporary citation for the expedition.

    Folks Huxford’s 1922 county sketch supplies the familiar precise date: “In 1821, four settlers moved to what is now Lowndes county.” He names Rountree, Folsom, Vickers, and Belote with their families. Huxford said his account relied chiefly on recent Valdosta Times sketches by Hortense W. Baker. Those underlying sketches were not located.

    Two earlier local accounts complicate the chronology:

    • An 1889 biography says Drew settled his “Coffee county road” lot in 1824.
    • R. E. L. Folsom wrote in 1899 that Washington Joyce established the first white settlement in present Lowndes County at the Miller Bridge crossing in 1824, and that Drew Vickers and Lawrence Folsom came next.

    These statements are not necessarily irreconcilable. “Original Lowndes,” “present Lowndes,” an exploratory trip, a legal land entry, and permanent occupation can refer to different events and geographies. They do show why an unqualified claim that all four families permanently settled present-day Lowndes in 1821 goes beyond the evidence now available.

    The safest wording is:

    Drew Vickers was one of the four men traditionally credited as the first permanent white settler group in northern original Lowndes County. Contemporary evidence places Drury Vickers in the regional record by 1820 and securely in Lowndes County by 1830; the reported 1815 expedition and exact 1821 family migration come from later histories.

    Origin, age, and migration

    The 1850 census is the best age-and-origin evidence found. It records Drew at about seventy-four and born in North Carolina, implying a birth around 1776. The public image is readable but not ideal, so 1776 should remain an approximate year, not an exact birth date. No birth, baptism, or parental record was located.

    The 1889 profile agrees on North Carolina and says he came to Georgia soon after the Revolutionary War. The 1793 Washington County militia transcription is geographically compatible with that account. By 1820 he was enumerated in Montgomery County, while a court transcription may place him on an Irwin County jury panel that same year. He was in Lowndes by the 1830 census.

    The records do not reveal whether the apparent Montgomery/Irwin overlap reflects a recent move, differing civil boundaries or registrations, or two men. The later record chain strongly favors one pioneer moving through the south-central Georgia frontier, but it should not be treated as a single-record proof.

    Household and family

    Sarah Vickers

    The 1850 population schedule places Sarah Vickers, about sixty and born in North Carolina, immediately with Drew in the same household. This is direct proof of co-residence and strong evidence that she was his wife. The census does not state relationships or maiden names.

    Find a Grave, WikiTree, and other compiled genealogies call her Sarah Elvington or Yelvington. No inspected marriage record, deed, Bible, probate, or death record gives that maiden name. Therefore:

    • Sarah as Drew’s wife: strong probable.
    • Sarah Elvington/Yelvington as her full identity: secondary-supported, not independently verified.

    Proposed children

    Public genealogies commonly list Lewis, Nancy, Henry, and Matthew Vickers; some add Benjamin. No located probate distribution, Bible, deed, guardianship record, or parent-naming vital record proves the complete child set.

    Proposed child Best evidence located Assessment
    Lewis Vickers A modern account of the 1827 land lottery calls Lewis Drew’s son. The 1848 sheriff’s notice says Lewis pointed out property in a judgment held by Drew. Strongest proposed child, but still not directly proved as a son.
    Nancy Vickers Fuquay Repeated in WikiTree and compiled genealogy. Secondary-supported only.
    Henry Vickers Linked in WikiTree and Find a Grave. Henry’s 1859 Brooks County estate record, as inspected, does not name Drew. Plausible, unresolved.
    Matthew Vickers Repeated in compiled genealogy; appears in the broader Vickers network. Secondary-supported only.
    Benjamin Vickers Appears in some reconstructions but not all child lists. Possible and disputed.

    The 1889 profile says William and Drew Vickers, then living on the old “Coffee county road” lot, were the pioneer’s grandsons. This is useful family testimony, but it supplies no intermediate parent.

    Proposed parents and sibling

    Modern trees sometimes identify Joshua Vickers as Drew’s father. The 1793 militia transcription lists Joshua immediately after Drury in Captain Parrott’s Company, but it states no relationship. Joshua could have been a father, brother, cousin, or unrelated same-surname neighbor. Drew’s parents remain unknown.

    Huxford called Rachel Vickers Folsom Drew’s sister. A 1938 Folsom genealogy instead called Rachel a daughter of Jonathan Vickers. No reviewed original record names Drew and Rachel as children of the same parent. Their proposed sibling relationship remains unresolved.

    The line Drew → Henry Vickers → Edna Vickers Ryall is not proved at its oldest link. Edna’s death record separately identifies Henry Vickers and Linnie Bradford as her parents, but no located original record makes that Henry a son of Drew. The dossier does not present Drew as a proved direct ancestor of the Barney families through Henry.

    Census households and slavery

    1820 Montgomery County

    The census records Drewey Vickers, with Drury as an alternate reading. The indexed original schedule reports twelve people: seven free white household members and five enslaved people. The free household consisted of one male forty-five or older, two males aged ten through fifteen, two males under ten, one female aged twenty-six through forty-four, and one female aged ten through fifteen. The enslaved people were recorded only by age and sex, not by name.

    This record proves neither the names of Drew’s family members nor the later compiled child list.

    1830 Lowndes County

    The census records Drew Vickers in Lowndes County with fifteen people total, seven of whom were enslaved. The schedule does not name other free or enslaved members of the household.

    1840 Lowndes County

    Ancestry indexes Drewy Vickers in Lowndes County and supplies original image identifiers. The original-image locator was independently confirmed. Because the columns could not be read reliably at the available resolution, no exact 1840 household or slavery total is asserted here.

    1850 Lowndes County

    The population schedule records Drew at about seventy-four, Sarah at about sixty, both born in North Carolina, and Drew as a farmer. The image appears to value his real estate at about $2,000, but that figure should be confirmed from a full-resolution scan before being quoted as exact.

    The separate slave schedule visibly contains five consecutive enslaved-person entries under Drew’s name. The people were not named, and the available public image was not clear enough for a responsible age-by-age transcription.

    Land and property

    1820 lottery

    The Georgia Journal lists Drury Vickers under Montgomery County among fortunate drawers in Georgia’s 1820 land lottery. This proves a successful draw but does not identify the parcel on the newspaper page.

    District 13 land in old Irwin

    A 1833 Thomas County sheriff’s notice gives the strongest directly inspected land description. It levied on Drury’s 490-acre Lot 389 in the 13th District of original Irwin, then Thomas County, for his 1833 tax. Ownership or taxable responsibility is established; personal residence on the lot is not.

    A public historical KML separately says a state grant issued to Drewry Vickers of Montgomery County on November 11, 1830, for Lot 397 in District 13. The relevant placemark is headed “LL 397. LD 13. Vickers.” The official Georgia land-lot layer identifies the parcel as feature ID 103844, object 423625, a 490-acre original-Irwin lottery lot now in Thomas County. The grant image was not inspected, and Lot 397 must not be confused with the newspaper-proved Lot 389.

    Lowndes-area lots

    The public KML places Drewy or Drewry Vickers in Lowndes tax lists or a plat involving Lots 179, 181, 182, 187, and 188 from 1830 through 1844. The relevant placemarks are headed “LL 181, 188. LD 12. Vickers,” “LL 187. LD 12. Vickers,” and “LL 179, 182. LD 11. Vickers.” They report:

    • appearances on Lots 181 and 188 in 1830 and 1838;
    • an August 30, 1844 plat associated with those lots;
    • appearances on Lot 187 in 1838 and 1840;
    • appearances on Lots 179 and 182 in 1840.

    The index has a material internal conflict: its heading calls Lots 181 and 188 part of District 12, while detailed text calls them District 11. Both districts contain lots with those numbers. Find a Grave repeats the District 12 version without citing a deed. The lot and district cannot be settled from those derivatives alone.

    Tax-list placement can show a taxable interest without proving fee-simple ownership or residence. No inspected deed proves that Drew acquired Lawrence Folsom’s interest in Lot 187. The original tax digest pages, 1844 plat, state grant, and later deed recitals are priority records.

    Courts and civic activity

    The newspaper record shows Drew as an active creditor in Lowndes Superior Court:

    • In 1845 and 1846, sheriff’s-sale notices enforced a judgment in Drury Vickers v. Enoch Hall, with Sion Hall and John Hall as securities on stay. The levied property was Lot 271, District 12; the 1846 notice described a working saw and grist mill.
    • In 1848, another notice enforced Drew Vickers v. Asabell Rentfroe against the Rentfroe place. Lewis Vickers pointed out the property.

    These notices prove participation in civil litigation as a judgment creditor. They do not disclose the underlying debts or disputes.

    A modern transcription says Drew was selected for Irwin County’s first petit-jury panel in September 1820. It also says the panel was not called when court met the following March, so this is selection rather than proof that he heard a case.

    One modern local page claimed Drew served on the 1833 Lowndes grand jury. The published roster does not name him. That claim is contradicted and should not be repeated.

    No record was found showing Drew as a justice, sheriff, legislator, commissioner, militia officer, or church trustee.

    Militia service

    An online transcription titled “Muster Rolls for Washington County, GA 1789–1793” lists Drury Vickers and Joshua Vickers consecutively in Captain Parrott’s Company in 1793. Washington County then covered territory later organized into counties including Montgomery, where a man of the same name appears in 1820. The identification is geographically and chronologically plausible.

    Because the page is a modern transcription and supplies no original muster image or archive call number, the service is secondary-supported and unverified. It does not establish an officer’s rank, a pension, War of 1812 service, or a relationship between Drury and Joshua.

    Farming, stock raising, and the sugar-cane story

    The 1889 Valdosta Times life sketch, as reprinted in the Savannah Morning News, says Drew was the first white man to settle on the “Coffee county road” and that he introduced sugar-cane cultivation to the neighborhood. It recounts that Hartwell Mitchell, moving toward Thomas County, stayed overnight and gave Drew “a dozen cane for seed.” The article separately says Mitchell had obtained seed at St. Marys. Drew banked the cane, planted it in spring, and later supplied neighbors.

    The article describes early wooden roller mills and cane juice boiled in wash-pots. It also calls Drew a farmer and stock raiser, describes his hunting habits and old-fashioned clothing, names “Handy” as the leader of his dog pack, and says he kept tame deer and wild turkeys. According to the story, he kept gold and silver money in gourds in his kitchen loft and remained alert for thieves.

    This is the earliest located narrative devoted to Drew. As a retrospective local account, it does not prove that he was the first person to grow sugar cane locally.

    Death, estate, and burial

    The last verified life record is the 1850 census. No age-compatible Drew, Drewry, Drewy, or Drury Vickers was found in the 1860 federal census. The available 1860 matches belong to younger namesakes.

    The 1889 profile says Drew “lived to be near 100 years old.” That recollection is not enough to calculate a death year, especially without an exact birth date. It may explain later genealogical estimates, but it does not override the absence of a death or probate record.

    No Georgia probate file, will, administration, estate distribution, obituary, funeral notice, church burial record, or cemetery register for the pioneer was found in the documented online searches. Find a Grave places him in Vickers Cemetery near Hahira, gives no death date, and has no grave photograph, marker transcription, or plot evidence. That placement remains derivative.

    The correct conclusion is:

    • Born: about 1776, North Carolina — supported by the 1850 census; exact date unproved.
    • Died: after the 1850 census — exact date and place unresolved.
    • Burial: Vickers Cemetery is a plausible tradition, not verified by a marker or register.
    • 1845 death claim: contradicted by direct records.

    Lowndes County’s early record losses matter. Pre-1858 deed books were destroyed, and the Ordinary’s office burned in 1869. Those losses may explain the missing direct land and probate trail, but surviving court minutes, state grants, tax digests, loose papers, and later deed recitals may still contain evidence.

    Namesake exclusions

    The following records were deliberately excluded from the pioneer’s biography:

    • Florida probate and Indian War records for a Drewry Vickers born about 1820.
    • The 1860 Berrien County Drew Vickers born about 1831.
    • Drew R. Vickers, born 1854 and died 1924.
    • William Drew Vickers and twentieth-century Hahira or Barney men.
    • Records for Andrew Vickers without independent proof that Andrew was an alias of Drury.

    These exclusions prevent the most serious error in the public search results: extending the pioneer’s life with records belonging to a younger Florida namesake.

    Research coverage and negative findings

    This investigation searched the following online record groups:

    • federal population and slave schedules;
    • Ancestry census, probate, military, and non-population indexes and available images;
    • Georgia Historic Newspapers, including exact-name searches across variant spellings;
    • Newspapers.com for the 1899 settlement account;
    • Internet Archive page images and OCR for Harden and Huxford;
    • public Georgia land-lot mapping and a historical Lowndes land/tax KML;
    • Find a Grave and Vickers Cemetery;
    • WikiTree and other compiled genealogy leads;
    • Ray City History Blog’s Drew/Drury, lottery, court, and early-settlement pages;
    • the archived Washington County militia-roll transcription.

    No contemporary record of the alleged 1815 expedition or exact 1821 family migration was located. The Hortense W. Baker sketches behind Huxford’s account were not found. No original 1793 muster image, 1820 Irwin jury minute image, Georgia probate for Drew, death notice, obituary, church record, cemetery register, or photographed marker was found. Exact newspaper searches were complicated by OCR reading “Drew” as a verb, and some subscription images were available only at limited resolution.

    2. Retrieve the original 1830 and 1838 Lowndes tax digests, the 1840 tax entries, and the August 30, 1844 Vickers plat.
    3. Obtain the state grant for District 13, Lot 397, and reconcile it with the 1833 tax-sale notice for Lot 389.

    Final assessment

    Surviving records provide more than Drew Vickers’s name on a pioneer list. They trace a North Carolina-born man of the Revolutionary generation from Montgomery County to the old Irwin–Lowndes frontier, where he farmed, enslaved people, held property, and pursued claims in court. An 1889 local biography remembered his stock raising, hunting, and distribution of seed cane to neighbors.

    The evidence does not establish his exact birth or death dates, parents, complete list of children, burial place, or probate history. The 1815 expedition and 1821 migration appear only in later accounts; the 1845 death date and 1833 grand-jury claim conflict with direct records. These limits should remain explicit.
