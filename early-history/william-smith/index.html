    William Smith of Franklinville, Troupville, and Valdosta

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Research scope and conclusion

    This dossier concerns the William Smith born in North Carolina about 1797-1800 who settled at Franklinville in early Lowndes County, served as a court clerk and postmaster, kept Tranquil Hall at Troupville and Valdosta, and died in Valdosta on February 1, 1882.

    The identity is unusually well anchored for an early Lowndes settler. Smith wrote his own account of the county's first courts in 1880. His original 1882 obituary then joined that witness to Franklinville, the clerkship, Troupville, Tranquil Hall, Valdosta, his wife, and his two sons. An Atlanta reprint confirms the contemporary circulation of the death account.[12] Federal registers, court notices, censuses, and slave schedules independently support the same chain.[1-11]

    The evidence also corrects or narrows several familiar claims. Smith was signing as Superior Court clerk by an 1828-era dateline, earlier than Huxford's 1830 commission date. He was Franklinville postmaster by September 30, 1829, but the often-repeated July 7, 1828 appointment was not recovered from an original federal record. The precise location of his Franklinville land, the full office chronology, his wife's formal given name, his probate, and his exact burial place remain unresolved.[6,13-17]

    Birth, migration, and first-hand recollection

    The 1850, 1860, 1870, and 1880 censuses consistently place Smith's birth in North Carolina and imply birth about 1797-1800.[3-6] His original obituary says he was born in North Carolina in 1797 and died in his eighty-fourth year.[9]

    Smith's own letter in the Valdosta Times appeared on December 11, 1880. Newspapers.com's metadata assigns the image to December 18, but the photographed masthead clearly reads December 11; the printed issue controls. Signing "W. S.", Smith recalled the first Lowndes Superior Court at Sion Hall's house in January 1827, the next court at Francis Rountree's, and the severe cold. He closed by saying that after the 1828 term he rode eight miles to his "lonely domicile at old Franklinville, where we had settled but a short time before".[1]

    The initials alone would be insufficient. The obituary identifies William Smith as the Franklinville settler who later wrote the first-court account, and Hamilton W. Sharpe's participant history names Smith as Franklinville's first settler.[9][18] That makes Smith's authorship and the basic settlement chain verified.

    Early households and the 1840 namesake problem

    The 1830 Lowndes census lists a William Smith household with a man aged 30-39, a woman 20-29, three boys under ten, and three other adult men. It records no enslaved people.[2] The head's age and place fit the subject, but the common name and unstated relationships make this a strong probable, not verified, assignment.

    Two Lowndes households were indexed under Wm Smith in 1840. The page 249 household is the stronger match. Its head and adult woman were both 40-49, matching the later William and Mary; it included three adolescent boys and other residents. Two enslaved people were counted: one boy under ten and one woman aged 24-35. The page 238 namesake had a man 30-39, woman 20-29, and boy under five, with one person in agriculture and no enslaved people.[2]

    The later evidence says Smith had sons William and Henry, but it does not name every person in either early household. The page 249 household is therefore strong probable, while page 238 is retained as a same-name control.

    Wife and sons

    The named census sequence is consistent:

    • In 1850 William, age 50 and a hotelkeeper, lived with Mary Smith, 50, Charles S. Rockwell, 28, Mary E. Rockwell, 18, and William Ashley, 26.[3]
    • In 1860 William, 60, was a hotelkeeper and postmaster at Troupville. Mary Smith, 59, Laura Smith, 9, and Mary Smith, 7, shared the household.[4]
    • In 1870 William, 73, kept a hotel at Valdosta. Mary N. Smith, 72, Laura C. Smith, 19, Mary N. Smith, 16, and five other residents shared the dwelling.[5]
    • In 1880 William, 80, was a retired hotelkeeper at Valdosta. The continuation page explicitly identifies his wife, age 80. Her written middle initial appears to be "P"; Ancestry indexes it as "A".[6]

    The pre-1880 schedules do not state relationships. The Rockwells, Laura, and younger Mary cannot be labeled daughters or grandchildren merely because they shared the household.

    The original 1882 obituary directly identifies two sons, William and Henry, both of whom had died after the Civil War leaving families. It says William Jr.'s family had all died, while Henry's widow, four children, and one or two grandchildren survived. No original birth, marriage, or probate record was found for either son.[9]

    William's wife presents a real naming conflict. The censuses call her Mary and Mary N.; the 1880 image appears to read Mary P., while Ancestry indexes Mary A. A January 1892 Valdosta death notice calls her "Mrs. Polly Smith", identifies her as Uncle Billy Smith's widow, and says she was a sister of David Hutchinson of Adel.[10] The age, place, widowhood, and unique husband identification make Mary and Polly the same woman with strong confidence and establish her Hutchinson family connection.

    An original January 1870 Tranquil Hall advertisement is signed "WM. SMITH / MARGET SMITH".[19] "Marget" may represent Margaret, a nickname, a printing error, or a different person involved in the hotel. No marriage or Bible record settles the issue. The safest wording is: Smith's wife was recorded as Mary during 1850-1880 and as Polly at death; the advertisement's Marget Smith remains an unresolved conflict. The modern form "Mary Hutchinson Smith" is plausible, but her full legal name has not been proved.

    Court service

    The earliest contemporary clerk evidence conflicts with Huxford's later office table. A mortgage-foreclosure rule printed March 7, 1829 ends with Smith's certification of a May 5, 1828 Lowndes Superior Court minute, followed by "WILLIAM SMITH, Cl'k. Sup. Court, Lowndes county" and a "June 21" dateline. The year is not printed beside June 21, but the twelve-month publication sequence makes June 21, 1828 the strong-probable reading.[13]

    A July 1831 equity extract again carries Smith's Lowndes Superior Court clerk signature.[14] In March 1832 he certified an Inferior Court estray extract as "c. i. c." In June 1833 he certified the Lowndes grand-jury presentments; his name appears as clerk, not as a juror.[15]

    By 1835 Smith was signing probate notices as "c. c. o.", clerk of the Court of Ordinary. The same issue also shows him acting as administrator of John Sutton. Further signed Lowndes probate notices continue through the 1830s and 1840s.[16]

    Huxford's officer table begins Smith's Superior Court clerk term on January 20, 1830 and assigns Henry Blair through 1830. Sharpe wrote shortly after Smith's death that Smith had been elected clerk in 1827.[11,17] The records prove Smith's service by the 1828-era dateline, but they do not show whether Huxford recorded a later commission or recommission. The exact Blair-to-Smith handoff, election, and commission sequence remains unresolved rather than neatly disproved.

    Smith's obituary says he had more than once been Ordinary and had held the office about 1868-1870. That later elected service was not independently reconstructed. The antebellum signatures prove clerk work in ordinary business, not every later term as elected Ordinary.[9]

    Franklinville post office

    The federal Official Register lists Smith at Franklinville on four reporting dates:

    Register date Entry Compensation
    September 30, 1829 "Franklinville, Ga. / Wm Smith" $9.06
    September 30, 1831 "Franklinville, Ga. / Wm. Smith" $16.67
    September 30, 1833 "Franklinville, Ga. / William Smith" $26.96
    September 30, 1835 "Franklinville / William Smith" $28.08

    These are direct federal records and verify Smith as postmaster by September 30, 1829 through the September 30, 1835 reporting date.[7] They do not prove that he served continuously between every register date.

    Huxford gives July 7, 1828 as the appointment date. No original appointment register or postal location paper proving that date was found. The 1827 federal register has no Lowndes office, but that absence does not prove that a new office could not have opened before the 1829 report. The exact first appointment remains unresolved.[7,17]

    The 1860 population census independently describes Smith as both hotelkeeper and postmaster at Troupville.[4] It does not establish when he began that later postal service.

    Mount Zion deed and public roles

    Huxford printed a Mount Zion deed transcription he said he copied from the original. Sion and Enoch Hall conveyed about six acres, including the church, camps, and burying ground, to the trustees in 1831. The registration certificate says the deed was entered in Lowndes Deed Book A, pages 654-655, on February 26, 1833 by "WILLIAM SMITH, Clerk".[20]

    The deed book later burned and no manuscript image was located. Huxford's original-based transcription is strong evidence that Smith recorded the deed. Smith was not a grantor, grantee, witness, trustee, or first tenter in that instrument.

    Huxford also names Smith among Troupville's first commissioners under the act approved December 14, 1837.[17] The official act could not be inspected because the Digital Library of Georgia returned the same unavailable message twice. The commissioner claim remains strong secondary evidence pending the session-law page.

    An October 1839 notice signed at Troupville provides direct residence evidence. Smith reported that halves of bank notes mailed to him had been lost and asked newspapers to send their accounts to him at Troupville.[21] The notice does not make him a banker or public officer.

    In 1850 he advertised from Troupville that he would procure federal bounty-land warrants for eligible claimants.[22] This proves a claims-agent business, not Smith's own military service. No qualifying Lowndes militia return, commission, company roster, or Creek War report was found for him. Common-name military hits elsewhere in Georgia were rejected.

    Tranquil Hall and movement to Valdosta

    The 1850 census calls Smith a hotelkeeper, and the 1860 schedule calls him hotelkeeper and postmaster at Troupville.[3,4] Smith's obituary names the hotel as "Tranquil Hall" and says its table and hospitality were widely known.[9] Sharpe and later Lowndes histories agree on the basic business.[11,18]

    The railroad bypassed Troupville and led residents to the new town of Valdosta. A January 1870 advertisement says the proprietors of Tranquil Hall, formerly of Troupville, had opened at Valdosta. It is signed by William and "Marget" Smith.[19] The 1870 and 1880 censuses confirm William's Valdosta hotelkeeping and later retirement.[5,6]

    An 1870 newspaper advertisement also calls the house "Tranquil Hall" at Valdosta. The original obituary says the aging couple gave up the business about ten years before Smith's death and sold the building to D. M. Jackson.[9,19]

    Slavery and wealth

    The strong-probable 1840 household counted two enslaved people.[2] The 1850 Lowndes slave schedule lists William Smith as enslaving fifteen people. The name, county, and same-census hotelkeeper context make the assignment strong probable. The original image and index agree on the owner and total.[8]

    The 1860 schedule is geographically specific: "William Smith" at Troupville enslaved thirty-six people. The original page and index agree. The population schedule valued his real estate at $5,000 and personal estate at $23,000.[4,8]

    The schedules did not record the people's names. No identity can be carried responsibly across emancipation from age and sex alone. Sharpe wrote that the war was especially hard on Smith because his property consisted of enslaved people.[11] That is a contemporary acquaintance's retrospective statement. The schedules prove the holdings and their scale but do not prove that every dollar of the reported personal estate represented enslaved people.

    Land, tax, and the Franklinville site

    Huxford places Smith's Franklinville home on Land Lot 50 in the 11th District and says the post office moved there.[17] No original deed, grant, plat, tax row, or title chain tying this William Smith to Lot 50 was found online. The exact lot is strong local-history evidence, not original land proof.

    Available original tax pages preserved in related Lowndes projects were checked, but no uniquely identified William Smith row could be assigned without district, land-lot, and neighbor context. The 1839 signed Troupville notice and later censuses prove the migration more securely than an unattributed tax-index hit.

    Death, church, and burial

    The original Valdosta Times obituary says Smith died Wednesday morning at his Valdosta residence. With the issue dated February 4, 1882, the date was February 1. Funeral services were held at the residence that afternoon, and burial occurred Thursday morning, February 2, in "our cemetery".[9]

    Hamilton W. Sharpe's memorial says Smith had joined the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, many years earlier and became a regular attendant late in life.[11] No baptism, membership roll, or church minute was found online, so the statement remains a close contemporary's account rather than an original church record.

    The obituary does not name the cemetery. Find a Grave and broader searches produced user-created leads but no inspected marker photograph, burial register, sexton record, cemetery deed, or contemporary notice naming the ground.[23] The exact cemetery and grave remain unresolved.

    Polly Smith died at Valdosta on January 21, 1892, derived from the Thursday preceding the January 23 issue. She was reported to be in her ninety-fifth year.[10] An indexed probate entry for Mary A. Smith is consistent with the widow, but its manuscript contents were not recovered and cannot resolve the initial or support an heir or property claim.

    Chronology

    • About 1797-1800: born in North Carolina.[3-6,9]
    • 1827-1828: settled at Franklinville; witnessed early Lowndes courts and later recalled them.[1,9,18]
    • By June 21, probably 1828: signing as Lowndes Superior Court clerk.[13]
    • September 30, 1829: federally listed as Franklinville postmaster.[7]
    • 1830: strong-probable Lowndes household of seven, with no enslaved people recorded.[2]
    • 1831-1832: verified Superior and Inferior Court clerk signatures.[14,15]
    • February 26, 1833: recorded the Mount Zion deed according to Huxford's original-based transcription.[20]
    • 1835: verified Court of Ordinary clerk and Franklinville postmaster.[7,16]
    • October 1839: signed a notice from Troupville.[21]
    • 1840: strong-probable household of ten, including two enslaved people.[2]
    • 1850: Lowndes hotelkeeper; strong-probable owner of fifteen enslaved people.[3,8]
    • 1860: Troupville hotelkeeper and postmaster; thirty-six enslaved people; $5,000 real and $23,000 personal estate.[4,8]
    • January 1870: Tranquil Hall advertised at Valdosta.[19]
    • 1870: Valdosta hotelkeeper.[5]
    • December 11, 1880: published his first-court recollection.[1]
    • 1880: retired hotelkeeper living with wife Mary; original-image middle initial appears "P", while the index reads "A".[6]
    • February 1-2, 1882: death and reported burial at Valdosta.[9]
    • January 21, 1892: widow Polly died at Valdosta.[10]

    Search coverage and remaining targets

    The main unresolved record targets are:

    1. Smith's original clerk election return, commission, and the Henry Blair handoff;
    2. the original Franklinville postmaster appointment and location paper;
    3. an original Lot 50 deed, plat, tax row, or title chain;
    4. William and Polly/Mary's marriage, Bible, probate, and named-heir records;
    5. proof of the "Marget Smith" identity;
    6. Tranquil Hall deeds and business records;
    7. church membership minutes;
    8. a cemetery register, marker image, sexton record, or burial deed.

    This dossier describes the accessible online evidence checked through August 19, 2026. It does not imply that undigitized courthouse, church, cemetery, postal, or federal records do not exist.
