    Jose Bryant: the reported Okapilco-Mule Creek settler

    Back to the Early History Profiles index

    Prepared August 18, 2026; expanded August 19, 2026

    Bottom line

    Jose Bryant is a name preserved in late nineteenth-century Lowndes County history, not a person who has yet been identified in an original early county record. The earliest located newspaper item, published in 1889, says that Joe and John Bryant settled at the old Indian town at the junction of Okapilco and Mule creeks.[36] Two later articles in the Valdosta Times describe Jose Bryant as the first white settler in the neighborhood west of present Morven. R. E. L. Folsom's bylined account, printed October 14, 1899, says 1828. A separate unsigned history printed October 23, 1900, says 1823 and adds that Jose was unmarried, enslaved “a dozen or two” people, and planted the first crop in the county.[1-3] These accounts were written decades after the event, cite no early record, and conflict on the settler's name, whether one or two Bryants were involved, the year, and who deserved the title of first settler.

    Folks Huxford later named John Bryan and paired him with Abraham Hunter.[4] Original records prove that a substantial John Bryan household was in Lowndes County by 1830, that John wrote from Lowndes in 1836, and that John and Abraham Hunter appeared on the same 1840 census page.[5-9] Jane Hunter's 1912 obituary identifies John Bryan as her father and says she was born at Okapilco, an Indian town that became his home.[37] That is the first located newspaper source explicitly joining the documented John Bryan family to Okapilco. It still does not call John “Jose,” place him at the site in 1823 or 1828, or prove that the 1889 Joe and John were one person. John is central to the tradition, but he cannot safely be declared Jose Bryant.

    The most defensible conclusion is:

    • By January 1889, a signed local item said that “Joe and John Bryant” settled at the old Indian town at the junction of Okapilco and Mule creeks. The same item credited James Rountree, not either Bryant, as Lowndes County's first settler.[36]
    • Huxford's cited 1890 “the Bryans” wording is consistent with that plural tradition, but the original 1890 page remains unlocated.[4]
    • The earliest located item naming the settler “Jose Bryant” was printed in 1899; its date is 1828, not 1823.[1]
    • An independent-looking but still retrospective 1900 article uses “Jose Bryan,” dates the move to 1823, and supplies the only located claims about marital status, slaveholding, and the first crop.[3]
    • John Bryan is documented in Lowndes from 1830 through 1850 and is a plausible subject of the tradition. No located source proves that Jose and John were the same person.[5-10]
    • Abraham Hunter's probable record chain places him in Screven County in 1827 and 1830. That conflicts with a simple version of Huxford's story in which Hunter settled beside John Bryan in the Okapilco-Mule Creek area in 1823 or 1828.[11-13]

    Evidence standard

    This dossier uses four grades:

    • Verified: an original or near-contemporary record directly supports the statement.
    • Strong probable: several records form a coherent identity chain, but one link is not explicit.
    • Tradition: a later historical account reports the claim without showing its early evidence.
    • Unresolved: the available evidence conflicts or does not identify the person.

    The spelling Jose is retained because it appears in the 1899 and 1900 accounts. It should not be silently normalized to Joe, Joseph, Josiah, Hosea, John, or another given name.

    The historical accounts

    The 1890 “Bryans” tradition

    Huxford quoted a January 17, 1890, description attributed to Dr. James R. Folsom, writing as “Bohemian,” saying that the first settlement was on the Okapilco “by the Bryans.”[4] This is a later plural-Bryan version of the tradition. It does not give a first name, a year, an exact site, or a source for the statement. The original page has not been found, so the wording is still known only through Huxford's 1948 quotation.

    The access gap is now better defined. B. T. Allen published the Berrien County Pioneer at Sparks before moving it to Tifton and renaming it the Tifton Gazette in April 1891.[25-28] Georgia Historic Newspapers' online Gazette run begins January 22, 1892, almost two years after the issue Huxford cited.[25-26] The catalog mentions University of Georgia microfilm for the successor title but does not say whether the reels include the 1888-1891 Pioneer. The next useful step is a holdings inquiry for the exact January 17, 1890, issue, not another broad OCR search.

    The 1889 “Joe and John Bryant” account

    An earlier item has now been located. A correspondent signing as “Savage” wrote in the January 12, 1889, Valdosta Times:

    James Rountree was the first settler of Lowndes, Joe and John Bryant settled Brooks county at an old Indian town at the junction of Okapilco and Mule creeks.[36]

    The item was headed “Correcting a Munchausen Story” and challenged something attributed to the Quitman Herald. It gives no settlement year, land description, source, or explanation of the relationship between Joe and John. It distinguishes two Bryants rather than treating Jose and John as variant names for one man. That makes a simple Jose-equals-John identification less likely, although it does not tell us whether “Joe” meant Joseph, Jose, or another form. The 1889 writer's separate claim that James Rountree was Lowndes County's first settler also conflicts with the later first-settler language about Jose. The original full newspaper page and article crop are retained in this project.

    Two accounts in the 1899 Valdosta Times

    The October 14, 1899, issue contains two distinct statements.

    An unbylined county profile on page 21 says:

    Seven or eight miles west of Morven Jose Bryant made the first settlement ever made by white men in this section of the country, where was a large Indian village.[2]

    It supplies no year, origin, family, land description, or later history.

    On page 22, R. E. L. Folsom's “Historic Sketch of Lowndes County” says:

    The first white settlement in this section was made on this road, in the fork of the Okapilco and Mule creeks, in Brooks county, at an old Indian town, by Jose Bryant, in 1828.[1]

    The original full-page image plainly reads 1828. A modern online transcription changes the date to 1823.[14] That transcription is a useful finding aid, but it is not faithful on this important point.

    Folsom's account establishes what he reported in 1899. It does not establish who Jose was or prove the settlement date. No cited deed, tax return, census, survey, letter, or family record accompanies the statement.

    The 1900 account and the origin of the 1823 date

    An unsigned history in the October 23, 1900, Valdosta Times says:

    Early in the year 1823, Jose Bryan emigrated to an Indian town which had been vacated by the Creek Indians, on the purchase of the territory embraced in old Irwin, by the State of Georgia. This settlement was in the fork of the Okapilco and Mule creeks, and on the Coffee road, which had been opened by General Coffee by virtue of a contract with the State of Georgia.[3]

    It continues that Jose was unmarried, “owned a dozen or two slaves,” and made the first crop grown in Lowndes County. This is the earliest located source for those biographical details and for the 1823 date. The article is unsigned and does not identify its evidence. A 1933 newspaper story reprinted this same paragraph and identified the old issue; it is a traceable reprint, not an independent witness.[15]

    The 1899 and 1900 articles agree on the name, Coffee Road, the fork of Okapilco and Mule creeks, and an old or former Indian town. Only the 1900 article calls the town vacated. The articles also disagree on the year. Neither was contemporary with the event. Publication-safe writing should therefore say that Folsom reported 1828 in 1899, while the 1900 account reported 1823 and added the personal details.

    Huxford's John Bryan and Abraham Hunter

    In 1948, Huxford wrote that John Bryan and Abraham Hunter came from North Carolina and settled in the forks of Okapilco and Mule creeks. Elsewhere he summarized John Bryan's 1836 letter.[4] Huxford did not explain why the earlier Jose Bryant became John Bryan or cite a record joining the two names. His account is useful local history, but the name substitution is a hypothesis until an earlier source supplies the bridge.

    Candidate identity: John Bryan

    Documented record chain

    The strongest candidate is the John Bryan who appears in Lowndes records from 1830 through 1850:

    • 1830 census: John Bryan headed a Lowndes household with one white man aged 40-49, one white woman aged 20-29, and 23 enslaved people.[5]
    • 1834-1844 taxes: indexed Lowndes tax returns place John in several militia districts. The 1844 entry in Captain Blair's district reports 12 enslaved people.[6]
    • 1836 letter: John Bryan wrote from Lowndes County to Governor William Schley on August 22, 1836. Reporting Creek parties on the Florida frontier, he asked for 1,500 mounted men. The signed manuscript survives in the Georgia Archives.[7]
    • 1840 census: John headed a Lowndes household with four free white people and nine enslaved people. Abraham Hunter appears on the same original census page.[8]
    • 1844 litigation: sheriff-sale notices named “John Bryant and Israel Folsom” as plaintiffs in a Lowndes case. This verifies the Bryant spelling in a local legal context but does not identify Jose.[16]
    • 1850 census: John Bryan, 64, Georgia-born, was a farmer reporting $10,000 in real estate. Mary J. Bryan, 40, and William Bryan, 16, lived in the household; the census did not state relationships.[9]

    This chain verifies a prominent Lowndes resident. It does not verify an 1823 or 1828 arrival.

    Age and origin conflicts

    The records do not form a perfectly clean identity chain. The 1830 age band implies birth between 1780 and 1790, and the 1850 age implies about 1786. A photographed-marker memorial reports February 17, 1789, to October 28, 1854.[17] Those three can describe one man. The 1840 schedule, however, places the oldest white male at 60-69, implying birth between 1770 and 1780. That may be a tally error or a different John Bryan; the surviving record does not resolve it.

    Huxford said John came from North Carolina. The 1850 census says Georgia. No direct record located in this project gives John a North Carolina birthplace. His origin and parents remain unresolved.

    Family and the Hunter connection

    Jane Hunter's 1912 obituary says plainly: “Her father, John Bryan, was one of the first settlers in this county. She was born at Okapilco, an Indian town, which was her father's home after the Indians left this country.”[37] The 1850 census independently places Abram Hunter, 44, and Jane Hunter, 19, in one household and marks both as married within the year.[19] Derivative memorials and a 1973 compiled family history also identify her as Jane Bryan, John's daughter, and Abraham Hunter Jr.'s wife.[17-18,31]

    The obituary is direct published evidence of the claimed father-daughter relationship and of Jane's birth at John's Okapilco home, but it appeared about fifty-eight years after John's death and does not name its informant. No probate, Bible, marriage, deed, or civil record located in this project states the relationship; John died before the 1858 courthouse fire, and the Ordinary's office burned in 1869.[32] The father-daughter link is therefore strong probable, directly stated in a contemporary obituary and supported by the household and derivative evidence, but not original-record verified.

    Even with that limitation, three facts give the Bryan-Hunter association a real record base: John and Abraham Sr. were on the same 1840 census page; their households were near each other in 1850; and Jane was married to an Abraham Hunter by 1850.[8-9,19-20] This helps explain why later local history paired the surnames. It does not prove that John and Abraham arrived together or that John was Jose.

    Slaveholding claim

    The 1900 article says Jose enslaved “a dozen or two” people.[3] The 1830 John Bryan household included 23 enslaved people.[5] The numerical resemblance is noteworthy but not an identity proof. It may reflect a remembered feature of the documented John household, or it may be coincidence or later reconstruction. The 1830 schedule was recorded years after either proposed settlement date.

    Abraham Hunter as a test of the later story

    Abraham Hunter is verified in Lowndes by 1840. The census lists a white man aged 70-79, a white woman aged 50-59, and 29 enslaved people. In 1850, Abraham was 80, North Carolina-born, and living with Ann Hunter, 73.[8,20] Lowndes tax records also list him in 1840 and 1844.[21]

    The likely earlier chain points elsewhere:

    • The official 1827 land-lottery register places Abraham Hunter in Hunter's District, Screven County, when he drew Lee County lot 290, district 6.[11]
    • The 1820 Screven census has an original-page entry reading “Abrm Hunter.” Its age structure fits the elder man's household and could include the younger Abraham, but the schedule gives no relationships.[29]
    • The 1830 census lists an Abraham Hunter household in Screven County whose ages fit the later Lowndes couple reasonably well.[12]
    • Georgia Archives holds Abraham Hunter surveys in Screven County from 1797 through 1831, including 1822 and 1831 plats.[13]

    The Screven and Lowndes records form a strong-probable, not absolute, identity chain because the early documents do not state age and kin throughout. If they concern the same man, he was still tied to Screven in 1827 and enumerated there in 1830. That conflicts directly with a story that he migrated from North Carolina to the Lowndes settlement in 1823 or 1828. His North Carolina birth does not mean he came directly from North Carolina at that time.

    The two Abrahams can now be separated more clearly. In 1850, Abram, 44, and Jane occupied dwelling 52, while Abraham, 80, and Ann occupied dwelling 54 on the same page.[19-20] The younger household continued in Brooks County in 1860 with Abram, Jane, and four children.[30] These records verify two men, but they do not state that they were father and son. That relationship remains strong probable and derivative-supported, not direct-record verified. Abraham Jr.'s residence before 1850 remains unresolved.

    Geography of the reported settlement

    The accounts describe a place on the Coffee Road at the fork or junction of Okapilco and Mule creeks, seven or eight miles west of Morven, at or near a former Creek settlement.[1-3,36] Jane Hunter's 1912 obituary separately says that she was born at Okapilco, an Indian town that was her father John Bryan's home after the Indians left.[37] This is specific evidence for John's association with the Okapilco Indian-town neighborhood by about 1830, based on Jane's reported age. It does not state that John owned land there in 1823 or 1828, identify a surveyed parcel, or say that he was Jose. No inspected deed, grant, plat, tax page, or land-lot record supplies an exact legal description for Jose or John at the site.

    The original 1844 tax image places John in Captain Blair's District and gives Lowndes as the land county, but it supplies no lot number, watercourse, Coffee Road, Okapilco, or Mule Creek reference.[6] Earlier index entries place him in Captain Godwin's and Captain Grantham's districts. Those district labels cannot be converted into the creek-fork site without a contemporary boundary description. Georgia Archives' headright and bounty plat searches found John Bryan namesakes in other counties but no target Irwin or Lowndes plat for Jose, John, Joseph, Josiah, or Hosea Bryan/Bryant.[33]

    The absence has a documentary reason. Georgia Archives' Lowndes County inventory says the courthouse burned in 1858 and the Ordinary's office in 1869. Its listed court, estate, bond, and deed holdings do not preserve the usual pre-fire county trail needed to identify John's parcel.[32] Five pre-fire Lowndes tax digests survive on microfilm, but the inspected pages establish county or militia-district presence, not the fork.[34]

    The phrase “fork” should not be expanded into a precise modern coordinate without map and deed evidence. Creek courses, road alignments, historical names, and county boundaries can change. The geographic claim remains a reported local tradition until connected to an original land record.

    The Irwin County sheriff candidate

    Georgia Archives preserves 1817 and 1818 Irwin County letters signed John Bryan or John Bryant; the 1818 document identifies the signer as sheriff of Irwin County.[22] These are authentic, early records and show that a man of that name held office in the parent county before Lowndes was created. They do not state age, family, residence at Okapilco, or later Lowndes identity. No document was found linking the Irwin sheriff to the 1830 Lowndes John Bryan. He must remain a separate candidate rather than being merged by name alone.

    The Irwin archive inventory identifies unindexed deed indexes beginning in 1821/1822, court minutes beginning in 1820, miscellaneous bonds for 1822-1827, and an execution docket beginning in 1822.[35] Those series may contain a later bridge, but they require manual microfilm review. The surviving miscellaneous-bond run starts after the sheriff's 1817-1818 tenure, and the county-officers' bond series begins in 1838. The official-letter grouping under one name is not proof that the 1817, 1818, and 1836 writers were one person.

    What is and is not known about Jose

    Supported as historical tradition

    • A January 1889 correspondent said Joe and John Bryant settled at the old Indian town at the Okapilco-Mule Creek junction.[36]
    • Jose Bryant or Bryan was said in 1899 and 1900 to have settled at a former Creek town near the Coffee Road and the Okapilco-Mule Creek fork.[1-3]
    • The earliest located account that names Jose and supplies a year dates the settlement to 1828; the next year's article dates it to 1823.[1,3]
    • By 1890, a broader version credited “the Bryans” with the first settlement on the Okapilco.[4]
    • Jane Hunter's 1912 obituary identified John Bryan as her father and said she was born at his Okapilco Indian-town home.[37]

    Not proved

    • Jose's full legal name or whether “Jose” was a nickname, error, or distinct given name.
    • His birth, parents, spouse, children, death, burial, probate, or descendants.
    • His identity as John Bryan, the Irwin sheriff, Joseph Bryan, Josiah Bryan, Hosea Bryan, or any other candidate.
    • A North Carolina origin.
    • Presence at the reported site in 1823 or 1828.
    • Ownership of land at the site.
    • The 1900 claims that he was unmarried, enslaved a dozen or two people, and grew the county's first crop.
    • A joint migration with Abraham Hunter.

    Namesake and variant controls

    Searches included Jose, Joseph, John, Josiah, and Hosea with Bryan, Bryant, and Briant spellings. No relevant pre-1850 Jose, Joseph, Josiah, or Hosea record was found in Irwin or Lowndes. Exact-name Jose results were later people or index noise. John Bryan and Abraham Hunter records in other Georgia counties were not merged without continuity evidence.

    An Ancestry member tree repeats the Lowndes John Bryan census records, 1789-1854 dates, and Bryan-Hunter family links, but leaves John's parents unknown.[23] It is a lead compilation, not an independent source. Find a Grave biographies and relationship links were likewise treated as derivative unless supported by a record or readable marker.[17-18,24]

    Reproducible search coverage

    The project searched stored Barney/Lowndes research before external sources. Online work then covered:

    • Newspapers.com page images and OCR for the 1899, 1900, and 1933 accounts;
    • Georgia Historic Newspapers for Bryant/Bryan legal notices and spelling controls;
    • Ancestry's 1820, 1830, 1840, 1850, and 1860 federal censuses, Georgia tax digests, 1827 land lottery, probate index, military category, all-collection exact names, and one public member tree;
    • Georgia Archives File II Names, Headright and Bounty Plats, loose plats, county-record microfilm inventories, tax-digest inventories, and the Lowndes County Indians file;
    • Find a Grave, FamilySearch, Internet Archive, Google Books, HathiTrust, WikiTree, Geni, and Geneanet access surfaces;
    • exact and variant searches for Jose/Joseph/John/Josiah/Hosea Bryant/Bryan/Briant and Abraham Hunter.

    The 1890 upgrade search covered Georgia Historic Newspapers OCR for 1889-1891, Newspapers.com OCR and title browsing for 1888-1891, Chronicling America, the US Newspaper Directory, Internet Archive, WorldCat, FamilySearch catalog, Google Books, HathiTrust, and web exact phrases. The exact 1890 page was not found, but the search located the January 12, 1889, Valdosta Times item naming Joe and John Bryant at the creek junction.[36] The Berrien County Pioneer issue falls before the digitized successor run, and whether a predecessor microfilm survives at UGA is still unknown.

    FamilySearch required sign-in in the available session. Google Books returned a quota error, HathiTrust presented an access wall, and some Ancestry collections require a subscription. These limitations are recorded because an inaccessible repository cannot support a negative finding. The Lowndes record trail is also limited by courthouse and Ordinary's-office fires; relevant surviving Irwin series are unindexed and were not inspected on microfilm.

    “Not found” in this dossier means not found in the documented online search. It does not mean that no record ever existed. The best next evidence would be surviving pre-1858 deed or tax abstracts tied to the creek fork, family papers naming the original settler, the original 1890 “Bohemian” article, or an earlier source used by Folsom or Huxford.
